# Bot Profiles Dossier (#287)

These profiles cover ten historically verified Oregon Trail emigrants whose family compositions, professions, and documented behaviors translate directly into distinct NPC play-styles. Each entry is grounded in primary accounts and Wikipedia-traceable sources; "unknown" is used wherever the record is genuinely thin.

### 1. Sager Family

- **Year:** 1844 (departed Independence, MO, spring)
- **Leader profession:** `farmer` (Henry Sager was an Ohio farmer who relocated the family repeatedly chasing land)
- **Composition:** 2 adults (Henry Sager, m; Naomi Sager, f) + 7 children: John (13), Frank (11), Catherine (9), Elizabeth (7), Matilda Jane (5), Hannah Louise (3), and Henrietta (newborn, born on the trail)
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `faithful` — The family traveled under the protection of Oregon-bound missionaries and relied heavily on Dr. Theophilus Degen and later Marcus Whitman; Catherine's memoir records constant prayer and community dependence after both parents died en route
- **Distinguishing trait:** Both parents died on the trail (Henry from typhoid/injuries near Fort Hall, Naomi from childbed fever shortly after); the seven orphaned children completed the journey under the care of fellow emigrants and arrived at the Whitman Mission, where they were adopted by Marcus and Narcissa Whitman — the only fully orphaned family to survive the full crossing in the emigration record
- **Fate:** All seven children reached the Whitman Mission (1844); three (John, Francis, and Henrietta) were killed in the 1847 Whitman Massacre; the four surviving sisters — Catherine, Elizabeth, Matilda Jane, and Hannah Louise — lived to adulthood in Oregon
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sager_children ; Catherine Sager Pringle, *Across the Plains in 1844* (memoir, 1860, digitized at Oregon Historical Society); Tobie, Harvey E., "The Sager Children," *Oregon Historical Quarterly* 27:3 (1926)
- **Notes / unknowns:** Henry Sager's exact birth year varies by source (c. 1812–1816); Naomi's maiden name sometimes given as Hein or Heine — uncertain. The number of children confirmed at 7 by Catherine's own memoir.

### 2. George Donner Family

- **Year:** 1846 (departed Independence, MO, April)
- **Leader profession:** `farmer` (George Donner was a prosperous Illinois farmer and captain of the Donner-Reed Party)
- **Composition:** 2 adults (George Donner, m, age ~62; Tamzene Donner, f, age 45) + 5 children: Elitha (14), Leanna (12), Frances (6), Georgia (4), Eliza (3); plus hired teamsters (Jacob Donner's family traveled alongside, adding ~4 adults and 7 children to the larger wagon cluster)
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `generous` — George Donner was elected captain by acclamation, shared supplies freely, and Tamzene reportedly distributed food to others even as their own stores ran low in the Sierra; generosity at cost of self-preservation is the defining pattern
- **Distinguishing trait:** George Donner suffered a serious hand wound from a chisel while repairing a wagon axle at Alder Creek; the wound became gangrenous and incapacitated him, directly preventing the family's escape attempt in February 1847 — a single equipment-repair event determined their fate
- **Fate:** George died of gangrene at Alder Creek camp, February 1847; Tamzene survived into April but reportedly died (or was killed) at the lake camp after staying to nurse George; 3 of the 5 Donner daughters survived to adulthood, rescued by the Fourth Relief party
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donner_Party ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Donner ; Stewart, George R., *Ordeal by Hunger* (1936; revised 1960); King, Joseph A., *Winter of Entrapment* (1992)
- **Notes / unknowns:** George Donner's exact birth year is disputed (c. 1784–1787). Number of hired hands in immediate George Donner camp: approximately 3–4, names vary by source.

### 3. James Reed Family

- **Year:** 1846 (same party as George Donner; departed spring)
- **Leader profession:** `banker` (Reed was a prosperous furniture manufacturer and businessman in Springfield, IL — no exact game match; `banker` is the closest for "wealthy merchant/investor" archetype; flag: actual trade was furniture manufacturing and land speculation)
- **Composition:** 2 adults (James Frazier Reed, m, age 45; Margaret Reed, f, age 32) + 4 children: Virginia (13), Patty (8), James Jr. (5), Thomas (3); plus Margaret's mother Sarah Keyes (elderly, died en route near present-day Alcove Spring, KS) and 2–3 servants/hired hands
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `pace_pusher` — Reed was famously impatient, pushed for the Hastings Cutoff to save time, quarreled with other emigrants over pace and route, and was ultimately banished from the party for killing John Snyder in a whip-fight altercation; urgency at the expense of group cohesion is his signature
- **Distinguishing trait:** Reed built the "Pioneer Palace Car," an enormous double-decker family wagon with a iron stove and upholstered interior — the heaviest and most ostentatious vehicle in the entire emigration, which slowed his team disproportionately through the Wasatch and contributed to the party's fatal late-season arrival at the Sierra
- **Fate:** Banished from the party in October 1846 after killing teamster John Snyder; rode ahead to California, organized and led the Second Relief rescue party in February 1847; his entire immediate family survived
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Reed_(pioneer) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donner_Party ; Murphy, Virginia Reed, "Across the Plains in the Donner Party" (Century Magazine, 1891); Stewart, George R., *Ordeal by Hunger*
- **Notes / unknowns:** Exact number of Reed servants varies (2–3); Margaret Reed's age at departure sometimes given as 32 or 33. The "Pioneer Palace Car" details are from Virginia Reed Murphy's memoir and Stewart's reconstruction.

### 4. Joe Meek

- **Year:** 1840 (Oregon-bound, with the American Methodist Mission supply train led by Rev. Jason Lee)
- **Leader profession:** `hunter` (Rocky Mountain fur trapper and mountain man 1829–1840; `hunter` is the direct game equivalent; no gap to flag)
- **Composition:** 1 adult (Joseph Lafayette Meek, m, age ~26) + likely 1 child (his daughter Helen Mar Meek by his Nez Perce wife Umentucken, born c. 1836, possibly traveling with him; some accounts put her at Lapwai with the Spaldings) — composition genuinely uncertain; effectively solo for game purposes
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `chaos` — Meek was famous for impulsive decisions, tall tales, dramatic reversals, and dragging companions into scrapes; his own memoir (*The River of the West*, 1870) is a catalog of near-deaths by bear, Blackfoot, and bad judgment presented as entertainment
- **Distinguishing trait:** By 1840 the beaver trade had collapsed; Meek traveled to Oregon not to farm but because the mountains were economically finished — he arrived with hunting skills but no farming knowledge, no capital, and no plan, which makes him the archetype of the trail traveler whose profession is irrelevant to his destination
- **Fate:** Settled in Oregon's Tualatin Valley, became a farmer and politician; served as first U.S. Marshal of Oregon Territory (1848); rode to Washington D.C. in winter 1848 to personally inform President Polk of the Whitman Massacre, a 3,000-mile solo ride that became legendary
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Meek ; Victor, Frances Fuller, *The River of the West* (1870, Meek's dictated memoir); Munnick, Harriet D., in *Oregon Encyclopedia* (oregonencyclopedia.org)
- **Notes / unknowns:** Exact composition of Meek's 1840 travel group disputed. Helen Mar Meek's whereabouts in 1840 are uncertain — she may have been left at the Lapwai Mission. Some sources say Meek traveled with a small group of former trappers; others say largely solo. Birth year 1810 is widely cited but unverified by document.

### 5. Marcus & Narcissa Whitman

- **Year:** 1836 (departed Liberty, MO, May; arrived Walla Walla region, September)
- **Leader profession:** `doctor` (Marcus Whitman held an M.D. from College of Physicians and Surgeons, Fairfield, NY, 1832; practiced medicine before and during missionary work — direct match, no gap)
- **Composition:** 2 adults (Marcus Whitman, m, age 34; Narcissa Prentiss Whitman, f, age 28) — no children on the 1836 crossing; traveled with Henry and Eliza Spalding and lay assistant William Gray (party of 4 adults + 2 Nez Perce guides)
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `sunday_rester` — The Whitmans were Calvinist Protestant missionaries; Sunday observance was non-negotiable for them and the Spaldings; their travel journals record repeated halts for Sabbath worship regardless of weather or urgency, and Marcus held Sunday medical clinics at the mission
- **Distinguishing trait:** Narcissa Whitman and Eliza Spalding became the first white women to cross the Rocky Mountains overland; their wagon (driven by Marcus) became the first wheeled vehicle taken as far as Fort Boise on the Snake River — not a full wagon crossing, but the furthest wheels had reached; the route they proved open enabled the mass migrations of the 1840s
- **Fate:** Founded Waiilatpu Mission near present-day Walla Walla, WA (1836); Marcus returned east in 1842–43 and helped guide the 1843 Great Migration back; both Marcus and Narcissa were killed in the Whitman Massacre, November 29, 1847, along with 11 others
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Whitman ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narcissa_Whitman ; Drury, Clifford M., *Marcus and Narcissa Whitman and the Opening of Old Oregon* (2 vols., 1973); Jeffrey, Julie Roy, *Converting the West* (1991)
- **Notes / unknowns:** The 1836 party is sometimes listed as 3 or 4 adults depending on whether Gray and the Nez Perce guides are counted. The wagon was abandoned or converted to a cart somewhere between Fort Boise and the Whitman Mission — exact location varies by source.

### 6. Tabitha Brown

- **Year:** 1846 (departed St. Joseph, MO, spring; traveled with the Applegate Train and then the disastrous Meek Cutoff branch)
- **Leader profession:** `teacher` (Brown founded the first orphan school in Oregon Territory in 1847, which became Pacific University; before emigrating she ran a school in Virginia and later Missouri — `teacher` is a direct match)
- **Composition:** 1 adult (Tabitha Moffatt Brown, f, age 66) — her son Orus Brown and his family traveled in the same general train, as did her brother-in-law Captain John Brown (age 77); for game purposes she is effectively a solo elder with loose family attachment
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `cautious` — Brown's own letters describe extreme caution and deliberation; she nearly died of exposure and starvation on the Umpqua Canyon section of the Applegate Trail, survived by finding a small coin in her glove that she traded for food, and wrote that she never took unnecessary risks again; her subsequent life was defined by careful institution-building
- **Distinguishing trait:** She crossed at age 66, older than virtually any other documented emigrant, on foot through much of the Umpqua Mountains after her horse gave out; her 1854 letter to her brother (reproduced in full by Oregon Historical Society) is the most detailed first-person account of the southern Applegate route's hardships
- **Fate:** Arrived in Oregon's Tualatin Valley winter 1846–47 near death; recovered, founded a school for orphaned children of the trail (1847), which grew into Tualatin Academy and eventually Pacific University (Forest Grove, OR); died 1858, age 78
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabitha_Brown ; Brown, Tabitha, letter to her brother and sister, 1854, Oregon Historical Society MSS 1508; Carey, Charles H., *History of Oregon* (1922)
- **Notes / unknowns:** Whether Brown traveled under Stephen Meek's personal guidance or merely in the same general train is disputed. The "coin in the glove" detail comes from her own 1854 letter and is widely cited but cannot be independently corroborated.

### 7. Ezra Meeker

- **Year:** 1852 (first crossing; later re-crossed westward in 1906 by wagon and again by automobile in 1915–16 to mark the trail)
- **Leader profession:** `farmer` (Meeker farmed hops in Washington Territory for decades; `farmer` is a direct match)
- **Composition:** 2 adults (Ezra Meeker, m, age 21; Eliza Jane Sumner Meeker, f, age 20) + 1 infant (Oliver, born shortly before departure, exact age at departure unknown — possibly weeks old); one ox team of 2 yoke
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `balanced` — Meeker's 1852 journal and his 1906 memoir *Ox-Team Days on the Oregon Trail* consistently describe methodical decision-making: he rested animals on schedule, rationed supplies carefully, adjusted pace to conditions, and arrived in Oregon without catastrophic loss; he is the anti-Donner
- **Distinguishing trait:** Meeker's 1906 reverse crossing (age 75, ox-wagon from Puget Sound to Indiana) to mark the trail with monuments was a national news event; his personal fortune was made and lost in the Washington hop industry; he is the only Oregon Trail emigrant documented to have crossed multiple times across six decades, giving him an unmatched longitudinal view of how the trail changed
- **Fate:** Settled in Puget Sound area, farmed hops, became a prominent Washington Territory booster; lost fortune in hop-louse blight (1891); spent final decades as the country's most prominent Oregon Trail preservationist; died 1928, age 97 — one of the longest-lived major Oregon Trail figures on record
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Meeker ; Meeker, Ezra, *Ox-Team Days on the Oregon Trail* (1922, revised ed.); *Personal Memoirs of Ezra Meeker* (1912)
- **Notes / unknowns:** Oliver Meeker's exact birth date relative to the 1852 departure is not pinned down in sources reviewed. Whether a second child accompanied them is unclear; some sources mention only Oliver for 1852.

### 8. John Bidwell

- **Year:** 1841 (Bidwell-Bartleson Party, first emigrant wagon train to California, though some members including Bidwell ended up in California not Oregon; party departed Sapling Grove, KS, May 1841)
- **Leader profession:** `teacher` (Bidwell was a schoolteacher in Missouri before the journey, then became a California rancher and politician; `teacher` is the closest game match for his pre-trail occupation; flag: he was effectively party secretary/organizer, not a manual-trade leader)
- **Composition:** The Bidwell-Bartleson Party numbered approximately 69 people at departure, including only 1 woman (Nancy Kelsey, age 18, with infant daughter Ann); Bidwell himself was a single 22-year-old man — for game purposes he leads a large bachelor-heavy train, not a family unit
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `aggressive` — Bidwell pushed for the California route despite no reliable maps or guides, organized departure over the objections of cautious members, and consistently argued for forward progress when others wanted to turn back; his memoir frames every difficult decision as one he drove through by force of argument
- **Distinguishing trait:** The Bidwell-Bartleson Party abandoned their wagons in the Nevada desert (near present-day Lovelock, NV) and completed the journey on foot and horseback — the only major emigrant party to deliberately abandon all wagons mid-crossing and still reach their destination; this makes Bidwell the origin point of the "what do we do when the wagons fail" problem
- **Fate:** Reached California November 1841; worked for John Sutter, discovered gold at Coloma (though James Marshall is credited with the formal discovery in 1848); became a major Sacramento Valley landowner (Rancho Chico) and four-time California gubernatorial candidate; died 1900
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bidwell ; Bidwell, John, *Echoes of the Past* (1900, memoir); Camp, Charles, ed., *James Clyman, American Frontiersman* (1928, contemporary account of same journey)
- **Notes / unknowns:** The party's final headcount at departure (69) is the most commonly cited figure but some sources say 64–80. The party split in present-day Nevada; Bartleson's subgroup took a different route. Bidwell's account of wagon abandonment is first-person; no independent corroboration survives.

### 9. Joel Palmer

- **Year:** 1845 (departed Independence, MO, May; arrived Oregon City, November)
- **Leader profession:** `carpenter` (Palmer was a carpenter and builder by trade in Indiana before emigrating; `carpenter` is a direct match; he later became a surveyor and Indian superintendent, but his 1845 profession is confirmed)
- **Composition:** 1 adult (Joel Palmer, m, age 30) — his wife Catherine and children remained in Indiana for 1845; Palmer traveled as a single man with hired teamsters; he returned for his family and brought them over in 1847
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `balanced` — Palmer's *Journal of Travels over the Rocky Mountains* (1847) is the Oregon Trail's most methodical trail guide; he recorded distances, water sources, grass conditions, and rest days with meticulous balance; contemporary emigrants used the Journal as a literal navigation tool, which implies its author was a balanced, evidence-based traveler
- **Distinguishing trait:** Palmer reconnoitered and helped negotiate the Barlow Road around Mt. Hood in autumn 1845 — the toll road that replaced the dangerous Columbia River raft section for most subsequent emigrants; his *Journal* became the standard guidebook for 1840s–50s emigrants and was reprinted multiple times, making him the trail's primary published authority
- **Fate:** Settled Dayton, OR; became Oregon's Superintendent of Indian Affairs (1853–57), where he negotiated multiple treaties with Pacific Northwest tribes; removed from office for being too sympathetic to Native land claims; died 1881
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Palmer ; Palmer, Joel, *Journal of Travels over the Rocky Mountains* (1847, digitized at Oregon Historical Society); Carey, Charles H., *History of Oregon* (1922)
- **Notes / unknowns:** Whether Palmer had any hired hands or traveled in a group with other named individuals in 1845 is not firmly established in sources reviewed — he likely joined a general train but led his own wagons. Wife Catherine's maiden name sometimes given as Caffee or Cauffman — uncertain.

### 10. Lansford Hastings

- **Year:** 1842 (first westward crossing); 1846 (the year of the Cutoff, traveling eastward to intercept California-bound emigrants)
- **Leader profession:** `banker` (Hastings was a lawyer and land speculator in Ohio before going west; `banker` is the closest game equivalent for "lawyer/promoter/speculator"; flag: actual profession was attorney and real-estate developer, not banking)
- **Composition:** 1 adult (Lansford Warren Hastings, m, age ~22 in 1842) — traveled with and later led large parties; in 1846 he was effectively a solo agent intercepting wagon trains, not leading a family unit
- **Persona variant suggestion:** `pace_pusher` — Hastings promoted his unproven shortcut through the Wasatch and Salt Desert on the basis of speed and convenience; his 1845 *Emigrant's Guide* explicitly argued that his route would save time; he pushed Reed and Donner to take the Cutoff despite having never driven wagons over it himself, and then rode ahead rather than guiding from the back
- **Distinguishing trait:** Hastings's *Emigrant's Guide to Oregon and California* (1845) was the most widely read trail guidebook of the 1840s and was factually unreliable in key respects — his description of the Salt Desert crossing was based on a horse traverse, not wagons, and his distance estimates were consistently optimistic; the gap between his written promises and trail reality is historically unique in the emigration literature
- **Fate:** Led a California-bound party over his Cutoff in 1846 without disaster (they crossed before the season turned); persuaded the Donner-Reed Party to follow — contributing directly to their entrapment; went to Brazil after the Civil War to establish a Confederate colony (Hastings, São Paulo state); died in Brazil, 1870
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansford_Hastings ; Hastings, Lansford W., *The Emigrants' Guide to Oregon and California* (1845, digitized at Bancroft Library); Stewart, George R., *Ordeal by Hunger* (1960)
- **Notes / unknowns:** Hastings's birth year is given variously as 1819 or 1820. Whether he had family with him on any of his western crossings is unknown — all sources treat him as traveling without dependents. His later Confederate Brazil colony ("Americana") is documented; date and cause of death (1870, yellow fever, per some sources) is plausible but not universally confirmed.

## Rejected candidates

- **Stephen Meek** (1845) — Operated the Meek Cutoff as a hired guide, not a family emigrant; he had no documented family unit on the 1845 crossing and his personal composition cannot be pinned down; also, his inclusion alongside Joe Meek risks NPC name-collision confusion in a game context.
- **Peter Burnett** (1843) — Burnett's family composition for the 1843 crossing is documented only at the level of "wife and several children" without names or ages in primary sources; without pinnable children counts his family profile is too thin to distinguish from a generic farmer family.
