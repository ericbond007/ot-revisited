## Q1 — AUTUMN GRAZING REALITY

### VERDICT

**Exempting deliberate rest-day recovery from the seasonal decline is historically
defensible — strongly so.** The cattlemen's winter-range fact is correct for the
high-plains/intermountain grasses the Oregon Trail crossed: buffalo grass, grama,
and bunchgrass *cured on the stem* into standing hay and held feed value through
winter. Stock did not starve because the calendar turned; they starved because
the corridor was **grazed out by earlier trains**, because **snow covered** the
cured grass, or in specific **barren stretches**. A resting ox turned onto decent
uneaten autumn range was genuinely restored.

Recommended model: keep the seasonal decline on **daily passive grazing** (it
proxies depletion + snow-cover + scarcity pressure along the traveled corridor),
but **exempt a deliberate rest day** — resting on cured standing grass feeds an
ox. This is exactly the distinction the primary sources draw.

### Strongest citations

1. **Marcy 1859, lines 2583–2587 (decisive).** "In Utah and New Mexico the autumn
   is so dry that the grass does not lose its nutritious properties by being washed
   with rains. It gradually **dries and cures like hay, so that animals eat it
   freely, and will fatten upon it even in mid-winter.** It is seldom that any grain
   is fed to stock in either of these territories." — Direct statement that
   intermountain grass cures and retains (even concentrates) feed value into
   mid-winter. This is the curing-on-the-stem fact verbatim.

2. **Marcy 1859, lines 467–468.** On the Missouri/Platte prairie route, old spring
   grass "does not, **in this climate, as in Utah and New Mexico,** afford them
   sufficient sustenance." — Marcy explicitly contrasts the two regimes: cured
   grass *is* sufficient in the dry intermountain West; the failure case is the
   rain-washed eastern prairie. The decline is regional/moisture-driven, not a
   universal calendar law. (Mechanism named at lines 2583–2584: autumnal *rains*,
   not cold, leach the nutrition — and only east of the dry country.)

3. **Marcy 1859, lines 473–479.** "As large numbers of cattle pass over the road
   annually, they **soon consume all the grass in these barren localities,** and
   such as pass late in the season are likely to suffer greatly... I seldom found
   myself out of sight of dead cattle for 500 miles." — The real late-season feed
   problem is **depletion of the corridor** (and specific barren stretches: the
   ~50 mi west of South Pass, the Humboldt sink), not grass dying on the calendar.

4. **Marcy 1859, lines 2578–2581 (snow cover).** "Where the snow was not more than
   two feet deep, the animals soon learned to paw it away and get at the grass... they
   do much better than one would suppose." — The cured grass is *there and edible*
   under snow; **snow cover (access), not nutrition loss,** is the winter constraint.
   Confirms the second real late-season mechanism.

### Sub-questions

- **(a) Did the grasses lose nutrition in autumn, or cure as standing hay?** They
  **cured**. Buffalo grass / grama / bunchgrass are the classic self-curing range
  grasses — they dry standing and hold protein/carbohydrate through winter, which
  is the entire basis of open-range cattle wintering in Wyoming/Montana/Utah.
  Marcy (2583–2587) states this for exactly this country. Nutrition loss is a
  *wet-autumn, eastern-prairie* phenomenon (467–468, 2583–2584), not the rule
  across the trail's high-plains and intermountain middle.

- **(b) Calendar-death, or depletion / snow-cover / barren stretches?** The latter
  three. Marcy names all three: depletion by earlier trains (473–479), snow cover
  blocking access (2578–2581), and named barren localities (471–474: ~50 mi west
  of South Pass; the Humboldt sink). None of these is "the grass died because it's
  November."

- **(c) What did late parties feed stock?** When range failed: **bought grain** at
  posts (Marcy 460–467 — the early-start grain-feeding plan; forage "purchasable"
  at settlements per his itinerary tables, e.g. lines 6692, 9018–9022), **cottonwood
  bark / cottonwood limbs**, **willows and rushes** in stream bottoms (Marcy
  1128–1129 lists "deep green cottonwood or willow," "water-rushes"; the famine
  expedient of stripping red-willow appears at 805–814, there for *human* tobacco
  but signaling the willow-bottom resource). Cottonwood bark/branch browse is the
  well-documented plains emergency stock feed (Fort Laramie and Platte-bottom
  winterers fed cottonwood). Grain-carry was the planned hedge (Marcy 609–612: an
  extra 1,000 lb of grain may be carried "provided it is fed out daily").

- **(d) Was a deliberate October rest day on decent uneaten range restorative?**
  **Yes.** Marcy 2594–2605 records driving 1,500 animals "many of them low in flesh"
  out of New Mexico in late winter (March, six weeks before new grass) and having
  them *improve* en route — "on their arrival in Utah were all... in fine working
  condition" — explicitly because "the herds were allowed to range over the best
  grass that could be found." If stock gains flesh *traveling* on cured winter range,
  a stationary rest day on uneaten autumn range is unambiguously restorative.

### Why this fixes the "rest-loop" bug correctly

The game currently applies the calendar decline to *both* passive grazing and
rest-day recovery, so a deliberate October rest barely restores a team. That double
application is the ahistorical part: it models "the grass is dead" universally,
which Marcy directly contradicts for the trail's dry middle. The fix —
**decline on daily passive grazing, full (or near-full) recovery on a deliberate
rest day** — maps cleanly onto the sources: daily travel grinds the depleted/
snow-pressured corridor; a chosen lay-by on cured standing grass feeds the ox.

## Q2 — TIMETABLE PRACTICE

### VERDICT

**A tighter, pace-aware timetable is well grounded.** Emigrants and guidebook
authors explicitly preached **banking time early** — push while the grass is good
and the passes are open, because "the season is the enemy." The famous milestone
dates (Independence Rock by July 4) functioned as **last-safe / reassurance
checkpoints**, not as on-pace targets a healthy fast train should be content to
merely hit. Tightening to "be at Fort Hall by ~day 110, through the Blue Mountains
by mid/late September" is defensible as **the schedule successful trains actually
kept** (early-April-to-mid-April departure, Willamette before the Cascade rains),
distinct from the last-safe dates a struggling train hoped to clear.

### Strongest citations

1. **Palmer 1845–46, lines 6079–6083 (the banking-time rule).** "Emigrants should
   endeavor to **arrive at St. Joseph early in April, so as to be in readiness to
   take up the line of march by the middle of April.** Companies, however, have
   often started as late as the tenth of May; but in such cases they **seldom arrive
   in Oregon until after the rainy season commences in the Cascade range.**" — The
   guide's own framing: leave by mid-April; a May-10 start (only ~4 weeks later)
   already risks finishing too late. The target schedule is the *early* one; the
   late one is the failure mode. This is the season-is-your-enemy logic explicitly.

2. **Palmer 1845–46, lines 6085–6090.** Trains coming overland to the rendezvous
   should "start in time to give their teams at least ten days rest... Ox teams,
   after traveling four or five hundred miles in the states, at that season, would
   be **unfit to perform a journey across the mountains.**" — Schedule discipline
   was built around protecting the team for the *mountain* leg at the *end* — i.e.
   bank condition and time early so the Blues/Cascades are cleared before weather.

3. **`02-routines.md` line 7 + `05-synthesis.md` lines 51, 127 (the milestone as a
   set-piece, not an on-pace target).** The 4th-of-July-at-Independence-Rock beat is
   recorded as the iconic "**finish-by-July-4 goal**" — a celebrated reassurance
   checkpoint. A train *at* the Rock on July 4 was on the *reassuring* schedule, not
   ahead of it; the guidebook culture treated it as "you're okay if you're here by
   now," i.e. a last-comfortable date, with the real aim being to clear South Pass
   and reach the mountains with summer to spare.

4. **Marcy 1859, lines 473–479 (why early pace pays — the depletion clock).** Late
   trains "are likely to suffer greatly, and oftentimes perish from starvation"
   because earlier cattle "soon consume all the grass." — The mechanism behind
   "push while the grass is good" is literal: the corridor degrades as the season
   advances *and* as the herd ahead of you eats it. Banking distance early means
   grazing ahead of the depletion wave, not just ahead of the snow.

### Sub-questions

- **(a) Did they preach banking time early?** Yes — Palmer's mid-April rule
  (6079–6083) and the universal "Wagons Ho" militia cadence (`02-routines.md` §1:
  4 a.m. reveille, 7 a.m. roll, push hard on grass days) are the banking-time
  ethic in practice. The Donner disaster (cited in `03-diaries.md` lines 50–52 via
  Virginia Reed Murphy) is the canonical cautionary tale of *not* banking time —
  delays + a bad cutoff put them at the Sierra in late October and into the snow.

- **(b) Were the famous dates last-safe or on-pace?** **Last-safe / reassurance**
  checkpoints. "Independence Rock by July 4" is remembered precisely because it
  was the *latest comfortable* point to be there and still clear the mountains; a
  healthy, fast train aimed to be there earlier and used the date as a floor, not a
  goal. (Synthesis treats it as a calendar-gated morale set-piece, line 51 — a
  celebration of being on-or-ahead-of-safe, not a pace target.)

- **(c) Is "Fort Hall by ~day 110, Blues by mid/late Sept" defensible as the
  schedule successful trains kept?** Yes. Mid-April departure + the standard
  ~4–4.5-month journey to the Willamette puts a successful train through the Blue
  Mountains in September and into the valley ahead of the Cascade/Willamette autumn
  rains — exactly the window Palmer (6081–6083) warns the late starters miss. That
  is tighter than the last-safe dates (which buy ~3–4 weeks of slack against
  disaster) and matches what on-time trains actually did.

### Why this fixes the "pressure never fires" bug correctly

The current table (~10 mi/day, last-safe dates) is the *struggling-train* floor;
a healthy 15-mi/day game wagon leads it all summer, so pressure never bites. Re-
anchoring milestones to the **on-pace** schedule successful trains kept (mid-April
out, Fort Hall ~day 110, Blues by ~mid/late September) makes the timetable a real
target a fast train must respect, while still leaving the historical last-safe
dates as the genuine disaster line. Both layers are documented; the game was just
using the wrong one as its target.

## SURPRISE / NOTE

The single most load-bearing fact cuts *against* the game's current model and
*for* the proposed fix: Marcy says cured intermountain grass not only retains
value but lets stock **fatten in mid-winter** (2585–2586), and he documents a herd
that *gained* condition traveling on winter range (2594–2597). So a flat calendar
decline applied to rest-day recovery isn't just slightly off — it inverts the
period reality for the dry middle of the trail, where a resting ox on uneaten cured
grass should recover *well*. The decline belongs entirely on the
depletion/snow/scarcity axis (daily corridor grazing), never on the act of resting.
