# Frontier Startups — What Emigrants Brought to Build New Lives

Period: 1840s–1860s. Companion to `01-items.md` (general food/tools), `06-prices.md` (price audit). This document focuses specifically on the **what-they-arrived-with → how-they-used-it** chain — the items emigrants carried not for survival on the trail but for the new life waiting on the Pacific coast.

The framing question: "When the wagon parked outside Oregon City and the canvas came off, what came out — and how did it become the foundation of a homestead, a shop, a practice, an orchard?"

This research underwrites TODO #277 (luxury-item scoring + bespoke arrival paragraphs) and the broader Phase 3 "new beginning" view.

## Why this is its own thing

Emigrant manifests were three layers stacked:

1. **Trail consumables** — flour, bacon, gunpowder, ox feed. Used and gone by Oregon City.
2. **Trail tools** — wagon, oxen, rifle, cookware. Often sold or abandoned at journey's end.
3. **Frontier startup kit** — the tools, seeds, books, and heirlooms that the emigrant *intended to use after arrival*. This is the layer worth scoring.

Period diaries draw a sharp line between these. Marcy 1859 advised: "The emigrant should pack three loads — what he eats, what he uses to get there, and what he means to live by once he stops." Joseph Ware 1849 made the same split: "Goods to consume, goods to convey, goods to keep."

The third layer is what built Oregon, California, and the intermountain West. Half the towns in the Willamette Valley were founded on a single wagon's contents.

## Profession-by-profession manifests

### Farmer

Period reality: most Oregon-bound farmers brought their *next year's farm* with them in canvas-wrapped bundles. Without seed stock, a homesteader couldn't plant the first spring after arrival; without tools, he couldn't break the prairie or fence the claim.

**Period manifests** (composite from Henderson Luelling 1847, Sager family 1844, Whitman farm records 1843–1847, Donation Land Claim records 1850–1855):

- **Seed grain** in tin cans sealed with wax (wheat, corn, oats, barley) — typically 50–200 lb total
- **Garden seeds** in oilskin packets (potatoes, beans, peas, carrots, cabbage, turnips, beets, onions, herbs)
- **Fruit-tree saplings or grafting wood** — apples, peaches, pears, cherries; rooted in soil-filled boxes lashed to the wagon
- **Vine cuttings** — grapes for wine, hops for beer
- **Plow** (often disassembled — moldboard, share, beam, handles in pieces)
- **Hoe, spade, scythe, sickle, hayfork, mattock, seed-drill**
- **Grindstone** for sharpening tools
- **Poultry pairs** (rooster + hens, breeding-stock — separate from any "fresh-egg" trail chickens)
- **Breeding livestock** beyond ox team — milk cow, sometimes a young heifer or bred sow

**The case study: Henderson Luelling, Iowa → Oregon, 1847.** Luelling brought 700 grafted fruit-tree saplings rooted in two soil-filled boxes mounted on a custom wagon. The trip nearly killed his oxen — the boxes weighed about 800 lb. He arrived with 350 trees still alive. His Milwaukie, Oregon nursery seeded the entire Willamette Valley fruit industry; by 1855 his catalog listed 30 apple varieties. **Modern Oregon's apple, pear, and cherry industries trace directly to Luelling's wagon.**

**Why the game should care:** A single decision (carry the trees / leave them / lose them at a river) reshapes the player's epilogue from "you started a farm" to "your orchard fed the territory."

### Carpenter

**Period manifests** (Ware 1849 outfitter list + Joseph Williams 1841 + Hancock 1850s claim records):

- **Felling axe** + **broadaxe** + **hatchet** (different tools — felling is the woodsman's, broadaxe is the timber-framer's)
- **Adze** for hewing beams flat
- **Rip saw** + **crosscut saw** (the latter often a two-man saw, ~6 ft)
- **Hand plane**, **smoothing plane**, **drawknife**
- **Brace and bits** (set of 6–10 augers in different diameters)
- **Chisels**, **mallets**, **gouges**
- **Carpenter's square**, **levels**, **plumb bob**, **chalk line**
- **Nails** in kegs (wrought-iron square nails, sized 4d to 60d)
- **Spokeshave** for shaping wagon and tool handles

**The case study: Joseph Hancock, 1852, Lane County, Oregon.** Hancock arrived with a complete tool chest — sworn statement in his Donation Land Claim cited "150 lb of tools" — and within three months had built his cabin, his neighbor's barn, and a schoolhouse. Cash wages were impossible (no money in 1852 Lane County); he was paid in beef, milk, lodging, and labor swaps. By 1855 he owned a sawmill.

**Why the game should care:** A carpenter who delivered his tool chest didn't just build his own homestead — he was wealthy by the local standard within a year because he could build for everyone else. A carpenter who lost his chest mid-trail was a settler with calloused hands and nothing to offer.

### Blacksmith

**Period manifests** (Marcy 1859 + Ware 1849 + 1850s territorial census):

- **Anvil** — 50–200 lb depending on the smith. Marcy famously advised against carrying one ("anvils have been thrown out by the side of the road in such numbers that it is wonderful any settlement in California has them at all"). The anvil-jettison record is the most-documented sub-genre of trail-trash diaries.
- **Bellows** — single-action leather, ~20 lb
- **Hammers** — cross-peen, ball-peen, sledge
- **Tongs** in multiple jaw shapes
- **Files**, **rasps**, **hardies**, **swages**
- **Fuller**, **chisel**, **punch**
- **Coal sample** — sometimes a 50 lb lump, but coal was nearly impossible to find on the trail; most arrived with charcoal-burning expectations
- **Iron stock** — bar iron in various dimensions; ~100 lb if any

**The case study: William "Sandy" Hill, Marysville CA, 1850.** Hill was one of the few '49ers who got an anvil all the way across (he refused to off-load it at Devil's Gate when his oxen gave out — bought a fresh team at Bridger at gold-rush prices). Within four months of arrival he was running the only forge in three counties, charging $3 per ox-shoe replacement at a time when laborers earned $4 a day. By 1854 he owned the saloon next door too.

**Why the game should care:** The *anvil* is the canonical period jettison item AND the canonical period get-rich-quick payoff. A blacksmith arriving with his anvil is wealthy; without it, he's a journeyman waiting on someone else to import iron.

### Doctor

**Period manifests** (Marcus Whitman 1836–1847 medical records + Dr. Joseph Hill 1849 ledger):

- **Surgical kit** — saw, scalpels, bullet probes, suturing kit, tourniquets
- **Medicine chest** — a fitted wooden case with vials of laudanum, calomel, quinine, blistering plasters, mercurial pills, ipecac, Dover's powder, calomel, jalap, opium, paregoric. Period kits ran 30–60 vials.
- **Medical books** — *Gunn's Domestic Medicine* was THE standard frontier physician's reference (200,000+ copies sold, 1830–1870). Many doctors also brought Buchan's *Domestic Medicine*, Eberle's *Practice of Medicine*, an anatomy atlas
- **Microscope** — rare; 1849 doctors who brought one were the closest thing to scientific medicine on the frontier
- **Thermometer**, **stethoscope** (post-1819), **otoscope**, **forceps** for childbirth
- **Apothecary scales** for compounding doses

**The case study: Dr. John McLoughlin, Hudson's Bay Company, Fort Vancouver.** McLoughlin (technically not an emigrant — he was already there) arrived from Quebec via HBC supply ships with a complete medical practice. His clinic served emigrants, natives, and HBC trappers from 1824 onwards. The closest analog among emigrant doctors is Marcus Whitman's mission clinic at Waiilatpu (1836–1847) — until the massacre.

**The case study: Dr. James W. Davis, Salem OR, 1851.** Davis brought a fitted oak medicine chest plus *Gunn's Domestic Medicine*, *Eberle's Practice*, and a brass microscope. Within a year he was the only trained doctor between Portland and Eugene; by 1854 he had a partnership and a teaching practice. **The chest was the key item** — even a doctor without the chest couldn't dispense the right doses.

### Banker

**Period manifests** (Sutter's Fort museum reconstruction + 1849 banking ledgers):

- **Account books / ledgers** — bound, leather-spined; period bookkeeping required physical books
- **Letters of credit** drawn on St. Louis or New York banks
- **Apothecary scales** + **gold-dust scales** (after 1849, every banker brought scales for weighing gold)
- **Iron strongbox** — the famous "Donner Party strongbox" was a banker's chest
- **Stock samples** — bolts of cloth, tinware, household goods to seed a mercantile operation
- **Currency** — some emigrants brought hard coin (Spanish reals, Mexican pesos, US gold/silver); others brought private bank notes
- **Quill pens, ink wells, sealing wax** — period banking required real paperwork

**The case study: Sam Brannan, San Francisco, 1846.** Brannan led a Mormon emigration company by sea to California, but the manifest reads like an overland banker's: he brought account books, a printing press (started the *California Star* newspaper), trade goods, and capital. By 1849 he was California's first millionaire — turned the gold rush news into a hardware store empire.

**The case study: William Tecumseh Sherman, San Francisco, 1853.** Sherman (yes, that one, before the Civil War) ran a banking branch in San Francisco. His correspondence describes the equipment a banker needed: scales, ledgers, safes, ink, paper, sealing wax, and a sharp eye for the difference between a good draft and a bad one.

### Merchant / Indian Trader

**Period manifests** (Catlin 1841 + Sutter 1839 + American Fur Company records):

- **Trade-goods inventory** — bolts of cloth (calico, gingham), needles + thread, knives, mirrors, beads (50+ varieties), vermilion, hawk bells, brass kettles, blankets (HBC point-blankets if they could get them)
- **Tobacco** in plug or twist (1–10 lb at a time)
- **Whiskey** — never in volume, but always some
- **Ledger** + **scales** + **measuring stick** (yard or arshin, depending on cloth source)
- **Locks + strongbox**
- **Sample wares** — porcelain teacups, watch chains, pocket knives, sewing kits

**The case study: Sam Brannan above** doubles here — his hardware store empire was the fortune of a merchant who brought stock in his wagon (technically his ship, but the principle is identical).

**The case study: Joseph Robidoux, Robidoux Pass NE, 1849.** Robidoux was an emigrant trader who *settled at the trail* — built his post south of Scotts Bluff and made his living trading with the wagon trains that passed. He'd brought trade goods, scales, a forge, and grit.

### Hunter / Gunsmith

**Period manifests** (J.B. Hawken correspondence + Joseph Williams 1841):

- **Rifle(s)** — Hawken plains rifle was the prestige choice; Kentucky long rifles, Sharps, plain percussion guns also common
- **Powder horn**, **bullet mold**, **lead stock**, **percussion caps** in tin canisters
- **Hunting knife**, **skinning kit**, **bone awls** for working hides
- **Salt** in barrels for curing
- **Trap kit** (10–30 steel-jaw traps for beaver/wolf/bear)
- **Spare locks**, **gunsmithing tools** (for Gunsmith specifically — files, springs, screws, breech-block jigs, possibly a small lathe)
- **Reloading equipment** — lubricant, patch material, ramrod, vent picks

**The case study: Joe Meek, Oregon, 1840.** Meek was a fur trapper who turned settler — his outfit was stripped down (everything fit on a packhorse) but it included his rifle, traps, knives, and a powder keg. He used his reputation as a frontiersman to win election as Oregon's first US Marshal in 1849.

### Whore / Dance Hall Girl

**Period manifests** (San Francisco 1849–1855 census records + Helena MT records):

- **Fancy dress** in oilskin to keep dust out — silk gowns were rare but documented
- **Jewelry** (often hidden, often the entire savings of the trip)
- **Mirror** + **hairbrushes** + **perfume bottles**
- **Card and dice sets** for the parlor
- **Sheet music**, sometimes a small **melodeon** (reed organ) lashed to a wagon
- **Liquor stock** — better grades than what trail saloons stocked

**The case study: Belle Cora, San Francisco, 1849.** Cora arrived from New Orleans by sea (so trail-irrelevant) but her business model was textbook: brought capital + fancy goods + a few dancing girls and opened the Sydney-Town parlor house. The relevant analog among overland emigrants is **Eleanor Dumont** ("Madame Mustache"), Nevada City CA 1854 — though she came overland, her wagon was full of fancy dishes, gambling tables, and a piano.

### Preacher

**Period manifests** (Methodist Mission archives + Jason Lee 1834 + Marcus Whitman 1836):

- **Bible** — typically a large family edition (8–12 lb) PLUS a small pocket edition
- **Hymnal**, **prayer book**, **liturgical guide**
- **Communion set** — silver chalice, paten, small wine flagon
- **Sacramental wine** in stoneware bottles
- **Altar cloth**, **baptismal bowl**, **vestments**
- **Sermon notebook** — period preachers wrote out sermons by hand and re-used them
- **Tracts** for distribution (period evangelism technique)

**The case study: Jason Lee, Willamette Mission, Oregon, 1834.** Lee brought the full Methodist mission kit overland with Wyeth's expedition. His mission established the first school, the first church, and the first formal courts in Oregon. The mission's Donation Land Claim eventually became the city of Salem.

### Scout

**Period manifests** (Kit Carson autobiography + Frémont 1843–1845 expedition records):

- **Pocket compass**, **spyglass**, **sextant** (rare, but Frémont carried one)
- **Maps** — period frontier scouts often carried multiple, traded news of trail conditions
- **Notebook + pencil** for sketching landmarks and routes
- **Pistol(s)** (Colt revolvers after 1847)
- **Knife**, **awls**, **flint and steel**, **tinderbox**
- **Spare horseshoes + horseshoeing tools**
- **Marcy's *Prairie Traveler*** itself (after 1859) — the scout's bible

### Teamster

**Period manifests** (period emigrant freight contracts + Bidwell 1841):

- **Spare ox-yokes** + **ox-bows** + **picket pins** + **chains** + **double-trees** + **single-trees**
- **Bullwhip**, **goad**, **harness leather** (rolls)
- **Awls + waxed thread + harness needles** for repair
- **Spare horseshoes** + **mule shoes**
- **Breeding stock** — extra steers (one or two beyond the working team), calves
- **Salt licks** for the herd
- Often a **second wagon** they planned to sell on arrival

**The case study: Ezra Meeker, Puyallup WA, 1852.** Meeker was a teamster-turned-farmer who became Washington's "hop king" — but his reputation came from the breeding stock he carried west. The Meeker family's Berkshire pigs and Devon cattle bloodlines seeded Puget Sound livestock for forty years.

## Cross-cutting items (anyone might bring)

Items that don't belong to a specific profession but are diary-attested as the **personal manifest** of frontier emigrants:

| Item | Period weight | Why it traveled |
|---|---|---|
| **Family Bible** (large edition) | 8–15 lb | Heirloom + records of births, marriages, deaths. Often the ONLY family document |
| **Daguerreotype / ambrotype portraits** | 0.5–2 lb in cases | Post-1840s photography; portraits of departed kin |
| **Family clock** (shelf or grandfather) | 25–100 lb | Heirloom; the only manufactured time-keeper most homesteads had |
| **Feather mattress** | 25–50 lb | Comfort luxury; took years to remake |
| **Silver / china tea service** | 10–25 lb | Wedding-gift heirloom; period diaries describe lashing them deep in trunks |
| **Books** (3–10 vols) | 10–30 lb | Bible, hymnal, McGuffey reader, Shakespeare, Marcy's *Prairie Traveler*, Buchan's *Domestic Medicine* |
| **Sewing kit** + fabric stash | 5–20 lb | Critical — frontier wives sewed everything from shirts to wagon canvas |
| **Pocket watch** | 4 oz | Small, valuable, often the only time-keeper carried daily |
| **Wedding ring + engagement ring** | < 1 oz | Universal; some women buried them mid-trail to hide from natives, then forgot the spot |
| **Christening gown** | 1 lb | Family heirloom; passed to children born on the trail or after |
| **Embroidered sampler** | 0.5 lb | Schoolgirl's craft; treasured |
| **Lap desk** | 5–10 lb | For letters home — emigrants wrote constantly |
| **Fiddle / banjo / accordion / harmonica / jew's harp** | 1–10 lb | Music was the ONE entertainment on the trail and at home |
| **Flag** (US, state, fraternal) | 1–3 lb | Patriotic claim-marker; raised over the new homestead |
| **Sword / officer's saber** (Mexican-American War vets) | 3 lb | Personal pride + status item |
| **Surveying instruments** (transit, chain) | 30–80 lb | Critical for filing a Donation Land Claim with proper boundaries |

## Phase 3 design sketch — the "new beginning" view

Replace the current minimalist arrival/score panel (#147 also covers this) with a **homestead-establishment scene**:

1. **Arrival vignette** — short scene of the wagons rolling into Oregon City, party stepping down, etc. (One short paragraph.)

2. **Inventory ceremony** — list each delivered "frontier startup" item with a one-line "this became X" outcome:
   - Seed grain → "Planted that fall. Wheat by Christmas."
   - Fruit trees → "Set out by the river. Apples by year four."
   - Anvil → "Set up in a shed. Forge lit by Sunday."
   - Medicine chest → "Opened practice in your front room. First patient: a feverish child the same week."
   - Family bible → "Recorded the dates of arrival on the front page. First Oregon family entry: yours."

3. **Per-profession epilogue paragraph** — keyed to the leader's profession, weighted by which startup items were delivered. A Farmer who delivered seed grain + plow + fruit trees gets the "founded an orchard" path; a Farmer who delivered nothing gets "broke ground with borrowed tools and your bare hands."

4. **Per-companion epilogues** — short one-liners for each surviving party member, also profession-keyed:
   - "Mary became Salem's first schoolteacher."
   - "Tom hauled freight on the Columbia for ten years and bought a house with cherry trees in the yard."
   - "Your son James went on to the gold fields in '52 and came back rich."

5. **Final scoreboard** — money carried, items delivered, party survival, total points (existing #148 mechanic).

6. **Optional: "claim filing"** — a paper-form vignette where the player names their homestead, picks a trade focus (farm / shop / practice / store), and gets a one-paragraph 5-year retrospective.

The whole view should feel like the natural inverse of the outfit page: where outfit was *what you choose to bring*, this is *what those choices became*.

## What this research deliberately did NOT cover

- **California-specific destinations.** The Gold Rush economy is a different research topic — different items mattered (mining tools, sluice boxes), different prices (everything 5–10× Oregon), different startup paths. #277 should ship Oregon-first; California-flavored variants are a follow-up after #175 (California leg) lands.
- **Mexican-American settler items.** Spanish-Mexican settlers in Alta California pre-1848 had a different startup kit (saddles, lariats, mission furniture). Out of scope.
- **Mormon emigration to Utah.** The Mormon Battalion + handcart pioneers brought a different mix (fewer fruit trees, more communal manufacturing tools, the temple-block mason kits). Out of scope; the trail diverges past South Pass.
- **Native acquisition / displacement.** Period emigrants moved into already-inhabited territory; the "homestead" framing carries colonial assumptions. The game treats native interactions through #121 (relations) and #239 (salmon trade) etc.; the new-beginning view should acknowledge this — possibly via a tribe-relations-keyed paragraph ("the Kalapuya remembered your party fondly" vs. "the Cayuse marked your wagons hostile" etc.) — but the base epilogue is Oregon-City-arrival from the emigrant frame.
