# Items Emigrants Carried

Period: 1840s–1860s. Sources: Marcy's *Prairie Traveler* (1859), Joel Palmer (1847), Lansford Hastings (1845), Joseph Ware (1849), OCTA, NPS, End of the Oregon Trail Interpretive Center.

## 1. Food staples (per adult, ~5-month / ~110-day journey)

The two canonical lists are **Ware 1849** (heavier) and **Marcy 1859** (leaner — Marcy assumed beef on the hoof and game would supplement). Quantities below are per adult unless noted.

| Item | Ware 1849 | Marcy 1859 | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | **200 lb** (or hard bread equivalent) | **150 lb** | Bread, pancakes, gravy thickener |
| Bacon / side pork | **150–200 lb**, packed in barrels of bran to keep cool | **25 lb** + beef on hoof | Primary fat & protein |
| Coffee | **25–40 lb** (green beans, roasted en route) | **15 lb** | Daily ritual; masked bad water |
| Sugar | **25–50 lb** | **25 lb** | Coffee, baking, morale |
| Rice | 25 lb | — | Stretch staple |
| Dried beans | 25–50 lb | — | Slow-cook protein |
| Dried fruit | 15–25 lb (apples, peaches) | — | Antiscorbutic, sweetness |
| Salt | 10–25 lb | listed | Preservation, cooking |
| Pepper | ~½ lb | listed | Flavor |
| Saleratus (baking soda) | 5–10 lb | listed | Leavening; also water-alkali neutralizer |
| Tea | 5 lb | — | Often preferred by women over coffee |
| Vinegar | 1–2 gal | — | Antiscorbutic, pickling, cleaning |
| Hardtack | optional | listed | Backup when fires impossible |
| Cornmeal | 50 lb | — | Cornbread, mush |
| Lard | 25–50 lb | — | Frying, shortening |
| Pemmican / "cold flour" (parched cornmeal + sugar) | optional | recommended | Trail snack, no cooking required |

Many parties also drove a small herd (4–6 head) of beef cattle and one or two milk cows.

## 2. Cooking & kitchen

Typically packed for a "mess" (family or 6–8 person group):

- **Cast-iron Dutch oven** with three legs and lid — bread, stews, baked beans
- **Iron skillet / spider** (spider = legged frying pan)
- **Iron kettle** (3–5 gal) for boiling water, washing
- **Coffee pot** (tin or copper), **coffee mill** (hand grinder)
- **Tin plates, tin cups, tin/horn spoons** — pewter and china broke; most diaries describe tinware
- **Butcher knife, carving knife, two-tine forks**
- **Water keg** lashed to the wagon side (5–10 gal)
- **Wooden bucket + dipper**
- **Rolling pin, dough box / breadboard**
- **Camp tripod or pothooks** ("trammel") for hanging kettle over fire
- **Tin reflector oven** (lighter alternative to dutch oven)
- **Churn** — a one-gallon churn lashed under the wagon would be agitated by the day's jostling — diary-attested butter trick

## 3. Tools

- **Axe** (felling) and **hatchet** (camp wood) — universal
- **Crosscut saw** and/or **handsaw**
- **Shovel and spade** — graves, mud, latrines, fire pits
- **Pickaxe / mattock** — required at rocky crossings
- **Auger and gimlet** — for re-pegging wagon parts
- **Drawknife, plane, chisel** — woodworking on broken parts
- **Hammer, nails (assorted kegs), screws**
- **Files, whetstone** — knife and axe maintenance
- **Awl + leather punch + harness needles + waxed thread** — harness/boot repair
- **Pliers, pincers**
- **Small bellows + hand-forge** (rare; most emigrants relied on fort blacksmiths — Marcy advised against carrying a full anvil; lists of jettisoned goods on the 1849 trail are full of abandoned anvils)
- **Rope (50–100 ft hemp), pulley/block-and-tackle** — for letting wagons down cliffs (Laurel Hill, Big Hill)

## 4. Hunting / firearms

Marcy and Ware both insist every adult man carry a long arm plus a sidearm.

- **Rifle** — most commonly a **.50–.54 caliber half-stock plains rifle** (Hawken-style); after ~1855 increasingly **percussion-cap** rather than flintlock
- **Shotgun** — double-barrel 12-gauge for birds, small game
- **Revolver** — Colt Walker / Dragoon / Navy by the 1850s; not always carried by farmers
- **Powder** — 25 lb black powder per rifle for the trip (Marcy)
- **Lead** — 25 lb in **bar form**, plus a **bullet mold** sized to the rifle
- **Percussion caps** — at least 1,000 (a tin)
- **Powder horn / powder flask, ball pouch, patch material, ramrod**
- **Cleaning kit** — worm, jag, oil, tow
- **Fishing tackle** — hooks, line, sinkers (small and cheap; widely diary-attested)
- **Knife / belt knife** — Bowie or butcher style

Realism note: most diaries report buffalo encountered briefly near the Platte; emigrants ate far more bacon and beef-on-the-hoof than wild game.

## 5. Medicine & first aid

**Yes — Epsom salts were standard.** Magnesium sulfate had been a household cathartic since the 1700s; period medical guides (Gunn's *Domestic Medicine*, 1830s) list it as the milder purgative alongside the harsher mercury-based calomel. Emigrants carried it for constipation, "bilious complaints," and as a wound-soak.

The typical 1840s–60s wagon medicine chest:

- **Calomel** (mercurous chloride) — purgative; given for "bilious fever," dysentery (toxic; caused mercury poisoning) — *we have it*
- **Epsom salts** — milder purgative, wound soak — *MISSING*
- **Quinine sulfate** — malaria/"ague" fevers, especially leaving Missouri — *we have it*
- **Laudanum** (tincture of opium) — pain, diarrhea, cough, sleep — *we have it*
- **Paregoric** (camphorated opium tincture, weaker) — children's diarrhea, teething — *MISSING*
- **Camphor / spirits of camphor** — rubbed on chest for colds, "cholera prophylaxis" — *MISSING*
- **Hartshorn** (ammonium carbonate, "smelling salts") — fainting, snakebite folk-remedy — *MISSING*
- **Castor oil** — purgative for children — *MISSING*
- **Peppermint oil / essence** — stomach upset, gas — *MISSING*
- **Citric acid or lemon syrup** — anti-scorbutic (Marcy specifically recommends) — *we have vinegar*
- **Whiskey or brandy** — anesthetic, disinfectant, "stimulant" for cholera victims — *we have whiskey*
- **Hartshorn liniment / turpentine / "Number Six" (cayenne tincture)** — rubs and wound dressings — *MISSING*
- **Sticking plaster, lint, bandages, splints** — *we have bandages*
- **Dover's powder** (opium + ipecac) — fevers, induced sweating — *MISSING*
- A **home medical guide** — Gunn's *Domestic Medicine* or Beach's *American Practice* was nearly as common as a Bible — *MISSING*

## 6. Personal effects & clothing

Marcy's per-man clothing (3-month outfit; emigrants doubled it):

- 2 flannel overshirts, 2 wool undershirts, 2 cotton drawers, 4 wool + 2 cotton socks, 4 silk handkerchiefs, **stout boots**, an extra pair of brogans, **broad-brimmed hat**, **poncho** or oilcloth
- For women: 2–3 calico/linsey-woolsey dresses, sunbonnet, shawl, apron

Other personal:

- **Sewing kit** ("housewife"/"hussif"): needles, pins, thread, scissors, thimble, button card
- **Soap** — homemade lye soap bars; ~5 lb per family
- **Tallow candles** — 2–3 lb; candle molds for re-pouring
- **Tinder box / flint & steel / lucifer matches** (matches widely available by 1845)
- **Bible** — nearly universal — *we have it*
- **Journal/diary, ink bottle, quill or steel pens, paper**
- **Comb, razor, strop, looking glass (small)**
- **Towels, washbasin (tin)**
- **Bedroll**: two wool blankets, comforter, pillow, india-rubber/oilcloth ground sheet
- **Tent** — A-frame canvas wedge, ~10×10
- **Pocket watch, spectacles**
- **Fiddle, harmonica, jaw harp** — diary-attested for nightly camp music — *we have fiddle, harmonica*

## 7. Wagon & oxen gear

- **Wagon** — farm wagon (~10 ft bed) with **bows + canvas bonnet** treated with linseed oil; NOT a Conestoga (those were freight wagons too heavy for oxen on the trail)
- **Spare wagon tongue** lashed underneath — *we have it*
- **Spare axle** — *we have it*
- **Spare wheel** (or at least felloes, spokes, hub) — *we have it*
- **Spare iron tire / hoop** — *MISSING*
- **Tar bucket + axle grease** — pine tar mixed with tallow, hung from the rear axle, used every 200 miles or so — *we have tar_bucket*
- **Linchpins, kingbolts, S-hooks** (Marcy: 6 S's and 6 open chain links per wagon) — *MISSING*
- **Heavy log chain + rope** — for double-teaming and lowering wagons — *we have rope*
- **Wagon jack** — *MISSING*
- **Oxen** — 4–6 head per wagon, plus 2 spares — *we have it*
- **Yokes** (one per pair) and **2 spare ox bows** per wagon (Marcy explicit) — *we have yoke; missing spare bows*
- **Bull whip / ox goad** — *MISSING*
- **Hobbles + picket pins + lariats** for night grazing — *MISSING*
- **Salt** (additional — for the stock) — handled via salt
- **Horseshoes / ox shoes + nails** (oxen need 8 small shoes each — they have cloven hooves) — *we have forge ox shoes service; missing inventory item*

## 8. Trade goods

Carried specifically for barter at Fort Laramie, Fort Hall, Fort Bridger, and with Plains and Plateau tribes:

- **Tobacco** (twist or plug) — most-requested item, by every account — *we have it*
- **Glass beads** — Venetian "seed" beads, especially blue and white — *we have it*
- **Small mirrors / "looking-glasses"** — *MISSING*
- **Brass thimbles, brass wire, hawk bells** — *MISSING*
- **Vermilion** (red pigment for face paint) — *MISSING*
- **Awls, fish hooks, needles** — *MISSING*
- **Cheap butcher knives / pocket knives** — *MISSING*
- **Wool blankets** (Hudson's Bay-style point blankets if affordable) — *we have blanket*
- **Calico cloth and ribbon** — *MISSING*
- **Powder, lead, percussion caps** (also a trade item west of South Pass) — *we have these*
- **Coffee and sugar** — willingly traded by tribes who'd acquired the taste — *we have*
- **Combs, jew's-harps, "jumping-jack" toys** — diary-attested novelty trades — *MISSING*
- **Hard bread / flour** — traded to mountain men running low — *we have*

## 9. Surprising omissions and quirks

- **Pianos and parlor organs** — myth confirmed: yes, some did try; the trail from Fort Laramie west is famously littered with abandoned ones. **Most period guides explicitly told emigrants NOT to bring them**; clipper-ship freight around Cape Horn was the actual route for heavy furniture.
- **Milk cows** — yes, very common. Most families brought 1–2; the daily-jostle butter churn trick is repeatedly diary-attested. (Already on TODO #139.)
- **Chickens** — common, in slat crates lashed to the wagon side; eggs were a real trail food. — *we have it*
- **Dogs** — common, often working dogs (drovers, watch). Diaries are ambivalent — some celebrated, some lost or eaten in famine years. — *we have it*
- **Cats** — much rarer than dogs; a few diary mentions but not a typical pack item.
- **Books beyond the Bible** — yes: school primers (McGuffey), trail guides, Pilgrim's Progress, almanacs, and the medical guide. Volumes of Shakespeare and Burns appear in some diaries; serious libraries did not survive past Fort Laramie.
- **Stoves** — many brought small sheet-iron camp stoves; most were jettisoned by the Sweetwater. Cooking was usually over open fires fueled by **buffalo chips** on the treeless plains (a near-universal surprise to Eastern emigrants).
- **No tents for some** — many slept under or beside the wagon; a true tent was a luxury.
- **Side-saddles, plows, looms, butter churns, barrels of seed grain** — all packed, all in jettison-pile lists. Lists from 1849 record "piles of most beautiful bacon" abandoned because parties had **over-packed Ware-quantity rations**.
- **No canned food to speak of** — tin canning existed (1810s) but was expensive, heavy, and rare on emigrant wagons until the Civil War. Marcy mentions desiccated (dried) vegetables as a novelty.
- **No coffee filters, no tea bags, no toilet paper** (corn cobs, leaves, or the *Missouri Republican*).
- **No refrigeration of any kind** — all meat was salted, smoked, or driven on the hoof.
- **Sextants/compasses** — rare among emigrants; they followed wagon ruts and trail guides, not navigation. Marcy's *Prairie Traveler* recommends a compass; few diaries mention owning one.
