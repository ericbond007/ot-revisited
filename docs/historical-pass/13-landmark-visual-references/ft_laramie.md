## What it actually looked like (1843–1860)

### Period A: Fort John, 1843–1848 (American Fur Company adobe trading post)

Emigrants approaching from the east crossed the North Platte bottomlands and saw, set on a low bluff above the junction of the Laramie and North Platte rivers, an adobe rectangle that looked — to eyes accustomed to 650 miles of unbroken prairie — like a small fortified town dropped from the sky. John C. Frémont, writing of his 1842 visit in his government expedition report, gave the first detailed physical inventory: *"A quadrangular structure, built of clay, after the fashion of the Mexicans, who are generally employed in building them. The walls are about fifteen feet high, surmounted with a wooden palisade, and form a portion of ranges of houses which entirely surround a yard of about one hundred and thirty feet square."* He noted two entrances: *"Over the great entrance is a square tower with loopholes… built of earth."* And his evening arrival produced perhaps the most-quoted line in all Fort Laramie literature: *"Its lofty walls, whitewashed and picketed, with large bastions at the angles, gave it quite an imposing appearance in the uncertain light of evening."*

That "whitewashed" qualifier is the visual key. The base material was sun-dried adobe brick, a warm tan-to-buff earth tone under any direct light — but the American Fur Company regularly whitewashed the exterior, making the fort's west and south faces blaze in the afternoon sun and appear nearly white at distance. Later accounts catch the fort at different stages of the whitewash cycle: some describe gleaming white walls, others find cracked, stained, graying clay — the whitewash fading between applications. Francis Parkman, arriving four years after Frémont in 1846, gave a more particular description, and the fort he saw was clearly showing its age: *"Built of bricks dried in the sun, and externally is of an oblong form, with bastions of clay in the form of ordinary blockhouses at two of the corners."* He noted the entrance in detail: *"There are two entrances, the main entrance having two gates with an arched passage intervening. A little square window, high above the ground, opens from an adjoining chamber, so that when the inner gate is closed and barred anyone inside may communicate with outside parties."* Inside, Parkman found a busy, crowded post: *"Tall Indians in their buffalo robes were striding across the area or reclining at full length on the low roofs of the buildings. Numerous squaws gaily bedizened sat grouped about in front of the rooms they occupied, their mongrel offspring, restless and vociferous, rambled in every direction."* He also notes a peculiar detail about the entrance blockhouse: it was *"adorned with a figure of a horse at full speed, daubed upon the boards with red paint."*

By 1849 — just months before the Army arrived — emigrants found the fur trade era's neglect unmistakable. William Kelly (May 28, 1849): *"a miserable, cracked, dilapidated, adobe, quadrangular enclosure, with a wall about twelve feet high"* with defensive bastions held up partly by timber props. Yet that same day Alonzo Delano, arriving June 12 at the moment of the Army's takeover, saw something different: *"Its neat white washed walls presented a welcome sight to us."* The conflict between Kelly and Delano is real, not a trick of sources — the whitewash was re-applied irregularly, and different faces of the fort caught the sun differently. Any single description of the pre-1849 fort captures one moment in its cycle of upkeep and decay.

The fort stood on a slight bluff, with the Laramie River running just to the north and east, and the North Platte visible further out. Both rivers were fringed with stands of cottonwood — the only tall trees for hundreds of miles in any direction. On the open prairie between the rivers, Sioux lodges were almost always present: Frémont (1842) noted *"a cluster of lodges belonging to Sioux Indians pitched under the walls."* By the 1840s the Oglala Lakota had a deeply established trading relationship at the post. Behind the fort to the west and southwest, the land broke toward the Laramie Mountains' foothills, and in clear weather the emigrants saw Laramie Peak for the first time — its snow-capped summit roughly 85 miles distant. Peter Decker (May 1849): *"I saw distinctly Laramie Peak and could distinguish the snow on its tops and sides, looks like a huge blue mound."* Dan Gelwicks (May 28, 1849): *"It is, at this day, covered with snow, which glitters in the sunshine like a diamond in the dark."* The peak was the visual announcement that the Great Plains were ending.

### Period B: US Army post, 1849–1860

On June 26, 1849, Lt. Daniel P. Woodbury purchased the fort for $4,000. The Army's first act was not to fortify — it was to open the walls. The adobe stockade's purpose as a closed trading post was obsolete. Woodbury designed the new post around an **open parade ground**, and within six months Fort Laramie had a two-story frame officers' quarters block, a soldiers' quarters block, a bakery, and two stables — none of them enclosed by the old walls. The adobe Fort John remained standing at the south end of what would become the parade ground, but it was no longer the center of gravity. It lingered, cracking and sagging, while the new frame buildings rose around it. The 1858 photograph by Samuel C. Mills — the earliest known photograph of Fort Laramie — shows the remains of the old adobe-walled fort at the parade ground's south end, flanked by a scatter of wood and adobe buildings arranged around an open rectangle.

The centerpiece of the new post was **Old Bedlam**, built in 1849 and the oldest known US military building still standing in Wyoming. Woodbury's design was vernacular Greek Revival: a two-story central block with symmetrical wings, a sandstone foundation, frame walls (clapboard-clad) against brick and lime-concrete infill, and a **two-story veranda** running across the front. Officers called it "the block"; everyone else eventually called it Old Bedlam, for the noise of the bachelor officers' evening parties. It was painted white from the beginning — the Greek Revival formula required it. From a distance the building read as a white horizontal mass with a two-story porch line, sharply distinct from the crumbling adobe fort still standing in the background.

The Army post was never enclosed by walls. Woodbury initially planned a 9-foot rubble-wall enclosure covering 550 by 650 feet, but cost overrode ambition and it was never built. The post was entirely **open** — buildings around a parade ground, flagpole at center, beyond which lay the plain. A tall flagpole flew the US flag continuously; for emigrants arriving after weeks of open prairie with no vertical markers except Chimney Rock and Scotts Bluff, the flag visible from two to three miles out was the first announcement that the fort was near. John Wood (June 14, 1849): the fort and its surroundings *"present quite an imposing appearance as you approach it and was surrounded with emigrants."*

By the early 1850s, the emigrant camp dynamic had crystallized: thousands of wagons were parked on the flat ground around the fort, livestock spread across the bottomland grass, and the scene was chaotic with the mixing of soldiers, emigrants, Lakota and Cheyenne traders, mixed-blood families, sutler's employees, and Mexican laborers. The sutler's store (adobe-walled, 1849–50, with a stone addition in 1852) was the true center of commercial life, and period accounts describe the interior as a chaos of: *"Indians mingled with soldiers, teamsters, emigrants, speculators, half breeds and interpreters."*

## Distinctive visual features

Cues that must survive in any generated image. A candidate missing these is wrong — discard it.

**Period A (1843–1848) — Adobe Fort John:**

- **Closed rectangle of whitewashed adobe walls, ~15 ft high** — the defining silhouette. The fort reads as a solid white/buff rectangle, not individual buildings. Bastions (blockhouse towers) at two diagonal corners break the roofline; the largest is over the main gate on the south face. If the image shows an open compound or individual separate buildings, it's wrong for this period.
- **Whitewash tone** — freshly whitewashed faces are near-white `#f2ede0`; weathered un-washed adobe is a warm tan `#c4a97d` to buff `#b89660`, deepening to `#9e7c4a` in shadow. The whitewash was never uniform: some faces bright, some fading to dirty tan.
- **Wooden palisade topping the walls** — a row of sharpened stakes above the adobe line, reading as a dark serrated edge against the sky.
- **Sioux lodges (tipis) clustered outside the walls** — perhaps 10–30 lodges on the open ground, especially to the north and east. Their conical silhouettes are the clearest human-scale indicator in the composition.
- **Cottonwood ribbons along both rivers** — the Laramie River green belt runs immediately north/northeast; the North Platte's cottonwoods are visible further. The cottonwood green `#6b8c45` against the sage-brown prairie `#9b8c6a` is the color context of the arrival.
- **No parade ground visible** — the interior is a walled compound. The exterior shows rolling prairie, sage, river bottoms, no formal open space.
- **Laramie Peak on the western horizon** — snow-capped `#e8e8f0`, blue-grey in atmosphere `#8fa8bf`, rising unmistakably above the foothills. Not always visible (haze, weather) but present in fair-weather compositions.

**Period B (1849–1860) — Open Army Post:**

- **Old Bedlam dominant**: two-story white frame building with two-story veranda, Greek Revival roofline. The whitest, tallest, most prominent structure in the frame. Its horizontal orientation (wide, low relative to height) distinguishes it from the crumbling adobe fort beside/behind it.
- **Open parade ground**: no enclosing wall, buildings arranged in a loose rectangle around open ground. The parade ground reads as a flat gravel/dusty rectangle.
- **US flag on a tall pole at parade ground center** — the flag is the visual apex; the pole is the vertical anchor of the composition, distinguishing this scene from any fur-trade-era image.
- **Adobe Fort John ruin in background** — the old fort's crumbling walls should still be visible at the parade ground's south end through roughly the 1850s, sagging and brown against the newer white frame buildings. If this is included, it provides visual period anchoring.
- **Sutler's store**: adobe/stone, lower profile than Old Bedlam, to one side of the parade ground.
- **Emigrant wagon camp spreading beyond the post** — wagons, tents, livestock visible in the middle/far ground. The camp is larger than the fort proper in peak emigration years.
- **Cottonwood ribbons unchanged** — the river-bottom trees remain the green anchor of both period compositions.

## Atmosphere and lighting

Fort Laramie's terrain is high plains transitioning to the Laramie foothills: elevation roughly 4,230 ft, open in all directions except the broken ground to the southwest. Summer weather follows the high-plains pattern — brilliant mornings, building cumulus by midday, afternoon thunder frequent. The Laramie and North Platte bottomlands trap morning mist in cool weather. For both period states, the canonical lighting is late afternoon from the west-southwest: the whitewashed (Period A) or white-painted (Period B) structures glow gold-white against a blue sky, the cottonwoods along the rivers casting long shadows northeast.

The adobe walls in Period A are at their most dramatic in raking late-afternoon light that carves deep shadow into the bastion corners and gate arch, emphasizing the fort's three-dimensionality. At midday the walls wash out toward chalk-white `#ece8df` and lose dimension. Dawn compositions work well with silhouette — the flat adobe rectangle against a pink-orange eastern glow.

In Period B, Old Bedlam's white clapboard faces reflect the late sun with a warm cream tone `#f5eed8`, and the wide two-story veranda casts a strong deep-shadow band across the ground floor — the most architecturally readable detail in any FLUX composition. The US flag catches the same light and reads as a color focal point (red and blue against cream-blue sky). Afternoon thunderheads behind the post, over the Laramie Mountains, are a common period diary trope and make a dramatic variant.

Sage smoke from emigrant cookfires would hang low over the wagon camp in the still evening air, adding a blue-grey haze to the middle ground in any dusk composition. The river mist in early morning gives the cottonwood belts a soft diffused edge.

## Period reference imagery

### Alfred Jacob Miller — *Fort Laramie* and *Interior of Fort Laramie* (watercolors, commissioned 1858–1860 for William T. Walters; sketches from 1837 expedition)

- **What it shows:** Miller is the **only known artist to have seen Fort William** (the predecessor cottonwood stockade, 1834–1841) in person — his 1837 sketches are the visual foundation of all early Fort Laramie reconstruction. His Walters commission (200 watercolors, 1858–1860) includes the exterior *Fort Laramie* (Walters 37.1940.49) showing the fort set against the Laramie River fork with Laramie Peak in the distance, and an interior scene (37.1940.150). He describes the post as approximately 150 ft square with bastions at diagonal corners. A stream (likely the Laramie fork) is visible in the foreground.
- **Link:** [Walters Art Museum 37.1940.49](https://art.thewalters.org/object/37.1940.49/) | [37.1940.150](https://art.thewalters.org/object/37.1940.150/)
- **What it adds:** The only eyewitness artwork of the pre-emigration fort form; the landscape context with Laramie Peak; the scale relationship between the post and surrounding terrain; tipi presence outside the walls.
- **Blind spot:** Miller visited in 1837 when Fort William (cottonwood stockade) was standing; Fort John (adobe) was built in 1841. His "Fort Laramie" paintings executed 1858–1860 reconstruct a fort he saw 20+ years earlier, blending memory with later information. They are artistic reconstruction, not direct observation of Fort John. The exterior painting's fort may conflate both structures. Additionally, Miller adjusted details in different versions — one removes tipis, another introduces a stream. Do not treat any single Miller painting as documentary.

### J. Goldsborough Bruff — field sketches (July 1849)

- **What it shows:** Bruff, a Washington D.C. engineer leading a California-bound party, made on-site sketches of Fort Laramie as it was being transferred to the Army. His July 11, 1849 sketch depicts the adobe-walled fort from a specific angle showing its construction details during the transition moment.
- **Link:** Bruff's journals and sketches are held at the Huntington Library; published as *Gold Rush: The Journals, Drawings, and Other Papers of J. Goldsborough Bruff* (1949).
- **What it adds:** The only known on-site drawing made during the Army takeover week. Shows the fort in its last days as a fur-trade post before military modification began.
- **Blind spot:** Linework only, no color reference. Reproduction quality varies across published editions.

### Samuel C. Mills — daguerreotype / photograph (1858)

- **What it shows:** The earliest known photograph of Fort Laramie. Shows the remains of the adobe Fort John at the parade ground's south end, with the frame buildings (including Old Bedlam) visible around the open parade ground. The US flag on the pole is likely visible.
- **Link:** Referenced in NPS Fort Laramie administrative history; held in NPS collections. See Fort Laramie NHS archives.
- **What it adds:** Ground-truth photographic evidence of what the post looked like 9 years after the Army takeover — still well within our 1843–1860 window. The most authoritative image for Period B composition.
- **Blind spot:** An 1858 daguerreotype or early photograph will show the post in its mid-Army phase, not the raw 1849 state. Old Bedlam was already 9 years old; the adobe fort may be further deteriorated than in 1849.

### Alexander Gardner — photographs (1868)

- **What it shows:** Gardner photographed Fort Laramie during the 1868 treaty negotiations: officers standing on Old Bedlam's two-story porch, soldiers on parade, the sutler's quarters, the river ferry, and wide landscape views showing the fort's relationship to the surrounding terrain.
- **Link:** [WyoHistory.org — A Treaty in his Lens](https://www.wyohistory.org/encyclopedia/treaty-his-lens-alexander-gardner-photographs-fort-laramie-1868)
- **What it adds:** Best photographic evidence of Old Bedlam's two-story veranda, its white exterior, and its relationship to the parade ground. Establishes the visual character of the period B post more clearly than any other source.
- **Blind spot:** 1868 is 8 years past our window's end. The Army had continued building; some structures visible in Gardner's photos were not present in 1849–1860. The adobe Fort John may no longer be visible in its 1849 form. Use Gardner for Old Bedlam's architectural character; do not use for fort-wide composition calibration.

### Library of Congress — Historic American Buildings Survey photos of Old Bedlam

- **What it shows:** HABS photographs of Old Bedlam after NPS restoration, documenting the Greek Revival clapboard exterior, two-story veranda, sandstone foundation, gabled central block and symmetrical wings.
- **Link:** [LOC HABS — Fort Laramie, Old Bedlam (WY-2)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.wy0002.photos/?sp=2)
- **What it adds:** Clearest architectural documentation of Old Bedlam's form — the NPS restoration targets the 1850s appearance, which is within our window.
- **Blind spot:** Modern restoration; NPS made interpretive choices about period state. Not a period photograph. Use for form/proportion, not color weathering or surrounding context.

### NPS Fort Laramie National Historic Site — current site photos

- **Link:** [NPS Fort Laramie](https://www.nps.gov/fola/index.htm)
- **What it adds:** Terrain photography, cottonwood belt along the rivers, parade ground topography, distant Laramie Peak sightlines.
- **Blind spot:** Modern landscaping (trees were deliberately planted post-period; the Gutenberg NPS monograph notes the parade ground evolved from "a gravelly flat" to "a tree-shaded greensward" after a water system was installed). The modern site has shade trees the 1849–1860 post lacked. Adobe Fort John is entirely gone; the NPS has a reconstruction model but no standing walls from the period.

## Composition references from period art

Miller's Fort Laramie exterior paintings consistently use a **wide, low horizon** with the fort as a medium-scale object in the middle ground — not dominating the frame. Sky is roughly 40–50% of the composition, the fort's walls roughly 1/5 of the vertical height. Laramie Peak appears in the upper left or right distance. Figures (Indians on horseback, trappers) are in the foreground for scale. The fort is seldom centered — Miller places it left of center with open prairie or the river fork to the right.

For Period B (Army post), the flag pole's vertical is the natural compositional anchor and should be placed roughly center-frame or slightly left, with Old Bedlam's horizontal mass to the right or left. The parade ground provides depth — an open sandy foreground reading back to the buildings. Emigrant wagons in the far middle ground anchor the human scale. This is a more horizontal, open composition than the closed-wall Period A — the Army post spreads laterally, while Fort John read as a single compact block.

Both periods benefit from a **sky-dominant composition** (50–60% sky) to convey the openness of the high plains setting. Unlike Chimney Rock where the vertical spire demands sky dominance, here the sky dominance comes from the emotional weight of the landscape — the fort is a small human thing in a very large country.

## What NOT to render

- **The NPS visitor infrastructure** (modern parking lot, interpretive signs, paved roads, entry kiosk, modern bridge over the Laramie River) — none of these existed.
- **The modern reconstructed adobe fort wall** — the NPS has a partial reconstruction at the south end of the parade ground. The actual Fort John adobe was completely gone by the late 19th century. A Period B (1849+) image should show the fort's walls as a deteriorating ruin, not a tidy reconstruction.
- **Mature shade trees on the parade ground** — in the 1849–1860 period, the parade ground was a dusty, gravelly flat. Trees were planted later after a water system was installed. Modern photos showing a shaded green parade ground are post-period.
- **The 1868 treaty council scene** — this frequently appears in popular Fort Laramie imagery (thousands of Indians camped across the plain). It is visually spectacular but entirely outside our 1843–1860 window.
- **The 1881 or later officers' quarters duplexes** — period-accurate imagery for our window shows Old Bedlam and simpler earlier buildings, not the elaborate 1880s officers' row.
- **Railroad infrastructure** — no rails within our period.
- **Modern barbed wire fences** — 1874+ technology; absent in our period.
- **The post-1850s hospital building** (the stone hospital dates to 1873) — not present in Period B early phase.
- **Greenery around the fort itself** — the fort sits on a bluff; vegetation was confined to the river bottoms. The fort's immediate surroundings were sage-scrub high plains, not meadow.

## Notes / open questions

- **The whitewash conflict.** Frémont (1842) and Delano (1849) both describe whitewashed walls; Kelly (1849, same week as Delano) describes cracked, dilapidated walls. These accounts are not contradictory — they describe different faces of the fort, different sides of the maintenance cycle, or even different moments in a single week when fresh whitewash was applied. The renderer should understand that Period A prompts can legitimately run from near-white `#f2ede0` (fresh wash, south face) to dirty tan `#b89660` (weathered face, north/east) — both are historically defensible. A single FLUX candidate can mix both tones.

- **Fort John's exact dimensions.** Frémont says ~130 ft square interior yard; Alfred Jacob Miller says ~150 ft square overall; NPS archaeology suggests the exterior walls were roughly 121 × 167 ft (an oblong, not a square). Parkman calls it "of an oblong form." For FLUX prompts use "rectangular adobe fort" rather than "square" to match the oblong archaeology. The 15-ft wall height is consistent across Frémont and Parkman.

- **The 1851 Fort Laramie Treaty.** Popular imagery places this on the parade ground at Fort Laramie, with thousands of Indians in their tribal encampments surrounding the post. This is wrong: the council was held at Horse Creek, 35 miles east, because there wasn't enough grazing for 9,000+ people's horses near the fort. Do not use "treaty council" imagery as the basis for fort-area scene composition.

- **Old Bedlam color.** Multiple secondary sources consistently describe it as white-painted clapboard. The HABS documentation and Alliance for Historic Wyoming description both support white. However, no period diary account I found describes the color explicitly — this is inferred from the Greek Revival tradition and from NPS restoration choices. The "white frame building" is almost certainly correct but worth flagging as inference rather than direct quote.

- **Miller's timeline problem.** Miller painted "Fort Laramie" in oil and watercolor for Walters in 1858–1860, working from 1837 field sketches. Fort William (cottonwood stockade, 1834–1841) is what he actually saw. Fort John (adobe, 1841–1849) was built 4 years after his visit, and by 1858 the Army had already transformed the site into an open post. Miller's paintings are therefore a reconstruction — probably blending his 1837 memory, secondhand accounts of Fort John, and some awareness of the 1849+ changes. They are valuable for landscape context and compositional conventions, but not architecturally precise for any single phase of the fort. The Bruff 1849 sketches (Huntington Library) would be the most direct evidence for Fort John's last appearance, though harder to access in reproduction.

- **Emigrant access.** The American Fur Company's fort was a commercial operation open to emigrant trade but not to overnight camping inside — emigrants camped in the field around the walls. The Army post after 1849 was a military installation; emigrants were served by the sutler's store but did not bed down inside the post. Both periods show emigrants camped outside, on the open ground, in the visual field between the fort and the rivers. This should be consistent across all variants.

- **Verification needed:** The specific claim that Woodbury's plan was for a 550 × 650 ft enclosure (never built) appears in multiple secondary sources tracing to NPS administrative history. Could not locate the primary document. The "gravelly flat to tree-shaded greensward" evolution of the parade ground is from the Gutenberg NPS monograph but without a specific date for when planting began. These details affect Period B late (1855–1860) compositions — the implementer should not add mature trees around the parade ground.
