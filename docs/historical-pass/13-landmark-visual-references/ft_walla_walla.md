**Period gates:** none currently set in code — implementer note below

**Dual-location warning:** There are TWO historically distinct forts called "Fort Walla Walla." The HBC post discussed in sections 1–7 was at Wallula on the Columbia River. ## 1. Why this landmark matters

Fort Walla Walla was the last significant supply stop on the Columbia Plateau before emigrants either launched boats on the Columbia River or committed to the overland route to The Dalles. After 1,870 miles from Independence — the whole long agony of the Platte road, South Pass, the Snake River desert, the Blue Mountains — emigrants saw the HBC Union Jack flying over adobe walls at the river confluence and knew they had crossed the continental interior. The Pacific drainage was below them. Oregon was real.

The emotional weight was immense. James Applegate's 1843 party spent two weeks at the fort building skiffs for the Columbia run. Narcissa and Marcus Whitman arrived here September 1, 1836 and were met at the gate by Pierre Pambrun, the HBC chief trader, who gave them the first real meal they'd had in months. The fort was the last civilized stopping point before the Columbia swallowed emigrants into the gorge.

Historically it mattered as a British presence in what was, until the 1846 Oregon Treaty, jointly occupied territory. HBC chief traders here — first Pierre Pambrun (1832–1841), then Archibald McKinlay (1841–1846), then a series of successors to 1855 — were de facto British administrators of the Columbia Plateau. The fort provisioned emigrants, purchased their exhausted livestock, and provided intelligence about conditions on the Columbia. After 1847 (Whitman Massacre, Cayuse War) and especially after 1848 (Oregon Territory organized as US soil), its British character became diplomatically awkward, and emigrant traffic partially rerouted to avoid it.

## 2. Name history and dating

**Fort Nez Percés (1818–c.1821):** The original name, given by the North West Company founders Donald McKenzie and Alexander Ross. Despite the name, the fort sat in the traditional territory of the Walla Walla and Cayuse peoples, not primarily the Nez Perce. The name reflected trade relationships rather than geography. Some sources continue using "Fort Nez Percés" as the technical name for the HBC post through its entire life; the Whitman Mission NPS literature uses this name for the site throughout.

**Fort Walla Walla (c.1821–1855):** When Hudson's Bay Company absorbed the North West Company in 1821 and took over the post, the name gradually shifted to Fort Walla Walla. For emigrants and most primary sources of the 1843–1855 window, it is uniformly "Fort Walla Walla."

**Old Fort Walla Walla / Wallula (post-1855):** After the HBC abandoned the site and the US Army built a new post 30 miles east, the original Columbia River site was called "Old Fort Walla Walla" to distinguish it. The town of Wallula grew on the adjacent land in 1862. The HBC site is now underwater — Lake Wallula, created by McNary Dam (completed 1954), covers the original fort location in approximately 16 feet of water.

**For the game:** Emigrants of 1843–1855 called this place "Fort Walla Walla" universally. The name "Fort Nez Percés" may appear in government documents of the period but was not the emigrants' usage. The game should use "Fort Walla Walla" without qualification for the active-HBC period.

## 3. Location and setting

The fort stood on the east bank of the Columbia River, half a mile north of the mouth of the Walla Walla River, and a few miles downstream from where the Snake River joins the Columbia. The site is modern Wallula, Washington. The confluence was — and remains — one of the most geologically dramatic spots on the entire Oregon Trail.

The Wallula Gap, immediately downstream, cuts through the Horse Heaven Hills: a two-kilometer-wide slot in columnar basalt where the Columbia squeezes through anticlines formed during the Miocene. Lewis and Clark passed this gap in 1805. Emigrants in the 1840s saw the same thing: massive dark basalt walls rising sharply from the river on both banks, revealing folded and layered strata in the exposed cliff faces. The "Twin Sisters" — two prominent basalt pillars on the east bank near the gap — were a landscape marker that appeared in period accounts. Above the cliffs, the Columbia Plateau stretched flat and treeless in every direction, covered in sagebrush, rabbitbrush, and bunchgrass. No shade, minimal water away from the river, and constant wind from the west funneling through the gap.

The fort itself occupied a low rise above the river, with the Walla Walla River's mouth visible to the south. From the water — and many emigrants arrived by raft or boat after the 1843–1847 period — the adobe walls and bastions would have been the dominant visual element above the flat riverbank, with the HBC flag flying from the flag post inside the stockade. From the land approach (east, from Whitman Mission 35 miles away), the fort appeared as a low white-walled square against the basalt and sagebrush, with the wide blue Columbia behind it.

The terrain around the fort was emphatically treeless. The Columbia Plateau at this elevation (roughly 700 feet) receives under 7 inches of precipitation annually — similar to a desert. Sagebrush extended in every direction except along the riverbanks, where willows and a few cottonwoods grew. The Blue Mountains were visible to the east and southeast on clear days as a blue-grey ridgeline — the same ridge emigrants had just crossed. The setting was therefore a complete terrain shift from everything east of Fort Hall: instead of grass prairie or high-altitude pine, emigrants found basalt, sand, sage, and a massive river.

## 4. Construction history and building materials

### Phase 1: 1818–1841 — The original log palisade fort

Work began July 11, 1818, under Donald McKenzie (North West Company) and Alexander Ross, with 95 employees. The site lacked forest, so workers had to cut timber 100 miles away and float it downriver to the building site. Ross recorded the founding camp:

> "On that day McKenzie, myself, and ninety-five effective men encamped on the site pitched upon for new establishment of Fort Nez Perces, about half a mile from the mouth of the little river Walla Walla."
> — Alexander Ross, 1818

The resulting structure was architecturally ambitious by fur-trade standards. Sources describe a **double palisade** design, unique among North West Company posts:
- **Outer wall:** planks 20 feet high and 6 inches thick, topped with a 4-foot balustrade with loopholes
- **Inner wall:** 12 feet high, with a 5-foot-wide "strong gallery" (elevated walkway for defenders) running between the two walls
- **Corner towers:** four towers at the corners, each housing cannon and water tanks for fire defense
- **Trade window:** a small hole in the inner wall through which trade was conducted, keeping the interior secure
- **Plan:** described as "oblong" in one period account, approximately 100 feet on a side

Alexander Ross called it "the strongest and most complete fort west of the Rocky Mountains, and might be called the Gibraltar of the Columbia." The nickname stuck in the historical literature.

When the Whitman–Spalding missionary party arrived September 1, 1836, William H. Gray (the mission carpenter) recorded the wooden fort in some detail:

> "Old Fort Wallawalla, in 1836, when the mission party arrived, was a tolerably substantial stockade, built of drift-wood taken from the Columbia River, of an oblong form, with two log bastions raised, one on the southwest corner, commanding the river-front and southern space beyond the stockade; the other bastion was on the northeast corner, commanding the north and east side of the fort. In each of these bastions were kept two small cannon, with a good supply of small-arms. These bastions were always well guarded when any danger was suspected from the Indians. The sage brush, willow and grease-wood had been cut and cleared away for a considerable distance around, to prevent any Indian getting near the fort without being discovered. Inside the stockade were the houses, store, and quarters for the men, with a space sufficiently large to corral about one hundred horses."
> — William H. Gray, *History of Oregon*, 1870 (describing conditions in 1836)

Gray's 1836 description gives the **pre-adobe wooden fort**: driftwood palisade, oblong plan, two bastions (not four) on the southwest and northeast corners, cleared ground perimeter. The water tanks in the corner towers (from the 1818 descriptions) may have been reduced to cannon-only by 1836. Interior: houses, store, men's quarters, and a corral area large enough for ~100 horses — the post ran a significant horse-trading operation with Cayuse and Walla Walla horse herders.

**The fire of October 5, 1841:** The wooden fort burned. The Charles Wilkes expedition had visited just days before; the Sinclair settler party arrived the following day to assist with salvage. The cause of the fire is not specified in available sources.

### Phase 2: 1841–1855 — The adobe fort (the iconic state)

After the 1841 fire, HBC rebuilt the fort in **adobe brick** — a complete material change from the log palisade. Buildings were constructed of wooden frames with adobe brick walls. The bricks incorporated local "wild rye" grass as a binding aggregate (standard practice for Pacific Northwest adobe); some bricks may have been sourced from or made with assistance from Marcus Whitman's mission at Waiilatpu, 35 miles east.

Sources on the post-1841 adobe fort are sparser on architectural specifics. Reconstruction was likely complete within one to two years of the fire, as the fort continued to operate as an active trading post through the 1843 "Great Migration" that put 800+ emigrants on the trail. The basic plan appears to have retained a stockaded enclosure with bastions — the "square stockade with corner bastions" form standard for HBC forts of the era. This is the fort that emigrants of 1843–1855 saw.

Key architectural features of the adobe phase, synthesized from available sources:
- **Adobe stockade walls** enclosing a roughly square courtyard — the 100-foot dimension from the original is the most likely retained plan, though the palisade construction method changed to brick
- **Four corner bastions** projecting from the walls, each capable of mounting cannon; the 1818 design specified four towers, and the rebuilt fort presumably followed the same defensive logic
- **Central gate** on the riverside (west) face, facing the Columbia
- **Interior buildings:** factor's (chief trader's) house/quarters, trade room/store, men's quarters, warehouse for furs and trade goods — standard HBC interior arrangement
- **HBC flag:** the HBC Red Ensign (the British Red Ensign with "HBC" lettered in the lower fly) flew from a flag post inside the compound. This flag was virtually identical in appearance to the British naval Red Ensign from a distance — red field with the Union Jack canton in the upper left. On the Columbia Plateau in the 1840s, it was an unambiguous statement of British presence in jointly-claimed Oregon Territory.
- **Cleared perimeter:** the sage had been cut back from the walls as in the log-fort period, creating a cleared zone for defensive visibility and to reduce fire risk

The 1841 rebuild was occupied continuously until 1855. Fort Nez Percés / Fort Walla Walla in the 1843–1855 period was the adobe square that most Oregon Trail emigrants encountered.

## 5. The abandonment and its aftermath (1855–1856)

The **Yakima War** (also called the Yakama War) began October 5, 1855, when Yakama warriors killed Indian agent Andrew Bolon. Within weeks, tensions swept the Columbia Plateau and placed the HBC fort in an impossible position: it was now on officially American soil (Oregon Territory since 1848), its British character was diplomatically untenable, and it was surrounded by Native nations engaged in armed conflict with the US.

HBC officers Archibald McKinlay, Anderson, and James Sinclair (the fort's acting manager in 1855) conferred with US agent Olney and made the decision to abandon. They destroyed the ammunition stored at the fort — a large quantity of powder and shot thrown into the Columbia — to prevent it from falling into Native hands. Olney provided an official receipt relieving Sinclair of liability. The property left behind was valued at $37,000. The white inhabitants were ordered out of the country.

The fort was then **looted and burned** — sources differ on the exact sequence: the HBC may have destroyed it themselves before departing, or Yakama and allied warriors may have burned it after the evacuation. The 1855 burning destroyed what remained of the adobe post.

**Key source distinction:** the October 5, 1855 date is also the anniversary of the 1841 fire — a coincidence noted in some accounts. The abandonment sequence is documented by the accounts of the HBC officers involved; the exact cause of the 1855 fire is unresolved.

**The 1856 ruin:** In the game's 1856 window, the site would have been a ruin of burned and crumbled adobe walls, collapsed roof timbers, and the debris of a looted trading post on the Columbia bank. The flag post was gone. The cleared perimeter had begun to fill back in with sagebrush. This is a brief visual state — the Army post at a different location opened later in 1856, ending any reason for emigrants to stop at the ruined site.

## 6. Traveler descriptions — primary accounts

**Narcissa Whitman, arriving September 1, 1836:**
The Whitman party arrived at the fort after months on the trail. Narcissa wrote that she and Marcus "excitedly galloped up to the gate" and were met by Pierre Pambrun, the HBC chief trader. She described sleeping in the fort's western bastion — "full of portholes and rifles with fire-arms" — and marveling at the breakfast Pambrun laid out: fresh salmon, potatoes, tea, bread and butter, followed by muskmelons he grew two miles from the fort (one measuring 18 inches). Narcissa's journal — composed before arriving — had imagined: "When we get to Walla Walla we shall feel as though we were in England." The reality was not England, but after five months of pounded buffalo meat, it was close enough.

Her post-arrival letters do not contain extended architectural description of the fort, but her reference to sleeping in the "bastion full of portholes" confirms the defensive character of the structure in 1836, and her relief at the hospitality is the emotional register Oregon Trail emigrants would repeat throughout the 1840s.

**William H. Gray, 1836 (as quoted in section 4):** The most architecturally specific surviving account of the pre-adobe fort. Gray was a carpenter and naturally noticed building construction. His description of "two log bastions" on the southwest and northeast corners, each with two small cannon and small-arms, is the closest thing to a floor plan available for the 1818–1841 structure.

**Jesse Applegate, 1843:**
Applegate's party was among the first large emigrant migration to use the fort at scale. After arriving, the men spent approximately two weeks at the fort sawing lumber and building skiffs for the Columbia River run — wagons and most livestock had to be left behind. Applegate recalled the Columbia departure:

> "I well remember our start down the river, and how I enjoyed riding in the boat, the movement of which was like a grapevine swing."
> — Jesse Applegate, *A Day with the Cow Column in 1843*

The 1843 parties knew the Columbia route was dangerous — and it was. A canoe capsized near the John Day River mouth the first day, and the rapids proved worse than expected. But for the first emigrants to reach the fort in significant numbers, it was the pivot point between wagon travel and river travel. The fort was where you sold your oxen, built your boat, and committed to the final leg.

**Paul Kane, July 1847:**
The Irish-Canadian artist Paul Kane traveled the Columbia Plateau in summer 1847, staying at Fort Walla Walla and making the detour to visit the Whitman Mission — four months before the massacre that would destroy it. Kane was one of the few people to systematically sketch HBC forts west of the Rockies; his work on Fort Vancouver is the best visual record of that post. His visit to Fort Walla Walla is documented in his journals and the later *Wanderings of an Artist Among the Indians of North America* (1859), though his sketches and paintings of the post are held at the Royal Ontario Museum and not widely reproduced in accessible sources. Kane recorded that at the fort, "a Nez Perce man offered to guide Kane to waterfalls on Palouse River" — he found the trip through "parched, rocky Snake River country" remarkable, arriving at Palouse Falls and noting he was "sorrey to leve such a piceresk cuntrye." His presence at the fort in July 1847 confirms it was still an active, functioning HBC post four months before the watershed Whitman Massacre.

## 7. Three period states for the game (1843–1860)

The game window straddles three visually distinct states of this landmark. **The code currently has no period gates set.** The implementer note at the end of this section recommends specific action.

### State A: 1843–1855 — Active HBC adobe fort at Wallula (DEFAULT)

This is what the overwhelming majority of Oregon Trail emigrants saw. The post was active, staffed by HBC traders, flying the Red Ensign, and operating as a resupply and boat-building depot.

**Visual character:**
- Adobe stockade, roughly square, approximately 100 feet per side, walls 10–15 feet high (adobe construction is typically shorter than log palisade; the 20-foot log walls of the 1818 original are unlikely to have been replicated in adobe)
- Four projecting bastions at the corners, each capable of mounting a small cannon; the bastions would project slightly beyond the wall face
- A heavy wooden gate facing the Columbia (west face), probably with a small observation platform above
- HBC Red Ensign on a flagpole — red field, Union Jack canton in upper left, "HBC" lettered in the lower fly — flying from inside the compound or from a post above the gate
- Interior buildings: factor's house (the most substantial, often a two-story structure in HBC posts of this size), trade room/store (ground floor), men's quarters (long bunkhouse type), stable/corral area, warehouse for furs
- The interior was functional and spare — HBC posts were commercial operations, not decorated residences
- Cleared perimeter: 30–50 feet of bare ground around the exterior walls, no sagebrush or willow within this zone
- The Columbia River immediately to the west — wide, blue-brown, powerful, the current visibly running toward the ocean
- The Walla Walla River mouth to the south, smaller, shallow, sandy
- Sage desert extending in all directions east and north; the Horse Heaven Hills basalt visible across the Columbia to the west
- The Blue Mountains as a blue-grey ridgeline to the east — the landmark emigrants had crossed weeks before
- No significant trees within the fort's immediate vicinity; a few willows and cottonwoods along the riverbank south of the fort

**Dominant palette:**
- Adobe walls: warm buff to pale tan, `#c8b48a` to `#d4c096`; the sun-dried clay of the Columbia Plateau reads warm and sandy, not the cool grey of earthen forts further east
- Basalt across the river: very dark grey-black, `#3a3a3a` to `#504838`; columnar, dramatic
- Columbia River: deep blue-grey in shadow, lighter blue under open sky, `#6888a0` to `#8cacb8`; very wide, the full width visible in the game's ground-level view
- Sage desert: blue-grey-green, `#7a8a6a` to `#90a070`; sparse, flat, extending to all horizons except the river
- Blue Mountains (background): hazy blue-grey, `#7080a0` to `#90a0b8`; the same "blue" that gave them their name, visible at 30–40 miles
- HBC Red Ensign: bright red field, `#cc2222`, with the Union Jack pattern in the corner canton
- Sky: large, high-desert sky; very little humidity means saturated blue, `#5088b8`, with possible thin cirrus

### State B: 1856 — Burned ruin

A brief state; use only if the game tracks a 1856 visit. The site is a ruin of crumbled and fire-blackened adobe walls, collapsed timbers, debris scattered by looters. The flag post is gone. The gate hangs open or has been removed. Sagebrush has begun to reclaim the cleared perimeter.

**Visual character:**
- Low broken adobe walls, 3–6 feet high in places, collapsed or fire-scorched in others
- Dark charring on the wall tops and interior surfaces
- Open courtyard, no interior buildings standing
- No flag
- The Columbia River and basalt setting unchanged — the landscape is indifferent to the fort's destruction
- Sagebrush pressing against the collapsed exterior walls

### State C: 1857–1860 — US Army Fort Walla Walla (30 miles east, different location)

**This fort is at a completely different location.** The US Army post established in 1856 and built out 1857–1858 was located at modern Walla Walla city, approximately 30 miles east of the original HBC site, at the confluence of Mill Creek with the Walla Walla River. The elevation is about 904 feet — higher than the Columbia River site, in the valley rather than at the river confluence.

The Army post went through several temporary sites before the permanent post was built at 13th Avenue and Rose Street (modern Walla Walla). The original buildings of the permanent post were made of **adobe brick, later boarded over and painted white** — a somewhat ironic repetition of the HBC construction method, though with an American military aesthetic. The compound included:
- Officers' quarters (five duplex sets plus the commanding officer's house)
- Two large barracks buildings for enlisted men
- Parade ground (center of the compound)
- Stables, blacksmith shop, granary, storehouse, sheds, sawmill
- **No stockade or palisade** — unlike the HBC fort, the Army post was an open cantonment with no enclosing wall (consistent with US Army post design of the period; see Fort Kearny)
- **US flag, not the HBC Red Ensign** — Stars and Stripes on a central flagpole on the parade ground

The 1859 photograph of the Army post (May 1, 1859, probably by Frederick Tracy Dent) shows this configuration: white-painted or whitewashed adobe buildings arranged around a parade ground with no surrounding wall.

**Visual character:**
- White (whitewashed or painted) two-story buildings arranged around open parade ground
- No enclosing wall or stockade
- US flag on central flagpole
- Walla Walla Valley backdrop — the valley floor is greener than the Columbia River site (more precipitation at 904 feet elevation), with the Blue Mountains more immediately to the east
- Mill Creek visible in the background
- Agricultural fields beginning to appear nearby (the town of Steptoeville, later Walla Walla, growing adjacent)

## 8. Arrival sequence (State A, 1843–1855)

For emigrants coming from Whitman Mission (35 miles east) or from the Umatilla River route (post-1848):

1. **Approach from the east across the sage plateau:** The trail descended gently toward the Columbia, with the Blue Mountains visible behind the traveler. The landscape was flat, treeless, and windswept — sage and bunchgrass to every horizon. The Columbia was not visible until very close; the cut through the basalt concealed it.

2. **First sight of the Columbia:** The river appeared suddenly as the trail dropped to the bank — enormous, blue, and swift. For travelers who had spent weeks on the Snake River, this was a different scale. The Columbia carried the full discharge of the inland Pacific watershed.

3. **First sight of the fort:** The adobe walls appeared across the low bank, set slightly above the river level on a low rise. The fort read as a compact square — warm buff adobe against the dark basalt across the river. The HBC flag was visible from some distance.

4. **Approach and gate:** The trail approached from the east along the south bank of the Walla Walla River, then turned north to the fort's main (west-facing) gate. The cleared perimeter around the walls made the approach feel exposed — there was no cover between the sage and the gate.

5. **At the gate:** The chief trader or a clerk greeted arriving emigrants. The gate was not always open to all traffic — the HBC controlled access to the interior and conducted trade through the formal trade room. The practice of conducting trade through a small window or wicket (documented in the 1818 construction) may have softened to a more open trade-room arrangement by the 1840s.

6. **The Columbia River decision:** This was the landmark where emigrants committed to the final leg. Before 1846 (and the opening of the Barlow Road), the only way to Oregon City was down the Columbia — rafts, canoes, or hired bateaux. The fort could supply planks, tools, and advice. Emigrants sometimes spent a week or two here building watercraft.

## 9. What NOT to render

- **No modern photographs of the HBC site.** The original Fort Nez Percés / Fort Walla Walla location is underwater — Lake Wallula covers it in approximately 16 feet of water since McNary Dam was raised in 1953. Any photograph labeled "Old Fort Walla Walla" or "Wallula historical site" in a modern context shows the nearby land above the old waterline, not the fort. There is no accessible ruin.

- **No Fort Walla Walla Museum reconstructions for the HBC period.** The Fort Walla Walla Museum in modern Walla Walla city depicts the US Army post era (1856+) at the Army's location 30 miles from the HBC site. Its reconstructed pioneer village and military buildings are historically interesting but represent a completely different fort at a different place and time. Do not use these images to inform the HBC fort's visual appearance.

- **No log-cabin palisade for the 1843–1855 period.** The log fort burned in 1841. By the time of the Great Migration (1843), emigrants saw adobe walls, not log planks. The William Gray description of "drift-wood palisade" applies to 1836 only.

- **No dramatic tree cover.** The Columbia Plateau at Wallula was essentially treeless except along riverbanks. The fort had no shade trees, no windbreaks, no ornamental plantings. A few willows along the Walla Walla River mouth would be visible to the south, but the fort itself sat in open sagebrush desert.

- **No stockade for the Army post (State C).** The permanent US Army Fort Walla Walla was an open cantonment without palisade walls — consistent with Army post design throughout the period. The white-painted adobe buildings around an open parade ground is the correct image, not a stockaded fort.

- **No Columbia River Gorge cliffs.** The Wallula site was at the upstream end of the Wallula Gap, not in the dramatic gorge further downstream. The basalt cliffs across the river were present but lower and more open than the famous Columbia River Gorge walls. The Twin Sisters basalt pillars were visible downstream. The landscape was wide and sky-dominated, not canyon-enclosed.

- **No vegetation that implies high precipitation.** The Columbia Plateau near Wallula receives under 7 inches per year. Sage, sand, and basalt are the correct ground cover. Do not render grass prairie (that was the Snake River), pine forests (that was the Blue Mountains), or lush riverbanks (that was Fort Vancouver, 250 miles downstream).

- **Do not confuse the two forts' flags.** The HBC fort flew the Red Ensign (red field, Union Jack canton). The Army post flew the Stars and Stripes. This is historically and visually significant — in the 1840s, that Red Ensign meant British territory in a contested zone. By the 1850s, after the 1846 Oregon Treaty settled the boundary at 49°N, the flag was anomalous — a British commercial flag over an American fort.

## 10. Visual character summary

### Active HBC adobe fort (1843–1855)

**Form:** A compact adobe square against a vast open landscape. The fort is not large — approximately 100 feet per side — but it is the only vertical structure for miles except for the basalt cliffs across the river. It reads as a sand-colored cube with projecting corner bastions, a flag flying from within.

**What makes this landmark distinctive from the eastern forts:**
- The Columbia River immediately behind it — a massive blue-brown presence filling the background, unlike the Platte (pale braided mud) or the Snake (often below canyon walls)
- Basalt geology — the dark fractured basalt across the river and visible in the Gap is totally different from the sandstone of Chimney Rock or the limestone of Fort Laramie's surroundings
- Sage desert — not grass prairie. The blue-grey-green sage covers everything; there is no sod, no buffalo grass, no tall-grass
- The HBC flag — unique on the entire trail; this is the one stop where a British commercial flag flies
- The Blue Mountains behind the traveler to the east — a visual reminder of the pass just completed
- No trees. Not a single significant tree except the willows along the river mouth to the south.

**The palette:**
- Fort walls: buff-tan adobe, `#c8b48a` to `#d4c096`, warmer and brighter than the grey sod of the Platte forts
- Basalt cliffs across the river: near-black dark grey, `#303030` to `#4a4038`
- Columbia River: deep, moving blue-grey, `#6888a0`; visibly wide and swift
- Sage plateau: `#7a8a6a` to `#8c9870`; flat to the horizon in all directions except the river
- HBC flag: `#cc2222` red field with `#1c2a6c` Union Jack canton
- High-desert sky: saturated blue, `#4a7eb0`, with possible dust haze near the ground
- Blue Mountains background: atmospheric blue-grey, `#7888a8`, fading to the horizon

## 11. Implementer note: dual-location and period-gate recommendation

**The core problem:** The game's `ft_walla_walla` landmark with `postKind: 'hbc'` and no period gates is currently accurate for the 1843–1855 window but becomes misleading for 1856 (ruined site) and 1857–1860 (a completely different fort at a different location).

**Recommendation:** Add `abandonedAfterYear: 1855` (or equivalent) to the HBC fort landmark. Then decide one of the following:

**Option 1 (simplest):** The landmark is permanently the HBC adobe fort. It disappears from the active landmark list after 1855. Emigrants in 1856–1860 encounter no "Fort Walla Walla" landmark. Historically this is partially correct — post-1847, emigrant traffic already partially rerouted to avoid the fort after the Whitman Massacre raised hostility in the area.

**Option 2 (accurate but complex):** Add a second landmark `ft_walla_walla_army` for 1857–1860 at the Walla Walla Valley location, with `kind: 'trading_post'` or `kind: 'military_fort'`, `postKind: 'us_army'`, and `activeAfterYear: 1857`. This landmark would be 30 miles east of the Columbia River site and would render the open-cantonment Army post (State C above) rather than the adobe stockade.

**Option 3 (current state, acceptable for v1):** Keep the current setup (no period gates, HBC fort rendered throughout). Note in code that this is a v1 simplification and the fort should technically close after 1855. Most players will never reach this landmark after 1855 since it's near the end of the trail.

**Recommendation is Option 1 for v1, with a code comment flagging Option 2 for later.**

## 13. Source notes and confidence flags

**High confidence (corroborated by multiple independent sources):**
- Fort built 1818 by North West Company under Donald McKenzie and Alexander Ross at Wallula, WA
- Original design: double palisade, 20-foot outer wall, 4 corner towers, ~100 feet square; Alexander Ross description and FortWiki corroborate
- Described as "Gibraltar of the Columbia" — Ross's own characterization, widely corroborated
- Fire October 5, 1841; adobe reconstruction followed immediately
- Adobe construction: wooden frame + adobe brick walls, local wild rye aggregate; Marcus Whitman mission as possible brick source
- Chief traders in sequence: Pambrun 1832–1841, McKinlay 1841–1846, later managers to 1855
- Yakima War 1855: fort abandoned, ammunition destroyed in river, property valued $37,000 left behind; documented by HBC officers' accounts
- Looted and burned 1855; dual-source confirmation (burned "at the beginning of the Yakima War")
- Site now underwater, Lake Wallula, McNary Dam completed 1953–1954
- HBC flag was the Red Ensign with HBC lettering, distinct from the plain Union Jack
- US Army Fort Walla Walla established 1856 at different location (modern Walla Walla city), permanent post built 1857–1858
- Army post original buildings adobe, later boarded and painted white; open cantonment without stockade
- 1859 photograph of Army post exists (Frederick Tracy Dent, May 1, 1859)

**Medium confidence (single-source or partially corroborated):**
- Post-1841 adobe fort plan retained ~100-foot square footprint from the original — plausible given HBC tendency to rebuild to similar plans, but no post-1841 architectural survey has been located in available sources
- Four corner bastions on the adobe version — the 1818 fort had four towers; the 1836 Gray description mentions only two bastions ("southwest and northeast corners"). The adobe rebuild may have simplified to two bastions or retained four. Available sources do not specify. The 1818 four-tower plan is used as the default assumption here.
- The specific height of the adobe walls — 10–15 feet is estimated based on typical HBC post dimensions; the 20-foot log outer wall of the 1818 version is unlikely to have been replicated in adobe
- Whether the 1836 Gray description applied to the same architectural plan throughout 1836–1841 (some rebuilding may have occurred between the 1818 original and Gray's arrival)
- Paul Kane's specific visual documentation of the adobe fort in 1847 — Kane was present and sketched; his Fort Walla Walla work is at the Royal Ontario Museum but not widely reproduced in accessible sources; the character of his sketches cannot be confirmed from available sources

**Could not verify / flagged for Dave:**
- The exact completion date of the adobe rebuild after the October 1841 fire. It was clearly complete by the 1843 Great Migration (800+ emigrants passed through), but whether it took months or over a year is not established in available sources.
- The specific interior layout of the adobe fort — factor's house, trade room, warehouse positions — is inferred from standard HBC fort layouts (consistent across Fort Hall, Fort Boise, etc.) rather than from a specific Fort Walla Walla plan. No interior plan document was located.
- Whether Narcissa Whitman's post-arrival letters contain detailed architectural description. The July 1836 letters describe her approach before arrival; the post-arrival letters (August–September 1836) were not accessible in full during this research pass. The "slept in the bastion full of portholes" detail comes from a secondary compilation.
- Paul Kane's oil paintings and sketches of Fort Walla Walla — these are cited as existing at the Royal Ontario Museum but could not be accessed or described. If Kane painted the adobe fort in July 1847, this would be the single best visual primary source for the post-1841 structure. Dave or future researchers should pursue the ROM collection.

## 14. Primary sources and references

- **Alexander Ross** — *Adventures of the First Settlers on the Oregon or Columbia River* (1849); *The Fur Hunters of the Far West* (1855). Ross was present at the founding of the fort in 1818 and served as its first factor. His descriptions of the construction and layout are the primary source for the 1818 fort dimensions.
- **William H. Gray** — *History of Oregon* (1870). Gray's 1836 description of the wooden fort is the most architecturally specific surviving account of the pre-adobe structure. Gray was trained as a carpenter and naturally noticed construction details.
- **Narcissa Whitman** — letters and journals 1836, collected in multiple compilations. Arrived at the fort September 1, 1836; her account captures the emotional experience of reaching the fort after the overland journey.
- **Jesse Applegate** — *A Day with the Cow Column in 1843*. Documents the 1843 Great Migration's use of the fort as a boat-building depot for the Columbia River run.
- **Paul Kane** — *Wanderings of an Artist Among the Indians of North America* (London, 1859). Kane visited July 1847 (four months before the Whitman Massacre); his sketches of the fort are at the Royal Ontario Museum.
