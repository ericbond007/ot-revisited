## What it actually looked like (1843–1860)

### Active mission (1836–November 29, 1847)

Waiilatpu — "the place of the rye grass" in Cayuse — sat on the north bank of the Walla Walla River, 22 miles upstream from its junction with the Columbia. The setting is a broad, open plateau valley: not mountain-enclosed but gently rimmed by rolling loess hills covered in bunchgrass and sage, with the timber-covered Blue Mountains visible as a dark ridge to the southeast, some 25 miles distant. The Walla Walla River runs in a cottonwood-and-willow corridor below the mission terrace — the only significant tree cover in the surrounding landscape. Everything else is open. The valley floor is flat to gently rolling, the color of dried rye grass in summer and autumn — pale straw `#d4c68c` — interrupted by the dark green ribbon of the river bottoms. The site itself was slightly elevated above the river to avoid the periodic floods that wrecked the mission's first (1836) building.

By the mid-1840s the compound was substantial enough that approaching emigrants from the east — descending out of the Blue Mountains — would have seen it from some distance as a cluster of low white buildings against the pale grassland. The mission house was the dominant structure: a T-shaped, story-and-a-half building with a wooden frame, adobe brick walls, and a roof of poles, straw, and earth. Narcissa Whitman and her husband had the walls smoothed and whitewashed inside and out with a solution made from river mussel shells, and later acquired paint from the Hudson's Bay Company at Fort Walla Walla to paint the doors and window frames green, the interior woodwork gray, and the pine floors yellow. At a distance the exterior read as bright white — conspicuous against the tawny grassland. It was a domestic-looking building, not a fort: windows (small, with glass obtained at considerable expense), a chimney, a covered porch, pitched rather than flat appearance at the roofline despite the earthen-pole construction. The main wing rose to a story and a half with three ground-floor rooms and sleeping space above; a long single-story wing extended from it, containing a kitchen, bedroom, and classroom.

Other structures were arranged around the mission yard without a formal stockade. Gray's residence (1840–41), a neat rectangular adobe building, sat roughly 400 feet east of the mission house. Between them stood the blacksmith shop — another adobe rectangle, 16 by 30 feet, built in 1841 from bricks salvaged from the dismantled first house. The gristmill complex stood to the south of the mission grounds, fed by an irrigation ditch dug from the Walla Walla River to a millpond formed by two long earthen dikes. By 1844, Whitman had completed a third and final mill with grinding stones 40 inches in diameter and a threshing machine and turning lathe on the mill platform. The millpond — a deliberate man-made feature in otherwise dry ground — would have been visible and distinctive from a distance. The emigrant house, a separate structure for transient travelers, stood at the north end of the compound. By late 1847, 70-plus people lived at the station, divided roughly as: 23 in the mission house, 8 in the blacksmith shop area, 29 in the emigrant house, and 12 at the remote sawmill cabin 20 miles up in the foothills.

The orchards were planted beginning in 1837 — both apple and peach trees, from sprouts obtained at Fort Vancouver. By 1843–1847 the trees were bearing age and occupied a distinct cultivated zone near the first house site and millpond. Wheat fields and vegetable gardens extended beyond the buildings. Narcissa's letters note producing enough wheat flour to supply other mission stations and sell to emigrants. The contrast between the irrigated green of the mission fields and the tawny dry hills around it was the dominant visual impression: in an otherwise brown landscape, the mission read as a small island of green and white.

Cayuse lodges — hide tipis — were typically pitched within sight of the mission but not inside the compound; the Cayuse maintained their own camps, sometimes dozens of people and horses. By 1847 the Cayuse presence was large and visible. Horses were everywhere: the Cayuse were equestrian people, and accounts mention herds in the hundreds. The landscape west and north of the mission compound would have been scattered with horse herds grazing the bunchgrass hills.

Period diary, **Narcissa Whitman, letter to her mother, 1838** (describing the mission house under construction): *"It is built of adobes, mud dried in the form of bricks, only larger — there are doors and windows of the roughest material, the boards being sawed by hand and put together by one who knows nothing about the work."* (This describes the transitional period, before the final whitewashing and painting. By 1843 the house was substantially more finished and domestic in appearance.)

Period diary, **Overton Johnson and William H. Winter, 1843** (*Route Across the Rocky Mountains*): Writing of the Walla Walla Valley and the mission's agricultural zone, they noted that *"Corn has been grown for some years in small quantities at Dr. Whitman's Mission, and some of the Walawala Indians have been induced to cultivate small patches of it"* — and described the valley as suitable for dryland grain with soil adequate for wheat, rye, and oats near streams, sparse timber (mainly cottonwood and willow along streams), and excellent bunchgrass range that carried animals year-round without supplemental feed.

Period account, **Paul Kane, July 1847** (artist, *Wanderings of an Artist*): Kane visited Waiilatpu in mid-July 1847, four months before the massacre, and sketched the mission compound. He noted the already-strained relations between the Cayuse and the mission residents. His field visit produced both a site sketch (referenced in Thomas Vaughan's 1971 edition of *Paul Kane, The Columbia Wanderer*) and portraits of Cayuse leaders including Tomahas, who would be named as Whitman's killer. Kane's presence establishes that the compound was intact and in active use as late as July 1847.

### Ruin state (December 1847 onward)

The massacre itself (November 29, 1847) did not immediately destroy the buildings — the initial attack killed people, not structures, and the Cayuse held survivors as captives in the mission house and emigrant house for about a month before Peter Skene Ogden of the Hudson's Bay Company ransomed them in late December. After Ogden escorted the 61 survivors to Fort Walla Walla, and after Cayuse sources learned that Willamette Valley settlers had begun burning Cayuse villages in retaliation, the Cayuse returned to Waiilatpu and burned the mission buildings deliberately — loading wagons and other property inside the structures first, then setting them alight.

What remained: adobe brick walls do not burn cleanly. The wooden framing, roofing, doors, and window frames would have burned to ash; the adobe walls would have crumbled and partially collapsed under loss of their structural timber, scorched and rain-soaked. By spring 1848, when Oregon Volunteers arrived to bury the massacre victims and conduct the Cayuse War campaign, they found charred and fallen adobe — low, crumbling earthen mounds with ash and debris inside, the outline of rooms still visible as broken mud-brick courses. The millpond dike would have remained (earthwork, not combustible), the irrigation ditch would still have cut across the ground, and the orchard trees — not set alight — would have continued standing, no longer tended. By 1849 the orchard would have looked wild: unpruned, possibly fruiting but untended, saplings competing with grass. By 1851–1855 the adobe ruins would have been softening further into the ground, the burned beams long since scattered or rotted, the walls reduced to low mounds of discolored earth. The millpond earthwork would persist. The Great Grave — where volunteers buried the massacre victims in March 1848 under an overturned wagon bed — would have been visible as a mounded depression north of the ruined mission house site.

Period context, **Oregon Volunteers, March 1848**: After arriving at the site, volunteers placed the disinterred remains (disturbed twice by wolves from their first shallow grave) in a new grave and covered it with an overturned wagon bed. This confirms that the site was accessible but the structures were already sufficiently destroyed that no building provided adequate shelter for a burial. The wagon bed used as a grave marker implies loose wreckage — wagon equipment from the mission's stores — was lying around the yard.

Key post-massacre visual facts:
- The main routes west after 1847 shifted north of Waiilatpu; the site became a detour from the main trail rather than a stop on it. After 1848 most emigrants would have bypassed the site entirely unless they specifically sought it out.
- The orchard survived physically — fruit trees are difficult to kill — and would have been one of the most visually persistent features of the ruin through the 1850s: a cluster of apple and peach trees growing wild, no longer pruned, no longer irrigated, but still alive.
- Adobe ruin color: weathered Walla Walla River adobe is a warm red-tan, close to `#b87a5a` when raw and darkening to `#8a5038` when rain-wetted and charred. Ash scatter and blackened timber debris would add black and dark grey tones among the earthen rubble.

## Distinctive visual features

### Active mission (game states ≤ 1847)

The cues a viewer must see to recognize the place. A generated image without these is wrong.

- **Whitewashed adobe T-house as anchor** — The mission house reads as a low, white, domestic building against tawny grass. The whitewash is bright: creamy white `#f4efe6`, conspicuous in the dry brown valley. Losing this contrast (making the building earth-tone) kills the recognition.
- **Story-and-a-half main wing + long low wing** — The T-shape is important: the main block is visibly taller than the lateral classroom/kitchen wing. Not a symmetrical house. Roofline is low-pitched, slightly ramped, not a steep New England pitch.
- **Millpond/irrigation ditch visible** — The deliberate man-made water feature south of the main buildings is distinctive in dry terrain. Pond and earthen dike visible; the mill structure stands near it.
- **Green contrast against dry hills** — The irrigated fields and orchard read green (`#7a9c58`) against the surrounding pale straw hills. This color contrast is the most visually distinctive feature of the setting.
- **Cottonwood/willow corridor along the Walla Walla River** — A dark green band at the bottom of the scene, lower than the mission terrace. Cottonwood canopy `#4a7040`.
- **Blue Mountains on horizon** — The dark conifer-covered ridge visible in the southeast, providing the background skyline. Not close — 20–25 miles. Dark blue-grey `#3a4a55`.
- **Cayuse horses visible** — Herds of horses grazing the surrounding hills. Period-accurate and compositionally grounding — Cayuse horsemanship was a defining feature of the landscape.
- **No stockade wall** — Waiilatpu was an open mission compound, not a fort. No log palisade, no watchtower, no defensive perimeter. Generating a fort is wrong.

### Ruin state (game states > 1847)

- **Low crumbling adobe mounds, not standing walls** — The ruin reads as low earthen rubble, not as tall burned-out standing walls. Adobe collapses inward after burning; the remains are a foot or two of discolored brick-and-earth debris outlining the former rooms. Color: scorched red-tan `#8a5038` with ash-grey `#6a6258` scatter.
- **The orchard still standing, unpruned** — The apple/peach trees are the tallest intact feature. Wild-grown, branchy, unmanaged. Bare or leafy depending on season. Ghostly domesticity: trees that don't belong in a ruined landscape but are real.
- **Millpond earthwork visible but water possibly gone** — The earthen dike outline remains, the pond itself may have silted or drained. A dark depression rather than a reflective surface.
- **Grave mound north of ruin** — The Great Grave as a low mounded feature, roughly covered. Not a monument (the 1897 obelisk belongs to no period in our window).
- **Overall palette shifts to muted ruin tones** — Ruin scene is dustier, grayer, warmer brown than the active scene. The green irrigated fields are gone, replaced by returning dry grass `#c4b878`.
- **Desolate open sky** — No human presence. No smoke from chimneys. The valley is unchanged; the buildings are the only difference.

## Atmosphere and lighting

The Walla Walla Valley has a semi-arid plateau climate: hot dry summers, cold winters with some snow, very little summer rain. Afternoon light in late summer and early autumn — prime emigrant arrival season — is intense and clear, with blue sky and long shadows late in the day. The valley floor is exposed and open; wind is a presence, moving the bunchgrass and ruffling the cottonwood leaves.

The active mission in afternoon light would have the whitewashed walls glowing warm white on the southwest face and falling into cooler shadow on the northeast, with the green fields catching the slanted afternoon sun. The millpond would catch a sky reflection. The Blue Mountains to the southeast would be a blue-grey backdrop in afternoon haze.

The ruin in any light reads melancholic: low broken shapes in flat terrain, no vertical accent, no smoke, the wild orchard trees the only thing with height. Dawn light would be the most affecting — the adobe rubble lit in warm peach `#e8b090`, the orchard silhouetted, the valley absolutely quiet. Storm light — the valley floor can see heavy weather rolling in off the Columbia Plateau — would drive the ruin palette toward grey-brown and dramatize the emptiness.

## Period reference imagery

### Nancy Ann Osborn Jacobs — pencil sketch (circa 1847, preserved through 1929)

- **What it shows:** A direct eyewitness site sketch inscribed "a correct representation of the Whitman Mission buildings at the time of Massacre, Nov. 29, 1847." Jacobs was a survivor of the massacre. This is the only period image by someone who was on site.
- **Link:** Oregon Historical Society Research Library, Portland. Collection Mss 5326 (OrHi 637 and OrHi 637 8x10 photographic copies). [Archives West entry](https://archiveswest.orbiscascade.org/ark:80444/xv80236)
- **What it adds:** The only primary visual record of the building layout from someone who was physically present. Shows relative positions and sizes of the main structures before they were burned.
- **Blind spot:** Pencil on paper; small scale. No color information. Architectural detail may be imprecise — Jacobs was a child survivor, not a trained architectural draftsman. The copy is from memory years later (pencil on paper, c.1847–1929 notation span suggests it may have been drawn from memory long after the fact, not at the time).

### Paul Kane — sketches, July 1847

- **What it shows:** Kane visited Waiilatpu in mid-July 1847 and sketched the mission compound. Published in Thomas Vaughan's *Paul Kane, The Columbia Wanderer: Sketches, Paintings, and Comment, 1846–1847* (Oregon Historical Society Press, 1971). Also portrayed Cayuse individuals at the site.
- **Link:** Royal Ontario Museum (primary Kane collection); Oregon Historical Society; [Paul Kane Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Kane)
- **What it adds:** The last known professional artist's eyewitness view of the mission while active, four months before the massacre. Establishes what the compound looked like in its final active summer.
- **Blind spot:** Recent Kane scholarship notes that the *Wanderings of an Artist* text as published was likely not written by Kane himself — it was ghostwritten or edited. The sketches themselves are primary, but Kane's verbal descriptions may be unreliable for period-accuracy detail. The sketch itself is a field sketch, not a finished painting.

### NPS Whitman Mission National Historic Site — modern interpretive maps and site photos

- **What it shows:** The current site with concrete inlaid footprints of the original buildings, the restored millpond and irrigation ditch, the restored apple orchard, the Great Grave site.
- **Link:** [NPS Whitman Mission](https://www.nps.gov/whmi/index.htm); [NPS Trees and Shrubs](https://www.nps.gov/whmi/learn/nature/treesandshrubs.htm)
- **What it adds:** Archaeological ground truth on building positions and relative footprints. The millpond and orchard positions are verified by excavation. The building outlines (concrete inlaid blocks) trace actual foundations. Modern site photos provide useful terrain, vegetation palette, and the relationship of the mission terrace to the Walla Walla River cottonwood corridor.
- **Blind spot:** No original buildings survive. The site is open, grassy, with managed vegetation — the period landscape was denser in cultivated vegetation (wheat, gardens, orchard at bearing age) around the structures. Modern photos show the ruin-period landscape (empty, open) but with none of the ruin material visible. The 1897 monument obelisk on the hill north of the site is prominently visible in modern photos — exclude entirely from both period renders.

### Erwin N. Thompson — *Whitman Mission National Historic Site* (NPS administrative study, 1964, Project Gutenberg)

- **What it shows:** The most detailed written architectural and physical description of the mission complex in the historical literature, compiled from primary sources including Narcissa Whitman's letters, missionary records, and HBC correspondence.
- **Link:** [Project Gutenberg ebook](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60756/60756-h/60756-h.htm)
- **What it adds:** Room dimensions, material descriptions (the mussel-shell whitewash, the HBC paint colors, the 40-inch grinding stones), the sequence of building construction, the agricultural extent. This is the source-of-sources for all physical description of the active mission compound.
- **Blind spot:** Thompson writes from secondary synthesis; he is not a period eyewitness. His descriptions are accurate for the primary source record but do not capture the experiential arrival view that an emigrant diary would give.

### Whitman College / Oregon Historical Society — *At Waiilatpu: Impressions and Recollections of Visitors to the Whitman Mission, 1836–1847* (1957)

- **What it shows:** A compiled anthology of visitor descriptions of the active mission, organized chronologically.
- **Link:** [Whitman College Archives](https://arminda.whitman.edu/islandora/waiilatpu-impressions-and-recollections-visitors-whitman-mission-1836-1847-1957)
- **What it adds:** Multiple eyewitness voices describing arrival at and impression of the active compound in the emigrant era. This is the densest single source for period visitor testimony.
- **Blind spot:** Researcher was unable to access full text online; content described in secondary sources only. The compilation exists and is held at Whitman College — implementer should attempt physical or inter-library loan access if detailed visitor quote selection is needed for interpretive signage.

### Overton Johnson and William H. Winter — *Route Across the Rocky Mountains* (published 1846, emigration of 1843)

- **What it shows:** An 1843 emigrant account with a chapter on "Whitman's Mission, on the Walawala." Establishes the valley's landscape (bunchgrass and cottonwood, dry plateau climate, suitable for grain near streams).
- **Link:** [Wikisource transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Oregon_Historical_Quarterly/Volume_7/Route_across_the_Rocky_Mountains_with_a_Description_of_Oregon_and_California/Chapter_3)
- **What it adds:** Ground-level landscape description from the emigrant perspective. Confirms the valley's open, dry character with cottonwood/willow limited to stream margins.
- **Blind spot:** The Waiilatpu-specific section is brief and focused on the valley's agricultural potential rather than the mission's visual appearance.

## Composition references from period art

The Jacobs sketch and Kane sketches, combined with the NPS site archaeology, suggest the following compositional reading:

- **Mission house is the visual anchor, placed center-to-left in frame** — the T-shaped main building with its whitewashed walls is the dominant structure; other buildings are lower and read as supporting elements.
- **Valley is wide and open; sky is large** — the site has no enclosing landforms within the immediate frame. The Blue Mountains are distant backdrop. A 60/40 or 50/50 sky-to-land split is appropriate.
- **The approach from the east (Blue Mountains side)** is the canonical emigrant view — coming down from the mountains into the valley, the mission appears first as white specks on the plain, then grows as buildings with Cayuse horse herds visible on the surrounding hills.
- **Foreground is dry bunchgrass** — the trail crosses open plateau before descending to the river-bench terrace where the mission sat. No trees close in the foreground.
- **The millpond catches light** — a horizontal reflective element south of the buildings; compositionally useful as a mid-ground anchor.
- **For the ruin state, the composition is the same but inverted in mood** — same open valley, same distant Blue Mountains, same cottonwood corridor. But the white buildings are replaced by low brown mounds, the millpond may be a depression, and the wild orchard trees are the only upright features near the mission site.

## What NOT to render

The following appear in modern site photos, romanticized paintings, or popular imagination — none belong in either the active or ruin period renders:

- **The 1897 memorial obelisk** — The tall monument on the hill north of the mission was built for the 50th anniversary. It is prominently visible at the modern site and in virtually every modern photo. Exclude from both states. It did not exist in any year of our game window (1843–1860).
- **The modern NPS visitor center, paved paths, interpretive signs, parking lot** — none existed.
- **A stockade or fort wall** — Waiilatpu was an open compound; no defensive perimeter was built. Generating a fort is incorrect.
- **The marble grave slab (1897)** — Same as the obelisk: post-period. The actual grave in 1848–1855 was a rough mound with an overturned wagon bed.
- **Reconstructed buildings or standing walls** — No part of the mission was reconstructed in period. The ruin is low rubble, not dramatic Gothic-ruin tall walls.
- **Fences, barbed wire, modern signage** — all post-period.
- **A railroad** — The Northern Pacific did not reach this area until the 1880s.
- **Modern Walla Walla urban development visible on horizon** — The city of Walla Walla did not exist in our window; Fort Walla Walla (HBC) was 22 miles downstream. The horizon should be empty valley and Blue Mountains.
- **Soldiers in Union Army uniform or post-Civil War military presence** — The Cayuse War (1848–1850) involved Oregon Territorial Volunteers in civilian/frontier dress, not regular Army. Any military figure in a post-massacre scene should not be in blue Army uniform with U.S. insignia.

## Notes / open questions

**Biggest open question — ruin state visual specificity:** The post-1847 ruin condition of Waiilatpu is poorly documented in accessible online primary sources. The key facts are confirmed (buildings burned by Cayuse after Ogden's ransom, all buildings destroyed, volunteers buried victims in March 1848), but no researcher-accessible period diary from an emigrant or soldier who *described* the burned site in detail was found in this pass. The 1848–1855 visual is inferred from: (1) what adobe construction burns to (scorched low rubble, not dramatic standing walls), (2) the persistence of the orchard (fruit trees survive fire at their roots), (3) the persistence of the millpond earthwork (non-combustible). If the implementer can access *At Waiilatpu* (1957, Whitman College) or the NPS administrative history full text (Crabtree, 1988), there may be specific accounts from visitors to the ruin that would sharpen the visual.

**Nancy Ann Osborn Jacobs sketch — uncertainty about date:** The Archives West entry dates this sketch "circa 1847–1929" which is a span, not a date. The inscription says it depicts the mission "at the time of Massacre, Nov. 29, 1847." Jacobs was born 1840, making her about 7 at the massacre. The sketch may have been drawn from memory decades later rather than drawn on-site in 1847. Treat it as a useful layout reference but not a precise architectural document.

**Paul Kane sketch — ghostwritten text issue:** Recent scholarship holds that Kane's *Wanderings of an Artist* text was not written by Kane personally and may not accurately reflect his field observations. His sketches are period primary sources; his verbal descriptions as published are of uncertain reliability. Cite the sketches, not the *Wanderings* text, as authoritative for visual detail.

**Active-state rendering challenge — "not a fort" is easy to miss:** FLUX and SDXL models have been trained on many frontier-fort images and will default to stockade walls unless explicitly suppressed. The prompt must include a negative or explicit open-compound framing: the mission was a cluster of low buildings in open terrain, not an enclosed fort.

**Ruin-state rendering challenge — the ruin is LOW:** Most "ruin" training data shows dramatic tall crumbling walls (European castles, Greek temples, etc.). Adobe burns and collapses inward; the Waiilatpu ruin after a season or two of rain would be low mounds — perhaps knee- to chest-height rubble — not dramatic standing walls. FLUX prompts must explicitly specify "low crumbling mounds" not "ruined walls standing." The wild orchard trees, not the building shells, are the tallest features. This is the unusual visual grammar that makes the ruin state distinctive.

**Route change after 1847:** Most emigrant traffic post-1848 bypassed Waiilatpu and crossed the Umatilla River at a different point. The ruin state is therefore a detour-stop, not a main-route stop — which affects who would have seen it in 1848–1860. For game purposes this is irrelevant (the player visits if they played a 1843–1847 game and might encounter the ruin on subsequent playthroughs), but it contextualizes why post-massacre diary descriptions of the ruin are rarer than descriptions of the active mission.
