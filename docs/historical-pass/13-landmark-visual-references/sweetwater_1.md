## Disambiguation: nine crossings, one landmark

The Oregon Trail crossed the Sweetwater River **eight or nine times** between its first encounter with the river (near what the code places at mile 830) and the final crossing at Burnt Ranch, just short of South Pass. WyoHistory.org documents crossings at Three Crossings (three fords within 1.5 miles), a Sixth Crossing, a Seventh, Eighth, and Ninth (Last) Crossing. Some modern surveys count eight; others nine, depending on how closely the Three Crossings cluster is counted.

The code models only `sweetwater_1` at mile 830 — the first encounter with the river, just before Independence Rock at mile 838. This is historically correct as the single game landmark representing the long Sweetwater Valley stretch. The other 7–8 historical crossings are not individually modeled. This is a reasonable compression; the first crossing was the one that mattered most emotionally (the relief of sweet water) and was the visual archetype for all the gentle Sweetwater fords that followed.

**For rendering purposes:** treat `sweetwater_1` as the first crossing — the initial ford just before the approach to Independence Rock. The river here is at its gentlest, still in the low valley east of the granite formations, with open sage country on all sides.

## What the crossing actually looked like

The first Sweetwater crossing was, by emigrant standards, a relief. After the North Platte — wide, turbid, sometimes treacherous — and after the brutal 30-mile dry haul from Bessemer Bend through the Emigrant Gap (Avenue of Rocks), the alkali flats, and the saleratus lakes, the emigrants descended into the Sweetwater Valley and found a river they could actually drink.

The Sweetwater at the first crossing was **narrow** — perhaps 30 to 50 feet across at summer ford, though William Clayton (1847) measured one Sweetwater crossing location at "seven or eight rods wide" (roughly 115–130 feet) and three feet deep — that measurement likely reflects a location closer to Independence Rock where the channel had narrowed between granite. At the first, easternmost crossing, the river would have been shallower and gentler still. Later crossings (Three Crossings, Sixth Crossing) involved deeper water and, in some years, wagon-box raising. The first crossing did not.

The **water itself** was the defining feature. After several weeks drinking from the silty, alkaline North Platte — and after the truly toxic saleratus lakes just upstream — the Sweetwater ran clear and cold. Clayton described it as "a little muddy but pleasant tasting" with tiny bright particles floating on the surface that initially looked like salt but had "not the least saline taste." Van Dorn (1849) called it "clear as crystal." The contrast with what came before was visceral.

The **surrounding landscape** at the first crossing was classic Wyoming high sage desert: open, flat-to-rolling, dominated by sagebrush (artemisia) and bunchgrass on both sides, with granite domes rising to the southwest (the Sweetwater Rocks, the formation that includes Independence Rock and Devil's Gate). The valley floor at the first crossing was relatively wide — perhaps a mile across — with the river cutting a shallow channel across it. Cottonwood trees were rare to absent this far east in the Sweetwater Valley; willows grew spottily along the bank but provided little shade. The sage was the visual texture of everything.

As emigrants climbed Prospect Hill, a 400-foot rise roughly 15 miles before the first Sweetwater crossing, the scale of what lay ahead was suddenly visible. Elizur Shaw (July 20, 1862) wrote: *"As we reached the summit of Prospect Hill, the whole Sweetwater country broke upon our view, with mountains north and south, evergreen timber and snowbanks on their sides, [and] sage plains between."* William Clayton (June 20, 1847) described "a vast extent of country to the south, west, and north" with distant ranges whose "summits are spotted with snow." By the time the wagon train reached the first crossing, those peaks were no longer a distant promise — the Wind River Range was visibly growing on the western and northern horizon.

## The name

The river's name was given by Anglo trappers working the region in the early 1820s. The most commonly cited attribution is **General William Ashley's trapping party, ca. 1823**, who named it because the water tasted sweet compared to the bitter alkaline sources they had been drinking in the surrounding high desert. Jedediah Smith and Thomas Fitzpatrick's crew was in the region in 1823–24; Ashley organized the first Rocky Mountain Rendezvous in 1825 along the Green River. By 1837, the Sweetwater was established enough in the trappers' geographic vocabulary that Alfred Jacob Miller's party, traveling with Sir William Drummond Stewart to the Green River Rendezvous, followed "the courses of the Kansas, Platte, North Platte and Sweetwater rivers" as a recognized route.

An alternative origin story — that a mule team spilled a load of sugar into the river, making it temporarily sweet — is widely repeated but is almost certainly folk etymology. It is a good story and may have circulated in emigrant camp conversation; it can be used as in-game flavor if handled lightly.

The functional truth underlying the name is real: the Sweetwater had **lower mineral content** than the alkaline water of the Platte, the Green Desert playas, or the saleratus lakes. After weeks on the Platte and then the toxic alkali stretch approaching from Bessemer Bend, the Sweetwater tasted genuinely sweet by contrast. The name was not metaphor — it was accurate reporting.

## The approach: 30 miles of misery first

The emotional weight of the first Sweetwater crossing cannot be understood without the 30-mile approach that preceded it. From the final North Platte crossing at Bessemer Bend (Red Buttes), the trail left the river entirely and struck southwest through what is now called the Emigrant Gap — a stretch of dry, alkaline terrain with no drinkable water, "stinking, toxic alkali waterholes," and dead cattle marking the hard sections. Edwin Bryant (July 6, 1846) described the terrain immediately after leaving the Platte as "an arid plain" with "wild sage, grease-wood," and "immense piles of rocks."

The intermediate water stops — Willow Springs (mile ~825) — provided some relief but Willow Springs was famously degraded by 1849 from animal contamination. The saleratus lakes just before the Sweetwater were visually spectacular but undrinkable: encrusted with white alkali that looked like snow, smelling, according to Pusey Graves (July 1850), "like stagnant soap suds." Animals forced to drink from the lakes could die.

And then the Sweetwater.

Pusey Graves, arriving at the Sweetwater River on July 7, 1850, wrote: *"Having reached the stream fatigued with trudging through heavy sand beds and very thirsty, I knelt at the edge of this Sweetwater River and for the first time slaked my thirst with pure water since I crossed the Big Blue River."* The Big Blue was in eastern Kansas — nearly 700 miles back. That span of bad or alkaline water gives the moment its weight.

The standard emigrant behavior at the first Sweetwater crossing included **stopping to drink immediately**, often directly from the river, and refilling water casks and canteens. Animals were watered extensively. Many parties rested a day here. The river had good grass on the banks for stock, and the cold water was actively pleasant in summer heat.

## Primary source voices

**Pusey Graves, July 7, 1850** — on arriving at the Sweetwater after the saleratus lakes:
> *"Having reached the stream fatigued with trudging through heavy sand beds and very thirsty, I knelt at the edge of this Sweetwater River and for the first time slaked my thirst with pure water since I crossed the Big Blue River."*

(Source: WyoHistory.org, Saleratus Lake article. Graves was a California-bound emigrant, 1850 gold rush wave.)

**William Clayton, June 1847** — on first reaching and fording the Sweetwater:
> *"The current is very swift, the water a little muddy but pleasant tasting, and by watching closely one could see on the surface numerous small bright particles floating which might at first sight be supposed to be salt, however the water itself had not the least saline taste."*

(Source: *The Latter-day Saints' Emigrants' Guide*, 1848. Clayton accompanied Brigham Young's pioneer vanguard company in 1847 and compiled his field notes into the standard Mormon Trail guide. His roadometer measurements were widely trusted.)

**Thomas J. Van Dorn, July 1, 1849** — on the Sweetwater near its headwaters, but capturing the river's character throughout:
> *"The Sweetwater is clear as crystal here and deep and very rapid. The clear, cool atmosphere we experienced in this mountain range is bracing and pleasant."*

(Source: WyoHistory.org, Ninth Crossing article. Van Dorn was a California-bound forty-niner. His description at the upper crossing applies to the whole river's character; the lower crossings were gentler and shallower but equally clear.)

**Elizur Shaw, July 20, 1862** — from Prospect Hill, first view of the Sweetwater country:
> *"As we reached the summit of Prospect Hill, the whole Sweetwater country broke upon our view, with mountains north and south, evergreen timber and snowbanks on their sides, [and] sage plains between. We could scarcely repress a shout at our first view of the mountains."*

(Source: WyoHistory.org, Prospect Hill article. Shaw traveled post-peak but the landscape was unchanged; this quote captures the first-view moment that immediately preceded the first crossing.)

**Rebecca Ketchum, June 1853** — departing the North Platte at Red Buttes:
> *"I believe we have left the Platte River for good… I have grown heartily sick of it."*

(Source: WyoHistory.org, Red Buttes article. The relief at leaving the Platte — even before reaching the Sweetwater — captures the emotional trajectory that culminated in the first crossing.)

## The ford mechanics

The first Sweetwater crossing was a **casual ford** for most parties in most years. The river's gradient was gentle, the bottom was gravelly or sandy, and the summer flow was low. Clayton records that his company's wagons "got over without difficulty or much loss of time" at a ford downstream of Independence Rock; the first crossing, even further downstream, was gentler still.

Depth at summer low water: approximately **1.5 to 2.5 feet** in the main channel at the first crossing. No documented cases of wagons lost specifically at the first crossing; the more dangerous Sweetwater crossings were Three Crossings (a narrow canyon where the current was swift and the rock walls offered no margin for error) and the Sixth Crossing during high snowmelt years.

Current: slow to moderate — the code's 1 mph is a reasonable approximation.

Width: the river was narrow enough that emigrants sometimes described it as a "creek" before seeing it. The sage and bunchgrass banks came down to the water at a gentle angle; no steep clay cuts as at the Platte or the Big Blue. Stock could walk in from either side without excavation.

There was typically **one solitary cottonwood tree** (or none) at or near the ford — Clayton notes "only one solitary tree standing beside the fording place" at his crossing point. This is characteristic of the Sweetwater Valley generally: cottonwoods were sparse, a stark contrast to the Platte bottomlands. Emigrants used sagebrush for fuel throughout the Sweetwater country.

## The ferry question

The in-code $2 ferry option is historically marginal at the first Sweetwater crossing. **No commercial ferry operated at the first crossing** during the main emigration years. Clayton (1847) notes that a ford formerly existed at one location but that emigrants had found a better ford nearby — indicating informal route adjustment, not a ferry operation.

Ferries appeared rarely on the Sweetwater and only at specific crossings and years when snowmelt kept the river unusually high. The Ninth (Last) Crossing at Burnt Ranch occasionally had a ferry in exceptional water years; Three Crossings had improvised ferry arrangements during the high-water season; but the first crossing, in the lower, gentler valley, was almost always fordable.

The $2 ferry in the code can be retained as a rare event (high-water spring crossing) but should not be presented as a standard amenity. The historical default was: drive the oxen in, cross in five minutes, refill your water casks, move on.

## What NOT to render

- **Rushing water or a dramatic ford.** This is not the Green River. No churning white water, no wagons tipping, no swimming oxen. The Sweetwater first crossing was easy.
- **Ferries.** No rope ferry, no flatboat. If a ferry is shown, it should read as exceptional (high water year) not standard.
- **Modern Independence Rock area development.** The rock itself (mile 838) is a separate landmark; this crossing is just before it. Do not show the rock's parking area, interpretive signs, or the paved road.
- **Dense cottonwood canopy along the banks.** Cottonwoods were nearly absent at the first crossing. Willows are possible in spots but should not dominate. The banks are open sage and grass.
- **The dramatic canyon character** of the later crossings (Three Crossings, Devil's Gate area). Those involve granite walls pressing to the river's edge. The first crossing is an open valley.
- **Mountain peaks too close or too large.** The Wind River Range was visible on the western horizon, but at the first crossing (east end of the valley) it was still distant — a snowy edge on the skyline, not a looming wall.
- **A large emigrant camp with dozens of wagons.** Parties did not pile up waiting here the way they did at the flooded Big Blue. A company of 5–10 wagons at the crossing is right; a mass encampment is wrong.

### Prompt notes

- The **kneeling emigrants at the bank** are the emotional center: they are drinking, finally
- **Oxen drinking in the stream** signals that the animals also recognize the good water; this was diary-attested
- **No cottonwoods** is a required visual constraint — their absence distinguishes the Sweetwater valley from the Platte and Big Blue bottomlands
- The **distant granite dome** (Independence Rock) anchors western direction without foregrounding a separate landmark
- **Wind River peaks on the horizon** are distant and snowy — a faint but real presence, not a looming backdrop
- **Sage dominates both banks** — this is the defining texture of the Sweetwater Valley, not green riverbank vegetation
- The ford is **calm, knee-to-hub-deep** — no splash drama; the water is clear enough to see the bottom
- **Late afternoon light** is historically apt (most parties reached the first crossing in the afternoon after the morning dry haul from Willow Spring)

## Relationship to adjacent landmarks

- **Willow Spring (mile ~825, 5–8 miles east):** Last water stop before the first Sweetwater crossing. A separate game landmark. By 1849 badly fouled by animal contamination. The contrast with the Sweetwater was extreme. Do not conflate visually — Willow Spring is a muddy, degraded stopover; the Sweetwater first crossing is a relief.

- **Independence Rock (mile ~838, 5–8 miles west):** The first Sweetwater crossing historically occurred just before the trail reached Independence Rock. The rock is a separate game landmark. The backdrop for `sweetwater_1` should show the granite dome of Independence Rock at distance on the western horizon as a visual waypoint — but not foregrounded. The water-cask-filling moment happens here; the name-carving and 4th-of-July celebrations happen there.

- **Devil's Gate (mile ~843):** Even farther west. Not visible from the first crossing. Do not include the dramatic canyon cleft in this backdrop.

## Code note: nine crossings not modeled

The historical trail crossed the Sweetwater River **eight or nine times** over approximately 120 miles between the first crossing and South Pass. The game models only `sweetwater_1`. This is an acceptable game compression; attempting to model all nine crossings would make the WY segment mechanical and repetitive.

The one flag worth noting: the code's `$2 ferry option` at `sweetwater_1` is historically loose. A ferry at the first crossing (the easiest one) is unlikely; if a Sweetwater ferry event is desired, it would be more historically grounded placed at the Three Crossings area (the canyon section, miles ~870–880) or the Sixth Crossing. The code's gentle ford stats (2.0 ft, 1 mph) are accurate; the ferry option should be presented as a high-water variant, not a standard amenity.
