## 1. Historical summary

Robidoux Trading Post was one of the most-described and most-complained-about stops on the entire trail corridor. Its brief existence — roughly three years at peak traffic — coincided almost perfectly with the California Gold Rush surge. Every 49er who came through Robidoux Pass either stopped here or recorded the sight of it.

The post was established in 1849 by order of Joseph Robidoux III (the founder of St. Joseph, Missouri), who directed his eldest son **Joseph E. Robidoux** to relocate the family's outfit from near Fort Laramie to the strategic pass over Scotts Bluff. The pass offered ample springs — described by emigrant accounts as among the best along the entire route — and cedar trees for fuel and repairs. The family had been working this corridor for years; Joseph E. was fluent in Lakota and several other native languages and had married into local Sioux kinship networks, which explains the permanent encampment of native families around the post.

The physical post was ruthlessly modest: a single log shanty by the roadside with the blacksmith's forge in one end and a grog-shop-cum-grocery in the other. A few more cedar-log huts clustered around it. Robidoux himself lived in an Indian lodge, not in the cabin. The structure sat beside a cool spring in a deep ravine where the wagon road had to detour from the main pass.

### The two-site problem

Secondary sources blur two distinct locations under the name "Robidoux Trading Post":

**Site 1 — The Robidoux Pass / Carter Canyon original (1849–1851).** This is the canonical game location. It sat at the south flank of the Scotts Bluff formation, where the wagon road swung south through the Wildcat Hills gap known as Robidoux Pass. This is what every 1849 and 1850 diary describes.

**Site 2 — The Carter Canyon relocation (1851).** In 1851, feeling "somewhat overrun by the emigrant hordes," Robidoux moved approximately one mile southeast into a more secluded canyon (present-day Carter Canyon). German traveler Frederick Möllhausen sketched this second post in 1851 while traveling with Prince Paul of Württemberg, and the 1993 reconstruction built near Gering, NE is based on Möllhausen's sketch of this relocated post — not the original. Möllhausen's sketch shows a low log structure with a sod or dirt roof, basically a fortified cabin with a corral extension. The reconstruction gives a reasonable visual baseline even though it represents the second-site layout.

**Why the confusion matters:** Mitchell Pass was blasted open in 1850 and widened through 1851. By the 1852 season, the vast majority of traffic had shifted north to Mitchell Pass. The Robidoux Pass route fell out of use, and both Robidoux's post and the nearby American Fur Company post (sometimes called "Fort John, Scott's Bluffs," located about six miles below Robidoux's in Helvas Canyon) were abandoned by approximately 1852. Emigrant diaries from 1850 returning eastward with Stansbury already note "a small rivulet, row of old deserted houses" — suggesting partial abandonment was in progress even that year during the off-season.

**A third confusion layer:** Some NPS sources and local tourist materials attribute the original 1849 post to Antoine Robidoux (Joseph's uncle, more famous for Utah and California exploits), but the weight of scholarly evidence — including Merrill Mattes's definitive Scotts Bluff National Monument history — points to Joseph E. Robidoux as the day-to-day operator at the pass site, with the broader family enterprise backing him. Antoine Robidoux's connection to the area is real but secondary.

### Route context

Robidoux Pass was the standard wagon route bypassing Scotts Bluff from 1841 through 1850. The bluffs themselves blocked travel along the North Platte riverbank; wagons were forced nine miles south and west through the Wildcat Hills gap, gaining a spring and cedar trees but losing proximity to the river. From the summit of the pass, emigrants got their first view toward the Laramie Mountains — many mistook distant Laramie Peak for their first glimpse of the Rockies.

After Mitchell Pass opened (ca. 1850–51), the route shifted north, skirting the base of the bluff itself. The shorter, river-adjacent route made Robidoux Pass economically irrelevant. The post lost its captive clientele and ceased operations.

## 2. Period diary accounts

### J. Goldsborough Bruff, 1849

Bruff was a meticulous Gold Rush diarist who documented the post in detail:

> "a cool clear spring and brook" in the deep gulch, with "a group of Indian lodges and tents, surrounding a log cabin, where you can buy whisky for $5 per gallon; and look at the beautiful squaws, of the traders."

*Gold Rush: The Journals, Drawings, and Other Papers of J. Goldsborough Bruff* (1849). Bruff's observation is the most-cited emigrant account of the post. His phrase captures the characteristic scene exactly: the spring, the clustered lodges, the single log cabin at the center, and the trade in alcohol at eye-watering markup.

### Captain Howard Stansbury, 1849

Government topographer Stansbury provided the most detailed physical description:

> "There is a temporary blacksmith's shop here, established for the benefit of the emigrants, but especially for that of the owner, who lives in an Indian lodge, and had erected a log shanty by the roadside, in one end of which was the blacksmith's forge, and in the other the grog-shop and sort of grocery."

On business practices, Stansbury observed that the proprietor's "stock of this establishment consisted principally of such articles as the owner had purchased from the emigrants at a great sacrifice and sold to others at as great a profit." Blacksmith services ran "seventy-five cents an hour" for use of the shop and tools.

*Exploration of the Valley of the Great Salt Lake*, Howard Stansbury, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (1852). Stansbury is the most authoritative contemporary source — he was a trained government observer.

### James Bennett, 1850

Bennett documented the post in 1850 and found it distinctly more settled than Bruff's description a year earlier:

> "a row of rudely constructed huts composed of cedar logs and mud," serving as a trading post or store, blacksmith shop, and dwellings, usually surrounded by Indian families of the Robidoux clan.

He noted "an encampment of near a 100 Sioux Indians" near the location.

*Overland Journey to California* (1850 diary).

### Elisha Douglass Perkins, 1849

Perkins encountered the post and offered a drier, more skeptical account:

> "a Frenchman has established himself in a little cabin as a jack at all trades, Blacksmith, Shoemaker, Tailor etc., & keeps a little stock of groceries, hardware, etc."

*Gold Rush Diary of Elisha Douglass Perkins* (1849). Perkins's description — "a little cabin," "little stock" — underscores that this was not an impressive establishment. He is also one of several diarists who identify Robidoux by ethnicity (French-speaking) rather than name.

### William Murray, 1849

Murray met "a French man named 'Rubidu'" on Monday, June 25, 1849, who had been living in the Scotts Bluff and Horse Creek area for some 13 years, indicating Robidoux's deep prior tenure in the region before the formal 1849 post.

## 3. Physical description

### Structure

The post was a single-room log shanty (or in the expanded 1850 version, a short row of cedar-log huts) with mud chinking. The structure was one-story, dirt or sod roofed, roughly divided internally between forge space and trade/grog space. No palisade or defensive stockade is mentioned in any diary account; this was not a fort. The logs were native cedar from the Wildcat Hills, giving the structures a reddish-brown color and a resinous smell.

Key distinctions from larger posts:
- No stockade or perimeter wall (confirmed by multiple accounts)
- No multi-building complex — two or three structures at most
- Forge was inside the main cabin, not in a separate building initially
- Proprietor lived in an Indian lodge beside the trading post, not in the log cabin

### Blacksmith evidence

Archaeological collections from the site include hand-forged iron artifacts: gun barrels, hide-scrapers, hoes, picket pins, a lantern hook, handcuffs, wagon-bow staples, ox shoes, wagon bolts, chain links, nails, a currycomb, and heavy iron grating. This gives an accurate sense of the forge's output: trail-repair work (wagon hardware, ox shoes) mixed with trade goods (traps, tools).

### Trade goods

- Whiskey / grog (primary profit item; $5/gallon per Bruff, 50 cents/pint in other accounts)
- Tobacco
- Gunpowder, lead, ball molds
- Basic foodstuffs (flour, sugar at extortionate markups)
- Secondhand emigrant goods (purchased cheap from desperate emigrants, resold at profit to the next wave)
- Iron hardware and nails
- Beads, pendants, buttons, and trinkets for native trade

The 1848 federal prohibition on trading liquor with Native Americans did not noticeably constrain Robidoux's operation — whiskey sales are confirmed in every major diary account.

### Native presence

Lakota (Sioux) and Cheyenne bands both traded and camped at the post. Bruff found a group of Indian lodges surrounding the cabin. Bennett counted approximately 100 Sioux encamped nearby. Robidoux's kinship ties to local Sioux families — he had married a native woman and had mixed-heritage children — meant the post functioned simultaneously as a commercial stop for emigrants and a node in native trading networks. Father De Smet, returning from the 1851 Horse Creek Treaty Council, baptized Robidoux's mixed-heritage children at the site.

The lodges or tipis visible around the trading post are as historically documented as the log cabin itself; they should appear in any authentic visual rendering.

## 4. Landscape and setting

### Geographic position

Robidoux Pass cuts through the Wildcat Hills nine miles west and south of present-day Gering, Nebraska. The pass is a gap in the escarpment that separates the North Platte valley from Pumpkin Creek to the south. Wagons coming from Chimney Rock (25 miles east) would have followed the south bank of the North Platte, then peeled south to swing around the bluff complex.

From Robidoux Pass, Scotts Bluff is visible to the north and northeast — specifically the main bluff mass rises pale brown and gray against the sky, layered sandstone and volcanic ash formations. The emigrants were in the Wildcat Hills, below the bluff's southern apron, looking up at the formation from the canyon floor.

### Topography at the post

The post sat beside a spring in a deep ravine where the wagon road had to detour. The canyon walls were clad in cedar — the first significant trees since the prairie east of Fort Kearny. A clear spring brook ran beside the post. This was a genuine rest-stop setting: shade, water, wood, and a blacksmith within reach after 300 miles of featureless plain.

### Color palette

Scotts Bluff's exposed rock layers are pale brown, gray-brown, and pinkish-brown siltstone with lighter cappings. The surrounding high plains are sage-green and dry-grass tan. The pass canyon itself was shadier and greener, with the cedar giving a dark resinous green against the lighter bluff faces. The North Platte (about nine miles north) was a flat, braided, pale ribbon across the valley floor but not directly visible from the pass floor.

### Sky and atmosphere

Nebraska summers: high sun, dry air, dramatic cumulus. Heat haze on the plain to the east. The Wildcat Hills create shelter from the prevailing west wind. Smoke from the forge would rise straight or curl gently. Anvil-ring sound carries far in the canyon.

## 5. Visual tone and period atmosphere

The post belongs to the same architectural canon as Jim Bridger's original Green River establishment before 1855: two or three log cabins without a proper stockade, a forge, a grog shop, extortionate prices, and a ring of native lodges that no emigrant sketch or description would omit. Joel Palmer called Fort Bridger "a shabby concern... built of poles and dabbed with mud." Robidoux's place is the same aesthetic — humbler, if anything.

Emotionally, this stop hit emigrants at a specific moment: they had just completed ~300 miles of open prairie from Fort Kearny with no commercial services whatsoever. The presence of a blacksmith — even an expensive one — was a genuine relief. Diaries register both the relief (working forge, fresh supplies) and the outrage (prices). The two tones coexist.

Visual markers specific to this post:
- **Smoke from the forge.** The blacksmith operated continuously during the emigration season. Smoke rising from the log cabin end is the prime atmospheric signal.
- **Clustered lodges or tipis.** Always visible in period accounts. Two to five lodges of Sioux families encircling the cabin.
- **Cedar logs with visible mud chinking.** Not hewn-plank construction; rough-cut cedar with gaps chinked in mud.
- **One horse or mule tied to the cabin side.** The proprietor's animal. Not a commercial livery.
- **Spring brook in foreground.** The cool clear spring Bruff mentioned was the reason wagons stopped here.

## 7. Source layer

### Primary sources consulted

- **J. Goldsborough Bruff, *Gold Rush: The Journals, Drawings, and Other Papers* (1849)** — most-cited emigrant account; Indian lodges surrounding cabin, whisky at $5/gallon, squaws of the traders. Bruff is one of the most careful and extensive Gold Rush diarists; his account is generally reliable.

- **Captain Howard Stansbury, *Exploration of the Valley of the Great Salt Lake* (1849, pub. 1852)** — detailed physical description of the dual-purpose log shanty (forge + grog shop), the living-in-an-Indian-lodge detail, the 75-cents/hour blacksmith rate, and the buy-cheap-sell-dear business model. Stansbury is the highest-authority contemporary source — a trained government observer.

- **James Bennett, *Overland Journey to California* (1850)** — physical description "rudely constructed huts of cedar logs and mud," the ~100 Sioux encampment. Slightly later than Bruff/Stansbury; shows the post in a more settled, multi-hut form.

- **Elisha Douglass Perkins, *Gold Rush Diary* (1849)** — "a little cabin... a little stock of groceries, hardware" — confirms small scale and French/mixed identity of the operator.

- **William Murray diary (1849)** — met "Rubidu" at Scotts Bluff and Horse Creek; confirms 13+ year prior residence, establishing Robidoux's long prior relationship with the region.

- **Frederick Möllhausen sketch (1851)** — the only known period visual of the post (specifically the second/relocated Carter Canyon post). Möllhausen traveled with Prince Paul of Württemberg; his sketch informed the 1993 reconstruction near Gering.

- **Father De Smet reference (1851)** — confirms presence of Robidoux's mixed-heritage children at the post, and Sioux family encampment.

### Secondary sources

- **Merrill J. Mattes, *Scotts Bluff National Monument, Nebraska*** (Gutenberg Project eBook, NPS Historical Handbook Series). The definitive NPS history; basis for most modern accounts. Confirms Carter Canyon as the 1851 relocation site and identifies Joseph E. Robidoux as the operator.

- **Merrill Mattes, "Robidoux's Trading Post at Scott's Bluffs and the California Gold Rush"** — *Nebraska History* (1949). The foundational scholarly article; the Nebraska State Historical Society holds this document.

- **NPS Scotts Bluff National Monument pages** — https://www.nps.gov/places/robidoux-pass.htm and https://npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/28/hh28l.htm

- **Robidoux Pass, Wikipedia** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robidoux_Pass — useful summary, cites Lewis 2006 (*Robidoux Chronicles*).

- **William Henry Jackson visual collection** — Jackson was a bullwhacker on the trail in 1866 and later photographed and painted the Scotts Bluff area extensively. His collection (the largest of its kind) is held at Scotts Bluff National Monument. By 1866 the Robidoux Pass route was abandoned and traffic had shifted permanently to Mitchell Pass; Jackson's work documents the Mitchell Pass corridor and bluff face rather than the Robidoux Pass canyon itself. Use for landscape tone (bluff color, sage prairie, sky) but not for the post location specifically.

## 8. Notes and open questions

### abandonedAfterYear recommendation

**Recommend `abandonedAfterYear: 1852`.** The weight of evidence supports 1852 as the de facto abandonment year:

- Traffic split roughly evenly between Robidoux Pass and Mitchell Pass in 1851.
- From 1852 onward, Mitchell Pass was "heavily favored" (NPS); Robidoux Pass traffic collapsed.
- Stansbury returning eastward in 1850 already noted "old deserted houses" at the site in the off-season, suggesting intermittent or seasonal operation even before full abandonment.
- Both the original Robidoux post and the nearby AFC "Fort John, Scott's Bluffs" post "likely ceased around 1852" (Mattes/NPS).

Setting `abandonedAfterYear: 1851` would be defensible (the pass route was effectively replaced that year) but slightly aggressive — the post did reopen in spring 1851. Setting `abandonedAfterYear: 1853` would be generous and covers any uncertainty about final closure. **1852 is the consensus year.**

### Who actually operated the post: III, IV, or E.?

Generational numbering in the Robidoux family is confused in secondary sources. The common secondary-source statement is that "Joseph Robidoux III of St. Joseph, Missouri" ordered the post established — this is the family patriarch, founder of St. Joseph, born 1783, died 1868. But Robidoux III was in his mid-60s in 1849 and did not personally travel to Scotts Bluff. The day-to-day operator is identified by the NPS Mattes text as "Joseph E. Robidoux, eldest son" — meaning Joseph Robidoux IV by generational numbering, or at minimum a son of the St. Joseph founder. The task brief's reference to "Joseph Robidoux IV" matches this identification. The family's fur-trade lineage ran: Joseph Robidoux II (Montreal) → Joseph Robidoux III (St. Louis/St. Joseph) → Joseph Robidoux IV / Joseph E. (the Scotts Bluff operator). Some sources conflate Antoine Robidoux (uncle; Utah/California trader) with the Scotts Bluff operation; Antoine's connection to the area is real but he is not the primary operator at this post.

### The reconstructed post at Gering

The 1993 reconstruction (built on Carter Canyon Road, Gering, NE) is based on Möllhausen's sketch of the 1851 second post, not the original 1849 Robidoux Pass location. The reconstruction was an L-shaped log cabin with a sod roof, built with 100-year-old hand-hewn logs. It was open to visitors until 2020 (noted as "(1995-2020)" in the Clio database entry). This reconstruction is useful as a visual reference for log-cabin scale and construction — but it represents a slightly more settled version of the post than the original 1849 single-shanty establishment.

### The "Fort John, Scott's Bluffs" second post (AFC)

The American Fur Company's post — sometimes called "Fort John, Scott's Bluffs" — is a separate establishment from Robidoux's, located approximately six miles below Robidoux's in Helvas Canyon. Some secondary sources conflate these two. The AFC post was established after the company sold Fort Laramie to the U.S. Army in 1849 and relocated part of its outfit to the Scotts Bluff corridor. It is not the post described by Bruff, Stansbury, or Bennett; those accounts consistently describe Robidoux's establishment.

### Languages and persona

The Robidoux family was French Canadian in origin. Period diarists consistently identify the operator as "a Frenchman" or use the phonetic "Rubidu." The post was a Francophone establishment in the middle of American emigrant country — a recurring feature of the fur-trade-era post network. Joseph E. Robidoux spoke Lakota, French, and English; his mixed-heritage family surrounded him. This persona detail is historically reliable and adds flavor to any game event triggered at this landmark.

### Price multiplier validation

The game's `priceMultiplier: 1.3` is if anything conservative. Stansbury explicitly describes the proprietor buying emigrant goods "at a great sacrifice and selling at as great a profit." Whiskey at $5/gallon in 1849 translates to roughly 10–15× the Missouri River retail price. Blacksmith work at 75 cents/hour was steep but not ruinous. The post held a near-monopoly on forge services for a 300-mile stretch. A multiplier of 1.4–1.5 would also be historically defensible; 1.3 stays within the "expensive but not predatory" framing the game balance probably prefers.

## 9. Summary for FLUX operator

This is a small, humble, opportunistic trading post — not a fort, not a compound, not an imposing structure. Think: one or two rough cedar-log cabins with sod roofs, mud chinking, and no defensive wall. The forge smokes from one end. Two or three Lakota tipis are visible beside or behind the cabin. A clear spring runs nearby. The setting is a canyon in the Wildcat Hills, nine miles south of the massive pale-brown Scotts Bluff formation, which is visible to the north as a distant layered landmark. Sage brush and dry grass fill the foreground. Summer heat. The whole scene reads as provisional — a business set up to catch trail traffic, not a permanent settlement. When you look at it, you feel the extortion before you even go inside.

Do not render: a stockade. A fort. Stone walls. A large compound. Adobe. A sign. A flag. A military appearance. Wagon wheel decorations. This was a log shanty in a canyon. That is all.
