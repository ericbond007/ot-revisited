**VK child ticket:** [#1116](https://projects.ericbond.net/tasks/1116)
**In-game mile:** ~1170 (landmark row in `landmarks.ts`; code ID `soda_springs`)
**In-game kind:** `spring / water` — described as "Carbonated mineral springs and intermittent geyser; everyone tasted, did laundry, marveled."
**Related landmarks upstream:** Bear River ford (~1095) · Big Hill descent (~1140) · Fort Bridger (~1070)
**Related downstream:** Hudspeth's Cutoff Junction / Sheep Rock (~1175) · Fort Hall (~1230)
**Period gates:** None — springs active from prehistoric times through our full 1843–1860 window. The Steamboat Spring was submerged by Alexander Reservoir in **1924** — well outside our window. ## 1. Why this landmark is different

Soda Springs is the most *talked about* stop on the entire trail between Fort Bridger and Fort Hall. At every other landmark emigrants note what they see and move on. At Soda Springs, they camp, linger, experiment, and write pages. John Bidwell's party in 1841 was the first emigrant wagon train to pass through; Father Pierre-Jean De Smet traveled with them. Within four years, the springs were standard knowledge for any westbound party. By the Gold Rush years the stop was practically required, with parties of strangers sharing cups at the springs and arguing about the best way to sweeten the water.

The reason is simple: the springs do something visually inexplicable if you have never seen naturally carbonated water before. They bubble. Constantly. Not from heat — the water is cool to the touch, often cold. Not from wind. Not from agitation. They bubble from the inside, the same rhythmic carbonated fizz that in 1843 Americans associated only with expensive apothecary soda fountains or imported mineral water. And in the middle of the high desert, three months from Independence, there are hundreds of them.

## 2. What it actually looked like (1843–1860)

### The Bear River valley setting

The approach to Soda Springs from the east (from Big Hill / Bear River Divide) puts the emigrant party in the Bear River valley proper — a broad, open mountain valley at roughly 5,730 feet elevation, the Bear River winding through it on the valley floor. Mountains visible to the south are the Bear River Range; volcanic cone formations (China Hat and China Cap) visible several miles to the north. Past volcanic lava flows cover the plains north of the spring cluster, protruding from the landscape in chunks.

The valley floor is green relative to what the emigrants have seen for weeks: tall native grasses, sedge, willow and narrowleaf cottonwood along the river bank. The sage-covered hills rise on both sides of the valley. This greenness is relevant — the springs themselves are set in a meadow-green context, which makes the white and rust-colored mineral deposits surrounding them all the more startling by contrast.

John Bidwell in 1841 described the scenery around the springs as "romantic" — a high compliment by period standards, meaning picturesque and unusual. The key feature visible from some distance on approach was the vapor or mist above the spring cluster, particularly from Steamboat Spring. Captain Bonneville described the scene in 1833 with Washington Irving's collaboration as "a singular and fantastic scene, suited to a region where everything is strange and peculiar."

### The spring cluster — dozens within a few hundred yards

Within a three-to-four-mile radius Bidwell counted "no less than 100 springs." Most were small — open holes in the ground or shallow depressions, some rising from the bottom of the river itself. They discharged in different ways: "some bursting out on top of the ground, others along the banks of the river, and some even in the bottom of the river." The one thing they all had in common was the constant bubbling of carbonic-acid gas rising through cold or slightly warm water.

The mineral deposits are what make the cluster visually distinctive in a way no other trail landmark matches. Wherever the water emerged from the ground, a sediment built up around the opening — calcium carbonate (travertine) precipitated from the mineral-rich water. Over years this built into cone and mound shapes. Bidwell noted mounds "no less than fifty feet high" (the largest, Pyramid Spring's travertine mound, was the most dramatic). The color of these deposits varied: white at the freshest discharge points, cream to buff at older terraces, rust-brown and ochre where iron compounds mixed with the calcium carbonate. The mounds looked like alien coral formations, or like something from a painter's fever dream — low, porous, hard-crusted, built up over centuries of slow deposition.

The ground between and around the springs was also visually distinctive. It was white and soft in places — "the soil is soft chalk, white and tenacious," one account noted — and hollow-sounding underfoot where gas pockets lay just below the surface. Bidwell recorded that the dry ground "made a noise as we passed over it with horses as though it was hollow underneath." You could hear the ground hiss.

### Beer Spring

The spring fur traders named "Beer Spring" (later identified as a predecessor to Hooper Spring) was a bubbling cold-water pool with a slightly bitter, ferrous, flat taste. Frémont explained the name in his report: the springs "have received their name from the voyageurs and trappers of the country" because of "the effervescing gas and acid taste." Rufus Sage in 1842 described one spring's water as "slightly acid and beer-like." J. Goldsborough Bruff in 1849 put it more charitably, noting the water "was fine, only needed lemon syrup, to render it perfect soda."

Emigrants' practical response to the beer-spring flavor was to sweeten it. Women dissolved sugar in their cups, added vinegar or citric acid from the medicine kit, mixed in dried fruit extract. The result — what Abigail Scott called in 1852 "a drink equal to any prepared soda in the States" — became a trail ritual, one of the few genuine pleasures available in the Bear River valley.

The ground surrounding Beer Spring was stained brown and rust from the iron compounds in the water — a visually distinctive patch of discolored soil around the low mineral mound at the spring's mouth.

### Steamboat Spring

This was the most dramatic of the cluster and the one that inspired the most diary entries. It sat roughly a half-mile from the main Beer Spring group, on the riverbank or close to it. The water discharged through a fissure or pipe-like opening in a rock — "out of a solid rock," Abigail Scott wrote — and the discharge was not continuous but periodic. The timing varied by observer: Overton Johnson in 1843 recorded "at intervals of fifteen seconds"; Elizabeth Dixon Smith in 1847 noted "every few minutes"; Samuel Hancock in 1845 described "gushing out in foam about six times a minute." The variations likely reflect fluctuations in underground CO2 pressure over the seasons and years.

What all observers agreed on was the sound. Frémont described it as making "very much the impression of a steamboat in motion." The sound was a deep, rhythmic chugging — exactly the noise a paddlewheel steam engine made discharging pressure, familiar to every emigrant who had taken a river boat. Hearing that noise in the Idaho mountains was arresting. Benjamin Franklin Owen, camping at Steamboat Spring on July 20, 1853, wrote that he was "neither am I yet able to solve the hidden mysteries that keep in motion the water that alternately raises & falls in those two pipes."

The visual: a column of water and vapor thrown up periodically "to a variable height of about three feet" — Frémont's figure — with some accounts stretching to three or four feet. Not a true geyser (it was cold-water CO2 pressure, not thermal heat). The water landed back in a pool around the rock, white and fizzing, then calmed until the next pulse. The rock surrounding the discharge point was crusted white with calcium carbonate buildup, the mound growing with each year of deposition.

## 3. Emigrant diary record

### John C. Frémont — August 1843

Frémont's Corps of Topographical Engineers expedition was the first to formally document Soda Springs for a wide audience. His report, published in 1845 and widely read by prospective emigrants, fixed the name "Steamboat Spring" in the national consciousness:

> "Crossing this stream, we descended a mile below and made our encampment in a grove of cedar immediately at the Beer Springs, which, on account of the effervescing gas and acid taste, have received their name from the voyageurs and trappers of the country... A white column of scattered water is thrown up, in form like a jet-d'eau, to a variable height of about three feet. It is accompanied by a subterranean noise which makes very much the impression of a steamboat in motion."

This is the most influential primary description — Frémont was the published authority most emigrants had read before departure, and his comparison to a steamboat engine is what gave the spring its name.

### Abigail Scott — July 19, 1852

Scott traveled the Oregon Trail in 1852 as part of a family emigrant party. Her diary is notable for its precise, non-sensational observational style:

> "About 11 o'clock, we came to Soda Springs. They are a great curiosity. The first view we had was two mounds of lime-stone rock. The water boils up about one foot from the top of the wall. It is quite cool. When sweetened with the addition of a little vinegar, it makes a drink equal to any prepared soda in the States. A half mile farther we came to the Steamboat Spring. The water puffs up out of a solid rock. It puffed to the height of one and two feet alternatively. In the afternoon, we came to the Soda Pool. This is a large rock reservoir. It boils up in one small chasm and runs off a little, but most of the water remains standing in the pool."

Scott's account confirms the core visual elements: the travertine limestone mounds around the spring openings, the cool temperature of the water, the sweetening practice, the Steamboat Spring's intermittent puffing, and the existence of the pool reservoir. Her description of water puffing "one and two feet alternatively" captures the variable pulse height.

### Captain Benjamin Bonneville / Washington Irving — 1833

Bonneville visited the springs in the late fall of 1833 during his Rocky Mountain trapping expedition. Washington Irving later compiled and dramatized his account in *The Adventures of Captain Bonneville* (1837), producing the most vivid social scene recorded at the springs — not from the emigrant era but from the fur-trade years that immediately preceded it:

> "It was a singular and fantastic scene, suited to a region where everything is strange and peculiar. These groups of trappers and hunters and Indians, with their wild customs and wilder countenances, their boisterous gayety and reckless air, quaffing and making merry round these sparkling fountains, while beside them lay their weapons ready to be snatched up for instant service."

Irving extended the description to a scene of improvised celebration: the assembled company drinking, pledging toasts, "singing, drinking and uttering peals of laughter, until it seemed as if their imaginations had given potency to the beverage and cheated them into a fit of intoxication." This is the clearest period account of what the springs *felt like* as a social space — a place of brief, strange recreation in a landscape that offered very little.

### Additional accounts (not quoted at length but cited)

**Elizabeth Dixon Smith, August 22, 1847:** Described Steamboat Spring as "a boiling spring it boils and foams and runs over 30 barrels in a day it boils up out of a stone the hole is a bout as large as a large dinner pot evry few minutes the water will bounce up 3 or 4 feet high the water slightly warm."

**Overton Johnson, September 7, 1843:** "A Spring where the water (which is quite warm), at intervals of fifteen seconds, is thrown several feet in the air."

**Samuel Hancock, 1845:** "These soda springs are well worthy a notice, possessing all the properties of pure soda water, the water gushing out in foam about six times a minute."

**John Bidwell, August 10, 1841:** First emigrant to document the springs in detail. Counted approximately 100 springs in a three-to-four-mile radius; described travertine mounds "no less than fifty feet high" around the largest springs; noted the hollow-sounding ground.

**Sarah White Smith, July 24, 1838:** "The water tastes like soda water, especially artificially prepared. The water is bubbling and foaming like boiling water... It is excellent for baking bread, so no water preparation is necessary." The first missionary description — bread leavened by carbonic acid rather than yeast, an immediate practical use that emigrants quickly adopted.

## 4. Distinctive visual features

The cues a generated image must include. An image without these is a generic Bear River valley landscape — discard it.

- **Bubbling spring openings in the foreground:** The bubbling should be visible — small continuous carbonation at multiple openings across the scene, like a spring that seems to be boiling without heat. This is the defining visual. Flat water fails the check.
- **White and rust-colored travertine mineral deposits:** Crusty calcium carbonate mounds and cones surrounding each spring mouth. Colors: white to cream at the freshest, rust-brown (#8B4513 area) where iron is present, buff (#D2B48C area) at older formations. Not just a patch of pale ground — they have three-dimensional structure, built up over decades into low cones and terraced forms. Some are knee-high, a few larger.
- **Steamboat Spring puffing in the middle distance:** The key dramatic element — a column of water and CO2 vapor rising several feet from a rock-rimmed opening, then subsiding. A still frame of a mid-puff moment (column visible, mist around it) is more useful than a quiescent frame. The rock around the opening is white with mineral crust.
- **Emigrants gathered around drinking:** Small figures at the springs, cups or hats held out toward the water. The social scene — a mix of resting, tasting, laughing. Not working. Not in distress. This is a rest stop with novelty value. Some women may be mixing sugar or vinegar into cups. Period clothing (bonnets, broad hats, rough linen shirts).
- **Green Bear River valley context:** The springs sit in a valley floor, not a desert or moonscape. Sage-covered hills rise on both sides. Cottonwood and willow along the river bank (off to one side of the spring cluster, not directly mixed with the springs). The contrast between the green valley and the white mineral crust is important.
- **Wagons in the background:** Pulled up and halted, not actively crossing or moving. This is a camp/rest scene. Canvas tops visible in the middle distance. Oxen unyoked or grazing on the meadow grass.
- **Volcanic landscape hints:** Visible lava outcrops on the hills or plains to the north. Not the primary focus, but the region's volcanic origin shows in the terrain — dark basalt poking through the grass, the flat-topped volcanic cones in the far distance.

## 5. Atmosphere and lighting

The spring cluster sits in open valley terrain — full exposure, no canyon walls to shade or channel light. Afternoon light is direct and high. Summer afternoons here build cumulus over the mountains; in late July and August a thunderstorm may approach from the south by mid-afternoon, providing the storm variant a dramatic context (the springs would continue to bubble while rain pelted the valley). The water in the springs catches sky color — deep blue on clear days, steel gray in overcast.

The most interesting light quality is the mist and vapor from Steamboat Spring. In cool morning air this rises clearly, backlit by morning sun from the east (the trail approaches from the east, so the party arrives with the sun at their backs in the morning and in their faces in the late afternoon). An early-morning arrival puts the sun behind the approaching party, so Steamboat Spring's vapor column would be lit from behind — a luminous, translucent plume against the western hills. Late afternoon puts raking amber light across the white mineral crust, turning the travertine formations from pale to golden. Both lighting conditions have strong visual potential; the default prompt targets afternoon.

The white mineralized ground near the springs is highly reflective. On a sunny afternoon, the spring area is one of the brightest spots in the scene — brighter than the surrounding meadow. This is a useful compositional anchor: the camera eye is drawn to that bright cluster.

The smell — a faint sulfurous note mixed with the mineral tang — is not renderable but affects period accounts consistently. Emigrants noted the smell from some distance before arrival, suggesting mild hydrogen sulfide mixed with CO2. This atmospheric element explains why some diaries describe "a distinctive odor" on approach; the visual rendering doesn't need to address it, but it is part of why the springs felt weird and alien to first-time visitors.

## 6. Period reference imagery

### John C. Frémont / Charles Preuss — 1843–1845 expedition maps and sketches

Frémont's cartographer Charles Preuss mapped the springs cluster in detail during the 1843 expedition. Preuss made careful maps of the Bear River valley and the spring locations. The resulting maps were included in Frémont's published report (1845) and constitute the earliest formal visual record of the spring cluster layout. Preuss is not known for artistic renderings, but the maps show relative positions of the major springs and the river.

- **What it adds:** The only period map of the springs' actual layout, distinguishing Steamboat Spring from Beer Spring and indicating relative distances. Useful for confirming compositional plausibility (springs cluster within a few hundred yards, Steamboat about a half-mile downstream from the main cluster).
- **Link:** Frémont's full report is available via Project Gutenberg and Internet Archive.
- **Blind spot:** Not a visual image — a topographic survey map. Cannot be used for mood, light, or color.

### J. Goldsborough Bruff — *Western side of the Soda or Beer Springs, near Bear R.* (August 1849)

Bruff, the meticulous diarist and illustrator of the Washington City and California Mining Association's 1849 Gold Rush party, made at least two drawings of the Soda Springs area: "Western side of the Soda or Beer Springs, near Bear R." and "The Beer-Springs." These are pencil sketches from the same 1849 series as his other trail drawings.

- **What it shows:** The spring area from a western perspective, likely showing the landscape context — valley floor, mineral mound forms, possibly emigrants at the springs.
- **Link:** Bruff collection at the Huntington Library (HM 8044, items 82 and 84); digitized portions available via OCTA and California Digital Library. [Huntington Library Bruff collection](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8pk0jbp/dsc/)
- **What it adds:** The only known period-emigrant sketches of the actual spring visual. Pencil-only, so no color, but the shape and scale of the formations would be captured.
- **Blind spot:** Pencil sketch, 1849 gold rush context (party was moving fast). May not show the full spring cluster or the travertine mound detail. Bruff's text quote ("the water was fine, only needed lemon syrup, to render it perfect soda") confirms he was there and drew it, but the specific visual content of the sketches is not confirmed from secondary sources.

### William Henry Jackson — *Hooper's Spring, Soda Springs, Caribou County, Idaho* (1871)

Jackson photographed Hooper Spring (the renamed Beer Spring) during the Hayden Survey in 1871. His photograph No. 315 in the 1871 catalog shows the spring with visible carbonic gas escaping and the mineral-crusted ground around it. This is the closest period-adjacent photograph of the actual springs during a time when the landscape was largely unchanged.

- **What it shows:** Hooper Spring (Beer Spring) in 1871 — the spring opening, the surrounding travertine-crusted terrain, visible gas escaping.
- **Link:** [ScienceBase USGS catalog item](https://www.sciencebase.gov/catalog/item/5ce84390e4b0b06920370502) — available as public domain JPEG from USGS Denver Library photographic collection. USGS reference: 57-HS-122.
- **What it adds:** The actual visual appearance of the ground around the springs — the mineralized crust, the spring opening shape, the valley context. Jackson photographed many western sites before development altered them; this is arguably the best visual record of the pre-reservoir springs.
- **Blind spot:** 1871, not 1843–1860. Some additional foot traffic and early development activity at Soda Springs by then. The spring itself is still pre-reservoir. Camp Connor (1863) had been abandoned by 1865; the 1870 Mormon settlement was just beginning. The springs look essentially as they did in 1850 — this is a very usable reference.

### General fur-trade-era art — Alfred Jacob Miller (1837)

Miller painted the mountain-man era in what is now Wyoming and southeastern Idaho during the 1837 Rocky Mountain Rendezvous. His paintings of mountain meadows, fur-trade encampments, and Indian gatherings — not Soda Springs specifically — establish the visual vocabulary for the fur-trade prelude described by Bonneville's account.

- **What it shows:** Open mountain valleys, cottonwoods, figures gathered around streams, the scale of human figures against wide western landscapes.
- **Link:** [Walters Art Museum Miller collection](https://art.thewalters.org/browse/creator/alfred-jacob-miller/) — multiple public-domain works online.
- **What it adds:** Atmosphere of the Bonneville-era scene — the social quality, mountain meadow palette, how figures cluster near water in the landscape. Closest period-art reference to the western mountain setting.
- **Blind spot:** Not Soda Springs specifically; Miller did not document the spring cluster. No mineral deposits, no bubbling water in his compositions.

## 7. Composition references from period art

The north_platte_1 calibrator offers a good contrast: that landmark's key composition is horizontal and extensive — wagons spread across a wide braided river. Soda Springs is the inverse: a clustered, intimate scene with the springs as objects of close attention, human figures bending toward the water, the Steamboat Spring's vertical plume as the only vertical accent.

Period landscapes of the West tend toward panoramic horizontals (Bierstadt, Catlin). Soda Springs should resist this. The springs need to be *close* — in the foreground and mid-ground, not horizon elements. The composition works best with:

- Springs in the **foreground**, mineral crust visible and detailed
- Figures kneeling, crouching, or standing at the springs in the **mid-ground**
- Steamboat Spring's vapor column as a **visual accent** in the middle distance, drawing the eye
- The Bear River cottonwood line and sage hills as the **background frame**
- The volcanic landscape (lava-studded hills, volcanic cones) at the far horizon

This is a landscape with a central curiosity, not a wide-open vista. The camera is at the springs, not looking at them from a distance.

## 9. Variants needed in-game

- **Default 1843–1860 fair weather:** The cluster scene with bubbling springs and Steamboat puffing — primary backdrop.
- **Storm variant:** Build off the default; storm variant should use the same foreground spring cluster but darken the sky and add wind motion in the cottonwoods. The bubbling continues regardless of weather.
- **Dawn variant:** The backlighting of the Steamboat Spring vapor column is a genuinely beautiful rendering option; worth testing as the "arrive-at-dawn" version.
- **No special period variants needed:** Springs were active and largely unchanged across 1843–1860. No period-gating required. The Fort Connor infrastructure (1863) and Alexander Reservoir (1924) that destroyed springs are both well outside our window.
- **Approach variant (optional):** The sight of the Steamboat Spring vapor column on approach, before the party is at the springs, would support a "you see something strange in the distance" narrative moment. This requires a wider shot from the east, with a small vapor plume visible above the valley floor.

## 10. What NOT to render

- **Modern Soda Springs, Idaho town:** The city exists on this site today; do not include buildings, streets, paved roads, the modern Caribou County courthouse, or any town infrastructure.
- **Alexander Reservoir:** The reservoir that drowned Steamboat Spring and Beer Spring was built in 1924. No water body large enough to submerge springs should appear. The Bear River is visible but is a modest mountain river.
- **The modern Steamboat Spring monument:** A small roadside monument stands near the original site today. Not period-accurate.
- **The 1937 captive geyser:** An entirely different structure (a capped wellhead on a municipal timer) — this is the #1 modern photo-search result for "Soda Springs geyser" and it looks nothing like the historical Steamboat Spring. Any image of a mechanical geyser cap or urban geyser should be discarded.
- **Fort Connor:** Built in 1863 by General Patrick Connor, this military post is three years outside our window. No log barracks, no fortifications, no military presence in the 1843–1860 scene.
- **Thermal geyser aesthetics:** Soda Springs is cold carbonated water driven by CO2 pressure, NOT a thermal geyser like those at Yellowstone. No superheated steam, no orange/yellow sulfur deposits, no thermal pools. The water is cool to cold. The color of the mineral deposits is white/cream/rust from calcium carbonate and iron, not the orange/yellow of thermal sulfur chemistry.
- **Dramatic geyser height:** Steamboat Spring threw water "to a variable height of about three feet" (Frémont). Modern Yellowstone associations will push the AI to render a 20-foot column. The puff is modest — a knee-to-waist-height pulse, with mist, not a dramatic tower.
- **The Shoshone encampment as background filler:** Shoshone and Bannock bands were present in the region and emigrants traded with them, but no historical account specifically documents a large Native encampment directly at the spring cluster. Don't add it as generic background — period accounts do not confirm it as a visual element at this specific landmark the way the Platte River accounts do for the Upper California Crossing.

## 11. Notes / open questions

### The Steamboat Spring's mechanism

The Steamboat Spring was not a thermal geyser. It was a cold-water spring driven by CO2 gas pressure accumulated in underground volcanic formations. The geologic explanation: past volcanic activity in the region left pockets of CO2 gas trapped beneath the water table. As water percolates through limestone formations, it becomes enriched with dissolved calcium carbonate and CO2. At the Steamboat Spring's location, an underground CO2 pocket was connected to the surface opening in a way that produced the periodic pulsing — pressure builds, discharges water and gas, pressure drops, refills, repeats. The timing variation across diary accounts (15 seconds to "every few minutes") likely reflects real seasonal and year-to-year pressure variations as the underground system fluctuated.

For the game renderer: the visual distinction from a thermal geyser is important because FLUX/SDXL training data is heavily weighted toward Yellowstone images. The prompt must explicitly suppress thermal geyser aesthetics: no superheated steam cloud, no boardwalk, no orange microbial mats, no high geyser column.

### The reservoir and what it buried

Alexander Reservoir was built in 1924, drowning both Steamboat Spring and the original Beer Spring (later Hooper Spring). When the reservoir was lowered in 1976 for maintenance, workers found a small locomotive the construction crews had left behind when the reservoir was built. The surviving springs accessible today — Hooper Springs Park, Octagon Spring — are outside the original reservoir area and still flow with naturally carbonated water. They represent what all the springs looked like in the 1840s.

### The mileage question

The 04-landmarks.md file lists Soda Springs at mile 1170, the header of this doc follows that. Some OCTA sources give 1145–1175 depending on route variant. The cluster extended over a few miles so a range is appropriate.

### What Bruff actually drew

The Huntington Library catalog records two Bruff Soda Springs drawings (HM 8044 items 82 and 84). These would be the closest primary-source visual references available. The full Bruff digital archive at the California Digital Library may have these digitized. This doc was unable to directly access and describe the image content. A future pass should retrieve and describe the Bruff sketches before the first FLUX generation run — they may show the travertine mound scale and spring layout more precisely than any other available source.

### Father De Smet

The Vikunja brief mentions Father Pierre-Jean De Smet 1840 sketches. De Smet traveled with the Bidwell-Bartleson party in 1841 (not 1840) as a missionary heading to the Flathead Nation. He departed the party at Soda Springs, continuing north toward Fort Hall while Bidwell's group turned toward California. De Smet was a prolific correspondent and may have produced descriptions or sketches, but this doc found no confirmed surviving De Smet visual of the springs. His 1841 account may exist in his collected letters (*Letters and Sketches with a Narrative of a Year's Residence Among the Indian Tribes of the Rocky Mountains*, 1843) but this was not directly accessed.

## 12. Code recommendation

The `soda_springs` landmark currently has the kind annotation "spring/water" and the note "Carbonated mineral springs and intermittent geyser; everyone tasted, did laundry, marveled." This is historically accurate and the kind annotation is correct. No rename needed.

The landmark does not model the spring-drinking interaction explicitly. A flavor event at this stop — "You fill your cup from one of the bubbling springs and drink. The water fizzes on your tongue, cool and mineral-sharp. Several emigrants are mixing in sugar and vinegar. You have never tasted anything like it." — is historically well-supported and would differentiate this stop from generic water-replenishment stops.

The Steamboat Spring's intermittent puffing could support a brief animation or particle effect in the backdrop (a CSS keyframe pulse on the steam/water column in the Svelte component) — but this is a rendering decision outside this doc's scope.
