# Three Island Crossing of the Snake River — Visual Reference

**Landmark ID:** `snake_three_island`
**In-game position:** ~mile 1450 (Fort Hall + 235 mi via Massacre Rocks / Salmon Falls)
**Location:** Near modern Glenns Ferry, ID (Elmore County)
**Kind:** River crossing
**Vikunja:** #1120 — https://projects.ericbond.net/tasks/1120

---

## 1. Why this landmark matters visually

Three Island Crossing is the most visually distinctive ford on the entire Oregon Trail. Most river crossings are a single channel. This one is a half-mile diagonal traverse using three cottonwood-and-willow-covered islands as stepping stones — the wagon train zigzagging from south bank to island one, island one to island two, island two to island three, island three to the north bank, with deep fast-water channels between each hop. Every adult emigrant who survived described it. Artists who depicted the trail returned to it. The shape — three diagonal green dots strung across a wide dark-green river, flanked by near-black basalt bluffs — is singular.

A second visual element makes it compositionally unique: the south-bank camp of wagons that *refused* to cross. At roughly every other season, companies split here. Half forded north; half peeled away south. The art in the JSX source already captures this — wagons mid-stream, and wagons still drawn up on the south bank watching.

In-game river stats: depth 5.0 ft, current 3 mph, ferry $6. The native ferry (Bannock-Shoshone, priced at 4 strings of beads) is the only alternative to the ford for the 1843–1860 game window. Gus Glenn's rope ferry postdates the game window by a decade (1869).

## 2. Period diary record

### Narcissa Whitman (August 13, 1836)

Narcissa Whitman was the first woman to travel the Oregon Trail to the continental divide. She passed Three Island Crossing as part of the 1836 ABCFM missionary expedition. Her journal entry is the earliest substantial firsthand description of the crossing:

> "The river is divided by two islands into three branches, and is fordable. The packs are placed upon the tops of the highest horses and in this way we crossed without wetting. . . . The last branch we rode as much as half a mile in crossing and against the current too, which made it hard for the horses, the water being up to their sides. Husband had considerable difficulty in crossing the cart. Both cart and mules were turned upside down in the river and entangled in the harness. The mules would have been drowned but for a desperate struggle to get them ashore. Then after putting two of the strongest horses before the cart, and two men swimming behind to steady it, they succeeded in getting it across."
>
> — *Narcissa Whitman, Journal, August 13, 1836*

**Notes on this source:** Whitman notes "two islands" creating "three branches" — consistent with how the crossing worked: two main islands defined three ford channels. In high water, the islands partially submerged, reducing visual count; in low water, sandbars extended the islands. The cart overturn is a critical visual cue: this was not a gentle wade. The "half a mile in crossing and against the current" on the final branch conveys the breadth and power of the Snake here.

### William T. Newby (September 11, 1843)

Newby emigrated with the Great Migration of 1843 — the first mass wagon emigration, ~875–1,000 people. His diary entry is the canonical account of the island-stepping technique with wagons:

> "We crawsed Snake Rive[r]. First we drove over a part of the river one hundred yards wide on to a island, the[n] over a northern branch 75 yards wide on a second island; then we tide a string of waggons together by a chane in the ring of the lead cattles yoak & made fast to the waggon of all a horse & before & him led. We carried as many a[s] fifteen waggons at one time. The water was ten inches up the waggeo[n] beds in the deepe plaices. It was 900 hundred yards acraws."
>
> — *William T. Newby, Diary, September 11, 1843* (original spelling preserved)

**Notes on this source:** The island-stepping geometry is exact: the first ford (100 yards) to reach island one; the second ford (75 yards, "northern branch") to reach island two. The chain-string method — tying 15 wagons together by the lead cattle's yoke ring, with a horse out front — is the period engineering solution to a 3 mph current. "Ten inches up the wagon beds in the deepest places" is why the art shows only canvas tops above the waterline in the deepest channels. The total distance of 900 yards (~half a mile) matches modern surveys of the crossing geometry.

### Robert Haldane Renshaw (1851)

Renshaw emigrated in 1851, late in the main emigration period. His diary, cited in NPS Three Island Crossing Overlook interpretive material, describes the practical engineering solution:

> "We crossed two slews to the second island. Here we put ox yokes under the wagon loads to raise them, and put four yoke of our best oxen to each of the four first wagons that crossed."
>
> — *Robert Haldane Renshaw, Diary, 1851*

He records that the four lead wagons crossed safely, and the teams were sent back to collect the remaining three wagons. All got over "our cattle and all safely." The ox-yoke-as-lifting-block method is a detail worth noting: emigrants improvised any available hardwood to jack the wagon box above the waterline before committing to the deep channels.

### Eli Cooley (1845)

Cooley's 1845 entry captures a high-water year when river levels partially submerged the islands, reducing the apparent count to two:

> "Weather fine to day — rode quite broken and sandy. Come about 2 miles to the crossing of Snake and crossed over part of the wagons — plenty of grass and some willow and sage. Here they is 2 islands in the river at the crossing and the water is quite deep and runs verry swift."
>
> — *Eli Cooley, Diary, 1845*

**Notes on this source:** The "2 islands" observation is critically important for visual calibration: island geometry changed year to year with seasonal flooding and river morphology. The description "plenty of grass and some willow and sage" is the vegetation note — low willow on the islands and sage up on the bluffs above the canyon walls. "Quite broken and sandy" describes the approach terrain from the east: the wagons descended a sandy, broken slope down to river level after traveling the sage plain.

## 3. The crossing decision: north route vs. south route

The most charged moment at Three Island Crossing was not the crossing itself but the argument before it. Wagon companies debated for days — or split overnight:

**The choice:**
- **North route** (cross): Shorter path to Fort Boise via the north bank. The Boise River valley was lush and well-watered. Thousand Springs, visible from the north bank, poured dozens of waterfalls directly from the basalt cliffs. The stock got good grass and water.
- **South route** (stay south bank): The "South Alternate" bypassed two dangerous Snake crossings. It was dry, sandy, dusty, and hot. Water access was limited because the Snake ran in a canyon inaccessible from the plain above. Stock suffered from the heat and alkali. The Malad River gorge required a major detour to skirt. But no one drowned.

About half the emigration chose each route in most years. Diaries from 1847 and later describe wagon companies separating at this point with genuine grief — families who had traveled 1,400 miles together saying goodbye, not knowing if they'd meet again in Oregon.

The decision was largely governed by water level. In low-water years (late August through September), the crossing was frightening but manageable. In high-water years, the fords between islands became swimming crossings — wagons afloat, oxen swimming, livestock swept downstream. Cooley's 1845 entry ("water is quite deep and runs verry swift") is a high-water record; most parties that year may have stayed south.

**Visual consequence:** This decision dynamic is already in the JSX art: south-bank wagons drawn up in camp while other wagons are mid-ford. The south-bank camp is not random background dressing — it's the other half of the wagon company, the ones who decided the Snake was not worth it today.

## 4. Shoshone-Bannock presence and native ferry

The Shoshone and Bannock people used the Three Island ford for millennia before the emigration. It was a traditional crossing between the buffalo plains culture of the eastern Snake River country and the salmon culture of the upper Columbia system. The crossing was, as one interpretive account puts it, "the link between the Indian Cultures of the Plains and the Midwest who hunted buffalo, and the ways of the Northwest tribes whose dietary staple was salmon and other fish."

For emigrants, the Shoshone were a practical resource:

**Native guides at the ford:** Young Shoshone men who could swim — unlike the majority of the emigrants — were hired to drive and ride livestock across the river. Many diaries reported they would not have made it without this assistance. One unattributed but frequently cited diary entry reads: "Wednesday [August] 6 — This morning we hired an Indian to show us the ford. After we saw him cross we determined to try it ourselves."

**Bull-boat crossing:** The last crossing of the Snake River (at old Fort Boise, downstream) used bull-boats while swimming the stock across — and the same technique was available here from Bannock-Shoshone operators. A bull-boat is a small round or oval frame covered with a single large buffalo hide, sealed with tallow. A competent operator could ferry a person, some supplies, or a child across deep water while livestock swam.

**In-game note:** The `nativeFerry` in `landmarks.ts` (`tribeId: 'shoshone'`, priced at 4 strings of beads) maps to this reality. The price note in the code cites Frizzell 1852 — Lydia Rudd Frizzell paid "a knife and a few strings of beads" for a crossing lift at a Snake River ford. The game price (4 beads + 1 lb tobacco) is slightly above cost to reflect that Three Island Crossing was the primary ford and Shoshone operators knew their market.

**No formal ferry until 1869:** Gus Glenn established a rope ferry about two miles upstream in 1869, founding the town that became Glenns Ferry. For the entire 1843–1860 game window, the only options were: ford via the islands, take the native bull-boat ferry, or detour south.

## 5. Setting and geology

### The Snake River canyon at Glenns Ferry

The Snake River at Three Island Crossing flows through the western Snake River Plain — a volcanic tectonic graben stretching from Glenns Ferry westward to Huntington, Oregon. The canyon walls are dark basalt: olivine basalt flows and phreatomagmatic tuffs deposited 1–12 million years ago, now eroded into sheer canyon walls with the characteristic columnar jointing of slow-cooled basalt.

The river is wide here — the islands exist precisely because the canyon floor widens enough for the river to spread and slow slightly. Upstream and downstream, the canyon walls pinch the river into faster, deeper channels. At the crossing point, the valley floor opens, the river braids, and the three islands emerge.

**Visible geology from the crossing:**
- Dark basalt canyon walls upstream and downstream, lighter above the waterline (dust and lichen), almost black below
- Columnar jointing visible in cliff faces — the diagnostic texture of Snake River volcanic country
- Sage-covered canyon rim above the basalt walls — the high plain emigrants traveled before descending to river level
- Sinker Butte and other volcanic remnants (tuff-capped basalt cones) visible in the middle distance upstream

**Owyhee Mountains:** The Owyhee Mountains are visible from the south bank to the southwest — a low blue range on the horizon above the canyon rim. They frame the distant view beyond the crossing and were the first significant mountain landform emigrants had seen since the Bear River Divide. Multiple diaries note the mountains as a reference point in this stretch of the Snake country.

**Vegetation:**
- *On the islands:* Cottonwood and willow — the same riparian species that appear at every Snake River gravel bar. The cottonwoods would have been in full leaf in July–September, giving the islands a bright green canopy against the dark river
- *On the south bank:* Sage and rabbitbrush, sandy bluffs, sparse bunch grass
- *On the north bank:* More grass, approaching the better-watered terrain that made the north route attractive

## 6. Visual composition notes for FLUX/SDXL

### What makes this scene unique

The make-or-break visual element is the **three diagonal islands** — not one island, not two. They must read as a clear diagonal chain from the upper-right (upstream/north bank side) to the lower-left (downstream/south bank side), because that is the geometry of the ford. Wagons crossed diagonally, always moving against the downstream current, stepping from island to island at a slight upstream angle to compensate for drift.

Secondary elements in priority order:
1. Three islands diagonal across the wide green river — cottonwood and willow canopy, sandy beaches at water's edge
2. Wagons mid-ford — white canvas tops barely above the waterline in the deep channels between islands
3. Dark basalt canyon walls flanking the scene upstream and downstream
4. South-bank camp of wagons that chose not to cross (or are waiting their turn)
5. Wide, sage-covered canyon rim above the basalt, Owyhee Mountains faint on the southern horizon
6. Shoshone guides (optional) visible at the water's edge or astride horses in the river

### What NOT to render

- Modern Glenns Ferry town, I-84 overpass, or any modern structure
- The Three Island Crossing State Park reconstructed wagon (an interpretive reproduction, not period)
- Gus Glenn's 1869 rope ferry (postdates the game window)
- A single crossing channel without the islands — that would be any Snake River ford, not this one
- Islands that are submerged or ambiguous — if the islands aren't readable, the composition has failed

## 8. Verification notes and open questions

### What is well-verified

- Three islands diagonal across the Snake River: confirmed by Whitman 1836 ("two islands into three branches"), Newby 1843 (100-yard + 75-yard fords between islands), multiple later accounts
- Island vegetation (cottonwood and willow): confirmed by Cooley 1845 ("some willow and sage") and by the general riparian botany of Snake River gravel bars
- Wagon depth in deep channels: confirmed by Newby 1843 ("ten inches up the wagon beds")
- Total crossing distance (~900 yards): Newby 1843
- North route vs. south route decision, roughly half-and-half split: confirmed by multiple period accounts and Idaho State Parks interpretive material
- Dark basalt geology: geologically confirmed; volcanic Snake River Plain basalt is the dominant rock at this latitude
- Shoshone guides hired to swim livestock: multiple diary references, confirmed by NPS and Idaho Parks interpretive material
- Gus Glenn's ferry in 1869: confirmed; safely postdates game window

### What is uncertain or unverified

**Island morphology:** The exact number, size, and geometry of the islands shifted over decades due to flooding. Modern photographs show the Three Island Crossing area with fewer and smaller islands than period descriptions suggest — the Bonneville Flood history and subsequent river manipulation (upstream dams) have significantly altered the Snake's gravel bars. Do not use modern photographs as the shape reference; use Newby's channel widths (100 yd + 75 yd) as the geometric anchor.

**Bannock vs. Shoshone distinction at this site:** The in-game `nativeFerry` uses `tribeId: 'shoshone'`. The Bannock were closely associated with the Snake River Plain in this era and often camped at major fords. The distinction between Bannock and Northern Shoshone bands at this specific site in 1843–1860 is not resolved in accessible secondary sources. Dave should know the code blurb ("Bannock-Shoshone raft") is ethnographically cautious but not precisely sourced to Three Island Crossing specifically. The Frizzell 1852 reference in the code comment is for a Snake River ford generally, not confirmed as specifically Three Island.

**Owyhee Mountains visibility:** The Owyhee range is southwest of Glenns Ferry and geographically plausible to see from the south bank. However, I could not confirm from period sources that emigrants specifically named the Owyhees as visible from this crossing. This is a reasonable compositional element but should be treated as plausible-period rather than diary-confirmed.

**Frémont at Three Island, 1843:** Frémont's 1843 expedition passed through this area in late September, and the reference that "early fall rains had caused the river to rise" and they "had to resort to their inflatable boat to cross" is plausible, but I could not pull the exact Frémont journal passage for this specific ford. His published report would have the entry; online sources give only paraphrases.

## 9. Code recommendation: model north/south as a player choice

The north route vs. south route decision at Three Island Crossing is the most significant unmodeled player choice on the Snake River leg. It is currently a passive event (the river crossing fires with its standard dice rolls), but historically this was a deliberate, consequential, agonized choice that shaped the second half of the journey.

**Recommended approach:** Surface a player-facing modal at arrival at `snake_three_island` that presents the two options explicitly:

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Attempt the ford (north route) | River crossing event fires with current stats (depth 5.0 ft, 3 mph). Success: north bank terrain improvements (better water access, shorter distance to Fort Boise by ~20 miles). Failure risk: wagon damage, livestock loss, drowning event. |
| South alternate route | Skip the ford entirely. Apply "dry alternate" terrain penalties: reduced water access, livestock attrition, +15 miles distance to Fort Boise. No drowning risk. |
| Native bull-boat ferry | Pay 4 beads (the existing `nativeFerry` price). Cross safely with reduced risk, but livestock still swim (retain some drowning/loss risk for the herd). |

The existing `nativeFerry` mechanic already models the third option. Making the first two explicit choices (rather than just "attempt crossing or don't") would make this landmark feel like the diary accounts — a real decision with real stakes, not just a dice roll.
