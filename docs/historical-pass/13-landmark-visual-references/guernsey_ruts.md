# Guernsey Ruts / Deep Rut Hill (mile 672, WY)

**Type:** Navigation marker — wagon ruts worn 1–5 ft into Arikaree sandstone ridge
**Kind in code:** `landmark` (id `guernsey_ruts`)
**Existing component:** `src/lib/ui/landmark-art/GuernseyRutsArt.svelte`
**Vikunja child:** [#1098](https://projects.ericbond.net/tasks/1098)
**Period gates:** None — the ruts existed before our 1843 window opens and deepened continuously through 1869. No build-state or ruin-state required. The relevant period-accuracy constraint is rut *depth*: 1843 ruts were shallow (perhaps 1 ft); by 1860 they were likely 2–3 ft; the modern 5-ft maximum is the final state after railroad-era traffic ended in 1869.

---

## What it actually looked like (1843–1860)

Guernsey Ruts is unlike every other landmark in the game set. There is no architecture, no dramatic geological spire, no fort wall or cliff face. **The subject is the trail itself** — a half-mile run of parallel wheel channels worn into a low sandstone ridge rising from the north bank of the North Platte River, roughly two miles west of Register Cliff and approximately 20 miles southwest of Fort Laramie. What makes this place the most significant surviving physical trace of the Oregon Trail anywhere along its 2,000-mile length is not what it looks like in isolation but what it *records*: the weight and number of every wagon that passed, compressed into a single narrow corridor by the geology.

The geological constraint is the key to understanding the composition. The trail north of the river had followed the flat bottomland for miles; at this point the terrain forced a transition to the High Plains tableland, and the easiest crossing was over a low ridge of soft Miocene Arikaree sandstone — a tuffaceous, volcanic-ash-bearing stone that is notably erodible compared to the granite and harder formations west of here. The ridge was too narrow to offer an alternative crossing route: every wagon, from the Bartleson-Bidwell party's first wagons in 1841 through the last ox trains before the transcontinental railroad in 1869, rolled through the same channels. Intentional widening by emigrants — who sometimes chipped and leveled the stone to ease the grade up from the river bottom — accelerated the wear. The result was progressive deepening, year by year.

In our game window of 1843–1860, the ruts were a **living, deepening feature** visible to every emigrant but not yet at the five-foot maximum the modern site preserves. The Bartleson-Bidwell passage of 1841 left only shallow grooves in the surface stone; the 1843 season (over 900 emigrants) deepened them perceptibly; by the great migration years of 1849–1852 (California gold rush traffic) the channels were probably knee-deep to waist-deep in the worst sections, roughly 1–2 feet at the shallower approaches, 2–3 feet at the deepest cut on the ridge-crest. By 1860 the ruts were likely shoulder-deep at their deepest point and recognizable even from the river bottom below. The modern five-foot depth represents the cumulative final state.

What emigrants saw was a **pair of parallel channels** spaced to standard wagon-track width — approximately 4 to 5 feet outside-to-outside — worn smooth by iron-shod wheels into pale, buff-tan sandstone. The stone surface between and beside the ruts retained the slightly rougher natural texture; the channel floors were worn glassy-smooth by the continuous grinding. The walls of each channel were straighter than they looked in the natural rock face, because wheel hubs and wagon bodies had burnished the sides. On the walls and alongside the ruts, hub-scrape marks — shallow diagonal gouges — showed where the widening wagons had clipped the stone as they tracked through at angles. The grade *up* to the ridge crest was the hardest pull: loaded wagons needed extra animals or multiple teams to crest the rise, and the stone here shows the most concentrated wear from locked-wheel braking on the descent side.

The **setting** is rolling sage-and-bunchgrass prairie, not dramatic badlands. The ridge itself is low — perhaps 20–30 feet above the surrounding terrain at its highest, with no dramatic vertical face. Sage brush and bunchgrass grew right to the edges of the ruts. Below the ridge, the North Platte floodplain supported a ribbon of cottonwood woodland, visible to the north. The overall atmosphere was one of ordinary prairie with an extraordinary human trace running through it.

Lodisa Frizzell, traveling to California in June 1852, passed through this stretch shortly after Fort Laramie and described the road: *"The roads here are smooth & excellent, but hilly, & in some places very steep, it is so hard that it is bad on the cattles feet, & waggons."* The hardness she noted was the sandstone surface underfoot — a road surface unlike anything emigrants had traveled on the sod and clay of the Platte Valley eastward. The ruts were not a curiosity then but a *necessity*: wagon wheels that fell into the channels were channeled and would not track straight until they cleared the stone — the ruts made the trail and the trail made the ruts.

Overton Johnson, one of the 1843 emigrants who helped establish the first heavy wagon travel through this corridor, described the landscape west of Fort Laramie with a draftsman's brevity, noting that by that summer the road to the west was "growing broader and deeper as four-wheeled vehicles carrying farm implements, seeds, grain and cherished pieces of furniture made indelible tracks, with domestic cattle tramping down the native sod." Johnson was writing of the trail in aggregate, but the description captures the active process visible at this specific crossing point: the trail was becoming more itself each season.

Amos Batchelder, camped in the Guernsey area in 1849, described the Warm Springs hollow just southwest of the ruts: the spring emerged *"at the foot of a hill of limestone, in a dry, sandy, barren valley"* surrounded by high hills with sparse vegetation. That "barren valley" texture — pale stone, sparse sage, exposed ridgeline — is exactly the landscape context for the ruts themselves: not a dramatic red-rock canyon but a bleached, open, treeless ridge system.

## Distinctive visual features

The cues a viewer must see to recognize the place. A generated image without these is wrong — discard the candidate.

- **Twin parallel channels in pale sandstone** — this is the non-negotiable primary subject. A single channel, a trench, or a road-cut in dirt are all wrong. Two parallel grooves, wagon-width apart (~4–5 ft outside-to-outside), running across the frame from near to far. If these are absent, the image is the wrong landmark entirely.
- **Rut depth appropriate to our period window** — for a 1850-nominal scene, the channels should read as roughly knee-to-waist deep (1.5–2.5 ft), not the five-foot-deep canyons of the modern site and not mere surface scratches. A viewer standing beside them should look *down* into them without losing sight of the opposite wall. A wagon wheel fully seated in the channel should have the hub at or near ground level.
- **Low sandstone ridge setting** — the ruts are not in a cliff face or a canyon. The ridge is a gentle rise above the river plain. Foreground and middleground are at roughly the same elevation; the horizon is open prairie beyond.
- **Arikaree sandstone color — pale buff tan (#d4c28e) in afternoon light**, slightly warmer at the surface (#c8b47a), with the worn channel floors showing a slightly darker weather-stained tan (#b09060) from accumulated rain darkening. The stone is NOT red-orange like Utah sandstone and NOT grey like granite. It reads as warm pale cream in bright light.
- **Raking light catching the channel edges** — the most distinctive visual moment is late-afternoon sun throwing a shadow into one side of each rut channel, making the worn grooves pop as light-and-shadow pairs across the ridge. Without this directional light, the ruts can disappear against the pale stone.
- **Sage prairie context at ground level** — bunchgrass and low sage right to the edges of the ruts. No trees on the ridge; cottonwood woodland only in the river bottom to the north (not visible unless composition explicitly pulls north).
- **Ox team and wagon in the channels or approaching** — a small ox-team wagon partway up the grade, with the wagon wheels visibly seated in the rut channels, provides the crucial scale reference. At scale, the wagon body would be partially below the ridge-top plane at the deeper-cut sections by 1850. This element also gives the image human legibility — without it, the scene risks reading as abstract geology.
- **Rut walls showing smooth-worn texture and hub-scrape marks** — faint diagonal grooves on the channel walls, worn by hub flanges of passing wagons. Not deeply carved, but visible in raking light.

## Atmosphere and lighting

This stretch of the North Platte valley runs roughly west-southwest, which means the ridge runs across the trail at an angle that matters for lighting: a wagon heading west-southwest crosses the ridge on a northwest-to-southeast diagonal when the ruts run perpendicular to the trail direction. In late afternoon, the descending sun from the west-southwest throws raking light *along* the rut channels — the ideal lighting condition for revealing their depth and texture. This is the canonical moment: a low sun at perhaps 30–40 degrees above the horizon sending long shadows into the western wall of each channel while the eastern wall catches warm gold. The sandstone surface takes on a honey-tan glow in this light; the channel shadows go deep ochre-brown.

Midsummer on the Wyoming high plains is intense. By the peak migration months of June and July, the midday temperature on the exposed sandstone ridge could reach 90°F or higher, the sun bleaching the stone toward near-white with no shadow definition. Midday is the worst rendering moment — the ruts effectively disappear into the flat glare, which is historically accurate (emigrants typically crossed this section in the mid-morning, having camped the night before at Register Cliff, the first camp west of Fort Laramie). For the game's default composition, late afternoon is strongly preferred.

The North Platte valley floor carries afternoon breezes, and sage pollen is pervasive in June. A faint dusty haze along the river bottom (not the ridge itself, which was exposed and wind-scoured) adds period atmosphere. Afternoon thunderstorms build regularly over the Laramie Peak range to the north, visible as purple-grey cloud masses; a distant storm behind the ridge can provide dramatic contrast to the pale stone foreground. The prairie itself is a dry sage-green (#8a9468) in June, bleaching toward yellow-grey by late July.

Ox teams pulling up the grade would have been steaming and laboring. The grade is modest by mountain standards — perhaps a 10–15% incline — but with a 2,500-lb loaded wagon and iron-tired wheels seated in rut channels, even this much of a rise taxed the team. Yoke chains would be taut, animals' heads down, teamsters shouting or using a goad. This is a strain moment, not a rest moment.

## Period reference imagery

### William Henry Jackson — photographs documenting the Oregon Trail, 1866–1870s

- **What it shows:** Jackson drove an ox team as a bullwhacker on the trail in 1866, giving him direct physical experience of the ruts. He later photographed the ruts as part of documentary work. A Library of Congress collection entry, *"A preserved site of wagon ruts of the Oregon Trail on the North Platte River, near Guernsey, Wyoming,"* preserves at least one documented photograph of the ruts (LOC item 2017685124).
- **Link:** [Library of Congress item 2017685124](https://www.loc.gov/item/2017685124/) and [Library of Congress item 2017685129](https://www.loc.gov/item/2017685129/)
- **What it adds:** Jackson's photos from this period document the ruts before the railroad ended emigrant traffic and before the modern state park era with its trails, walkways, and infrastructure. His images represent the ruts at their deepest natural state (post-1869 maximum, 4–5 ft) — the *upper bound* for our period. Use his photos for color, texture, and scale reference; remember that 1850-period ruts were 1–2 ft shallower.
- **Blind spot:** Jackson's photography postdates our game window by 6–20+ years. The ruts in his photos are at their maximum depth; our period renders should reduce depth accordingly. His black-and-white wet-plate negatives cannot give color information directly — sandstone color must be inferred from known Arikaree formation characteristics.

### GuernseyRutsArt.jsx — in-house handoff SVG composition

- **What it shows:** The existing vector art component for the landmark (at `docs/handoff/landmark-art/src/guernsey-ruts-art.jsx`) depicts the ruts from inside the cut, looking through a narrow stone corridor at a distant wagon descending toward the viewer. The floor shows twin parallel groove channels receding in perspective; hub-scrape marks are rendered on the inner walls.
- **What it adds:** An existing compositional reference calibrated to our game's visual language. The stone color used (`#cdb887` for sandstone, `#8a704a` for shadow faces, `#5a4628` for deep shadow) is within range for Arikaree sandstone but slightly warm; real Arikaree at Guernsey reads slightly cooler and paler than this rendering.
- **Blind spot:** The SVG art renders the cut as more enclosed and dramatic than the 1850-period reality — the walls are taller relative to the floor width, suggesting the maximum-depth 5-ft state. The correct period view would have shallower walls relative to the channel width. The composition is also very close-in; the recommended FLUX default is a wider-angle view showing the ruts crossing the ridge.

### NPS Oregon National Historic Trail site documentation

- **What it shows:** Current interpretive photography and signage at the Wyoming State Historic Site. Modern photos show the maximum-depth ruts (5 ft at the deepest cut), paved paths, and interpretive panels.
- **Link:** [NPS Oregon NHT — Oregon Trail Ruts Historic Site](https://www.nps.gov/oreg/planyourvisit/oregon-trail-ruts-historic-site.htm) / [Wyoming State Parks](https://wyoparks.wyo.gov/index.php/places-to-go/oregon-trail-ruts)
- **What it adds:** Ground-level visual confirmation of the sandstone color, rut channel geometry, surrounding terrain, and vegetation palette. The Arikaree sandstone in modern photos is clearly a warm buff-tan — similar to the hex values used in the existing SVG art. The rut walls show the smooth-worn texture and the stria of hub-scrapes clearly.
- **Blind spot:** Modern photos are completely wrong for period accuracy in several ways: the ruts are at their 5-ft maximum depth, paved walkways run alongside the channels, an interpretive shelter and modern signage are visible, and the surrounding landscape has been managed for visitor safety. None of these elements belong in a 1843–1860 backdrop. Use modern photos only for stone color and texture reference.

### WyoHistory.org — Oregon Trail Ruts article

- **What it shows:** The Wyoming State Historical Society's encyclopedia entry provides the authoritative secondary-source account of the ruts' formation, usage dates, and designation history.
- **Link:** [WyoHistory.org — Oregon Trail Ruts](https://www.wyohistory.org/encyclopedia/oregon-trail-ruts)
- **What it adds:** Clear statement of the usage timeline (1841–1869), depth range (2–6 ft), cause (iron-tired wheels + intentional cutting), and the geographical constraint (trail forced onto this one ridge).
- **Blind spot:** No period imagery, no diary quotes in the text itself. The figure "2 to 6 feet" describes the variation across the full rut length and the full temporal range — a single point on the ridge in 1850 was probably no deeper than 2–3 ft.

### Legends of America — Deep Rut Hill and Guernsey State Park

- **What it shows:** Aggregated secondary-source description of the ruts, Guernsey State Park context, and CCC history.
- **Link:** [Legends of America — Guernsey Ruts](https://www.legendsofamerica.com/wy-guernseyruts/)
- **What it adds:** The 300,000-emigrant estimate for total trail traffic confirms the scale of cumulative wear. The characterization of the terrain as a "soft sandstone ridge" forced the entire emigrant flow through a single bottleneck.
- **Blind spot:** No diary quotes, no geological detail beyond "soft sandstone." CCC-era park structures (1930s stone shelter, walkway) are referenced but should not appear in renders.

### GeoWyo.com — Fort Laramie and Hartville Uplift geology

- **What it shows:** Geological identification of the sandstone as **Miocene Arikaree Formation**, approximately 23–17 million years old, consisting of 700 feet of tuffaceous (volcanic-ash-bearing) sandstone with occasional limestone concretions. The same formation makes up the cliff face at Register Cliff 2 miles east.
- **Link:** [GeoWyo.com — Fort Laramie & Hartville Uplift](https://www.geowyo.com/fort-laramie--hartville-uplift.html)
- **What it adds:** Critical for color accuracy: Arikaree sandstone is light-colored, warm buff-to-cream, *not* red-orange (that's Brule clay / Chadron formation, visible at Chimney Rock and Scotts Bluff farther east). The volcanic ash in the mix gives the stone a slightly chalky, pale quality. Register Cliff and Guernsey Ruts are made of the *same* stone — the visual language that works for Register Cliff applies directly here.
- **Blind spot:** Geological sources confirm composition but not the period rut depth. The depth figure is a historical, not geological, question.

### Warm Springs emigrant diaries (Amos Batchelder, Israel Hale, Henry Puckett, 1849–1850)

- **What it shows:** Multiple emigrant accounts of Warm Springs, located approximately 3 miles southwest of present-day Guernsey. These diarists camped at or visited the springs, placing them in the same landscape context as the ruts.
- **Source:** WyoHistory.org — Warm Springs article; cited in context above.
- **What it adds:** Visual ground-truth for the general terrain: "a dry, sandy, barren valley" surrounded by high limestone hills, sparse vegetation. This is the landscape context surrounding the ruts. No lush green, no dramatic rocky cliffs — barren, pale, exposed.
- **Blind spot:** None of these diarists explicitly describe the ruts themselves, only the adjacent terrain and spring. The closest period descriptions of the ruts as a visual feature appear in aggregate emigrant accounts rather than single attributed diary entries (see Notes).

## Composition references from period art

Period imagery of the Guernsey Ruts as an *artistic subject* is nearly nonexistent before Jackson's late-1860s photographs — the site was simply part of the trail, not yet understood as a historical monument. Jackson's photographs, by contrast, treat the ruts as a subject in their own right, and they establish the compositional convention: **low-angle, looking along the rut channels from near to far**.

The recommended FLUX composition draws from what the ruts demand rather than what period painters chose:

- **Camera at ground level, maybe 2–3 ft above the ridge surface**, looking along the rut channels toward the southwest. This is the viewpoint a teamster or footslogger would have — you're looking at the next 50 yards of ruts stretching away from you.
- **The rut channels converge in perspective toward a horizon-level vanishing point** — two narrow parallel grooves in pale stone, widening in the foreground and narrowing to a point in the far middle-ground. This is the compositionally strongest rendering of the depth.
- **The ox team and wagon are in the middle-ground**, seated in the channels, perhaps 30–50 yards ahead — small enough to convey the scale of the ridge, large enough to be readable as a loaded wagon under strain.
- **No sky-dominant composition** — unlike Chimney Rock (where sky is 50–60% of the frame), here the ridge surface and the ruts are the subject. Sky should be 30–40% at most, with horizon visible but not dominant.
- **Raking light is mandatory** — the ruts are invisible without directional light throwing shadows into the channel walls. Flat or overcast lighting turns the ridge into undifferentiated pale stone. All FLUX candidates generated under overcast lighting should be discarded.

## Variants needed in-game

- **Default 1843–1860 standard view** — ruts at 1.5–2.5 ft depth, ox team in channels, late afternoon raking light
- **Weather variants** — storm (thunderhead to north), rain (ruts slick and reflecting sky) — wired via existing `BackdropPainting` weather hook
- **No period gates required** — the ruts existed throughout the game window. The depth variation is a visual detail; a single "mid-period" depth is acceptable for all years.
- **Approach variant (optional)** — low-angle view from the river bottom looking *up* at the ridge, with the rut channels visible as notches in the ridgeline and a wagon team at the top. This is the "about to climb" moment and could serve as an approach parallax layer.

## What NOT to render

Modern photos and the current state park present a site that is substantially different from our 1843–1860 window:

- **Five-foot-deep channel walls** — the modern maximum depth of ~5 ft is the fully-accumulated 1869 terminal state. Our period renders should show 1.5–2.5 ft maximum depth. A viewer standing beside the ruts in 1850 would look down into them, not stand beside a stone corridor at chin height.
- **Wyoming State Park infrastructure** — paved walking path alongside the ruts, interpretive metal-and-wood shelter, modern safety fencing along the channel edges, parking area, concrete interpretive panels. None of this existed before 1932 when the site was first set aside, and none belongs in a period render.
- **Modern 5-ft-deep "canyon" aesthetic** — FLUX trained on modern site photos may default to the slot-canyon look, with the ruts reading as chest-deep gorges. This is wrong for our period and somewhat wrong even for 1869.
- **Cut stone walls looking like masonry** — the ruts are worn smooth, not cut clean. The channel walls are irregular, rounded, show weather staining and hub-scrape marks. They should not look like they were cut with a saw or a stonemason's chisel.
- **A single rut** — the defining feature is *two parallel channels*, not one. A single groove reads as a road cut, not wagon ruts.
- **Red or orange sandstone** — the Arikaree formation is pale buff tan, NOT red-orange like Utah canyon country. Models may default to "sandstone = red canyon" aesthetics which are completely wrong.
- **Trees on the ridge** — no trees grew on this exposed sandstone ridge. Cottonwoods only in the river bottom well to the north.
- **Dramatic cliff faces or canyon walls** — the ridge is LOW. There are no sheer vertical faces, no canyon-scale drops.
- **Modern road or highway** — a modern highway runs near the site; in period it was open prairie.
- **Cattle or barbed wire** — both postdate the emigrant period (barbed wire invented 1874).

## FLUX failure modes (specific to this landmark)

The Guernsey Ruts are the hardest FLUX target in the landmark set because **the model has no strong training concept for "wagon ruts as landscape subject."** Common failure modes:

1. **Model generates generic prairie** — no ruts visible at all, just rolling sage hills. Happens when the rut description is insufficiently prominent in the prompt.
2. **Model generates a single road-cut or erosion gully** — reads the "channel in stone" signal but misses the "two parallel" and "wagon-width-spaced" constraints. Result is a single ditch or arroyo.
3. **Model generates red canyon / slot-canyon aesthetic** — fires on "sandstone" + "deep channels" and generates Utah/Arizona-style red rock. Wrong formation, wrong depth, wrong color.
4. **Model generates 5-ft canyon walls** — generates the modern maximum-depth site, which is too deep for our period and too dramatic for what the site actually looks like.
5. **Flat-light failure** — ruts are invisible in flat or overcast lighting; model may generate convincing stone surface but no visible channels. Always specify raking directional light.
6. **Wagon in wrong orientation** — model may place the wagon driving *across* the ruts (perpendicular) rather than *along* them. The wagon must be seated *in* the rut channels, tracking down their length.

**Recommended mitigation:** Use the existing `GuernseyRutsArt.jsx` SVG art as the compositional anchor for FLUX iterations. The SVG establishes the correct perspective (along the rut channels from near to far) and the correct scale relationship (wagon small in middle-ground). Generate 8–12 FLUX candidates and filter ruthlessly against the twin-channels + correct-depth + raking-light checklist. Expect to discard 60–70% of candidates.

## Code recommendations

The existing `GuernseyRutsArt.svelte` component (rendered from `guernsey-ruts-art.jsx`) renders the SVG overlay showing the rut channels, wagon in distance, hub-scrape marks, and small carved names on the wall. If a raster backdrop is added, the SVG layer should be retained as the interactive overlay (carving event trigger, wagon-progress indicator) while the FLUX image provides the ambient background.

The depth progression noted above (ruts growing from ~1 ft in 1843 to ~5 ft by 1869) is not currently modeled in code — the landmark has no period variant in `landmarks.ts`. This is intentional: representing rut depth at game-year resolution would require per-year backdrop variants, which is out of scope. A single "circa 1850" depth (~2 ft) works for all game years with no period gate required. If depth variation ever becomes desirable, it would be a `backdropVariant: 'early' | 'late'` switch rather than a full period gate.

## Notes / open questions

- **Diary excerpts for Guernsey Ruts specifically are sparse.** Unlike Register Cliff (which prompted name-carving and therefore shows up in many diary entries as an event) or Independence Rock (explicitly mentioned as a July 4 goal), the Guernsey Ruts were simply part of the road — emigrants passed through and noted the hard pull, but rarely stopped to describe the visual impression of the ruts themselves. The most reliable contemporary description comes in aggregate form from period scholars citing the trail's progressive wear, not from a single diarist at this specific location. The three diary excerpts used in this document (Frizzell on the road surface, Johnson on the trail's deepening, Batchelder on the terrain context) are the closest available primary material; they describe the experience of the terrain without singling out the ruts as a visual subject.
- **Depth estimation for 1850 is uncertain.** The figure "1.5–2.5 ft at the deepest" for 1850 is an inference from (a) the modern 5-ft maximum accumulated over 28 years of traffic, (b) the documented peak-traffic years of 1849–1852 when ~75,000+ emigrants used the trail in a three-year window, and (c) the assumption of roughly linear (if traffic-weighted) wear over the 1841–1869 period. No period diary records a depth measurement. The renderer should treat the 1.5–2.5 ft figure as a reasonable working estimate, not a documented fact.
- **The "intentional cutting" by emigrants** is documented across multiple secondary sources (NPS, WyoHistory) but the mechanism is not fully clear — it may have involved emigrants using picks or axes to notch the stone and ease the grade, or it may simply refer to the lateral chipping that occurred when wagon frames scraped the rock walls. The renders do not need to depict active cutting; the worn appearance of the channels implies it.
- **Jackson photograph availability:** The Library of Congress items 2017685124 and 2017685129 are identified as photographs of the Oregon Trail ruts near Guernsey from the period. The implementer should pull these directly and use them as color and texture calibration sources before beginning FLUX iteration.
- **Register Cliff relationship:** The ruts and Register Cliff are 2 miles apart and share the same Arikaree sandstone formation. The sandstone color palette established for Register Cliff (`stone: "#dcc18c"`, `stoneSh: "#9a7e50"` in `register-cliff-art.jsx`) is the correct baseline for the ruts as well — the ruts are slightly more weather-stained in the channels (darker floor, slightly greyer walls) but the surrounding exposed surface matches.
- **Brigham Young** led the first Mormon pioneer company through this area in 1847, and the Martin Handcart Company of 1856 passed through later on the same route. These are not relevant to the visual rendering but are context for the landmark's game significance.
- **CCC structures (1930s)** in Guernsey State Park are historically significant in their own right (National Historic Landmark, 1997) but are completely anachronistic to our game period. The stone shelters, stone retaining walls, and formal walkways must never appear in renders.
