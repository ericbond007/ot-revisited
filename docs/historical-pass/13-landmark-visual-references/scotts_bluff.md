## 1. What It Is

Scotts Bluff is not a single pinnacle like Chimney Rock but a complex of massive eroded sandstone and siltstone bluffs rising roughly **800 feet above the North Platte River** (summit elevation 4,659 ft). Where Chimney Rock is a spire visible from 30 miles, Scotts Bluff is a small mountain range — a wall of cliff. It was the **second-most mentioned landmark** in all emigrant journals on the overland trails, exceeded only by Chimney Rock.

The bluff forced a routing decision for every wagon train: the badlands between the cliff face and the North Platte River were impassable. Before 1851, wagons swung south through Robidoux Pass; from 1851 on, they went through the gap in the cliff face itself.

### The Five Rock Sub-Formations

From east to west, the complex contains five named outcroppings:

| Rock | Elevation | Height above valley | Position | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dome Rock** | 4,396 ft | 180 ft | Easternmost, freestanding | Cylindrical — emigrants called it a tower, lighthouse, cathedral |
| **Crown Rock** | 4,557 ft | 333 ft | South Bluff group | Knobbed summit, accessible enough that men rolled rocks from it |
| **Sentinel Rock** | 4,390 ft | 166 ft | South side of the pass gap | Squat watchtower column; forms south abutment of Mitchell Pass |
| **Eagle Rock** | 4,482 ft | 258 ft | North side of the pass gap | Halved-dome profile; historically "Sugar Loaf Rock"; forms north abutment |
| **Saddle Rock** | 4,659 ft | 435 ft (780 ft above river) | Highest point, central mass | Narrow buttress between two thin spires; the saddle notch is visible at distance |

Sentinel Rock and Eagle Rock are the gateposts of Mitchell Pass. Dome Rock, Crown Rock, and Sentinel Rock belong to South Bluff. Eagle Rock and Saddle Rock are Scotts Bluff proper. The combined mass stretches roughly 1.5 miles east-west.

## 2. Geology and Color

The formation spans ~33 to 22 million years of Oligocene-Miocene sediment. From bottom to top:

- **Orella Member (Brule Formation)** — oldest exposed rocks; siltstones and mudstones, greenish-gray. Forms the badlands at the cliff base.
- **Whitney Member** — massive, **pinkish-brown** volcaniclastic siltstone with interbedded volcanic ash beds; gives the mid-section its warm ochre-orange tone.
- **Gering Formation** — **pale brown to gray-brown** fine-grained volcaniclastic sandstone; contains thin ash layers and disc-shaped sand crystals.
- **Monroe Creek-Harrison Formation** — **pale brown and light gray** sandstone caprock; hard lime-cemented ledges ("pipy" concretions) that resisted erosion and created the dramatic flat-topped summit profiles.

The net visual result: a layered, horizontally banded cliff face running tan/cream at the top, through warm brown and ochre in the middle band, to darker gray-green at the base where erosion gullies cut down. The Whitney Member's pinkish-brown siltstone produces a reddish-orange flush in afternoon light — different from Chimney Rock's purer tan. The caprock appears nearly white in direct sun.

Vertical erosion gullies (running from top to base) are prominent features. Pine and juniper dot the upper slopes — the formation is tall enough to support conifers, a real visual distinction from the treeless lower formations.

Richard Burton (1860) observed: "yellow layers of boulder and pebble imbedded in marly clay" with "gashings of the rain of ages." Alfred Jacob Miller (1837) drew the complex and described an "immense fortification with bastions, towers, battlements, embrazures, scarps and counterscarps."

## 3. The Approach and Why It Matters

### What Emigrants Saw Coming

Scotts Bluff was visible from a great distance — after the flat Platte corridor, the sudden appearance of a mountain-scale wall on the horizon was a shock. General Philip St. George Cooke (1845) wrote:

> "Looming afar over river and plain was 'Scott's Bluff,' a Nebraska Gibralter; surmounted by a colossal fortress and a royal castle, it jutted on the water."

Washington Irving's account (via Captain Bonneville, 1837) described "high and beetling cliffs of indurated clay and sandstone, bearing the semblance of towers, castles, churches and fortified cities." The fortress/castle/castle metaphor saturates the diary record — almost every pre-1850 traveler reached for the same architectural vocabulary. It is not Chimney Rock's lonely spire but a whole medieval skyline on the prairie.

Ansel McCall (1849, Robidoux Pass era) wrote of arriving at the area:

> "The scenery is very beautiful, presenting to the view mountains, hills and valleys in every direction, changing the outlook entirely from that which we had been so long accustomed to, and convinced us that we were in reality approaching the Rocky Mountains, so long talked of. I do not know when I have witnessed a more beautiful sight."

## 4. Hiram Scott — The Naming Legend

The bluffs were named almost immediately after 1828 for fur trapper **Hiram Scott** (c. 1805–1828), a clerk with William Henry Ashley's Rocky Mountain Fur Company. Scott died during the eastward journey from the summer rendezvous, apparently from illness, abandoned by his companions near the Platte River. His skeletal remains were found the following spring — miles from where he had been left, suggesting he had somehow dragged himself that distance before dying.

The account was first recorded by Warren A. Ferris in 1830 (Scott placed in a boat by two companions, then abandoned; skeleton found on the opposite bank). Washington Irving (1837) gave a more dramatic version involving starvation and a 60-mile crawl. Jason Lee (1834) claimed Scott traversed 100 miles. The exact truth is unknown, but the essential fact — man abandoned, died alone, bones found — was current among fur trade circles by 1830, and the bluffs bore his name by the time the emigration began.

Rufus B. Sage, passing in 1841, composed a poem that includes: *"His bones, alas, were left to bleach and moulder 'neath the sun! / The night-wolf howl'd his requiem,— the rude winds danced his dirge."*

The Scott legend was known to emigrants — it seeded the moody, atmospheric character of the landmark in the diary record. Where Chimney Rock inspired measurement and sketching, Scotts Bluff inspired poetry and gothic description.

## 5. The Two Routes — Critical Period Gate

### Pre-1851: Robidoux Pass (South Route)

Up to and including 1850, Robidoux Pass was used "almost exclusively by successive waves of fur traders, missionary parties, soldiers and emigrants" (historian Merrill Mattes). The pass lies approximately **9 miles south** of the North Platte River, cutting through the Wildcat Hills south of the bluff complex. Wagons had to swing well away from the river — a detour of roughly 8 miles compared to the later Mitchell Pass route — before rejoining the Platte corridor near Horse Creek.

The Robidoux Trading Post (#1095) sat at the mouth of this pass. The two stops were inseparable for pre-1851 emigrants: you came through Robidoux Pass and you stopped at Robidoux's shop.

Peter Decker, heading to California in late May 1849, wrote near the pass:

> "Passed a blacksmith shop even it looks sociable in this wilderness, from tip of bluff… I saw distinctly Laramie Peak and could distinguish the snow on its tops & sides, looks like a huge blue mound."

James Clyman (1844), camping in Robidoux Pass before the trading post existed, wrote:

> "encamped in the midtst of Scotts blufs By a cool spring in a romantic & picturisque vally surounded except to the E. by high & allmost impassably steep clay cliffs."

From the south (Robidoux Pass approach), emigrants saw the bluff complex as a distant massive wall — the full width of the formation visible across the sage plain, with Saddle Rock's notched summit line dominant. It is an expansive view: huge sky, flat prairie, the bluff mass filling the northern horizon.

### Mitchell Pass Opens: 1850-1851

The natural gap between Eagle Rock and Sentinel Rock — called "Devil's Gap" by travelers of the era — had been noted since at least 1841. Father Pierre-Jean De Smet, with the first emigrant wagon train in 1840-41, noted it: "We found a narrow passage through between two perpendicular cliffs 300 feet in height." But the gap floor was rough and filled with debris, and the badlands approach from the east made it impractical without road work.

Around 1850, a wagon road was graded through the gap — "widened and filled" in the NPS formulation. Who performed the work is not definitively documented; the likeliest candidate is soldiers from Fort Laramie or civilian road contractors. The passage was narrow — barely one wagon wide. **In 1851, travel was roughly equally divided between both routes. From 1852 onward, Mitchell Pass was heavily preferred.** The name "Mitchell Pass" came later still, after Fort Mitchell (1864) was named for Brigadier General Robert B. Mitchell; during the pioneer period, travelers called it "Devil's Gap" or simply "the gap."

William C. Lobenstine, traveling through in 1851, gave the canonical description:

> "The rock itself is separated nearly at its middle, having a pass here fifty to sixty feet wide, ascending at both sides perpendicular to a height of three to four hundred feet. The passage through here was only made possible in 1851 and is now preferred by nearly all emigrants, cutting off a piece of eight miles from the old road."

The visual experience changed completely. From the Robidoux Pass southern detour — a wide-open view of the bluff mass from the outside — emigrants now drove INTO the cliff. Sheer walls on both sides, the gap barely wide enough for a wagon, the sky reduced to a strip overhead. The dust raised by single-file wagon traffic in the narrow cut was notorious.

T. S. Kenderdine (1858, bullwhacker) described the approach from the east: "Tumbling pell-mell down narrow passages, slowly crawling over abrupt ascents, we at length reached the bottom…" William H. Jackson (1866) called it "one of the steepest and worst gulches to drive through that we have yet had." Helen Carpenter (1856) noted "sheer walls," many inscriptions carved by earlier emigrants into the clay faces, and soldier graves on the bluff — the walls had become a vertical record of the passage.

William A. Carter (1857) captured the traffic-jam reality of the narrow cut: a large freight wagon stopped in the pass with a broken axle, a dying ox blocking the road, its sounds "reverberated along the bluff" while "thousands of Ravens" hovered overhead — the sublime and the awful in one narrow gap.

## 6. William Henry Jackson

The Oregon Trail Museum at Scotts Bluff National Monument holds the world's most extensive collection of **William Henry Jackson** original sketches, paintings, and photographs. Jackson first passed through in 1866 as a young bullwhacker on his way to the Salt Lake basin; he sketched the landmarks obsessively and later became the most important visual documentarian of the American West. His paintings and sketches of Scotts Bluff — the pass, the approach, the cliff faces — are the primary visual record of what the landscape looked like when trail use was still recent.

For FLUX generation purposes, Jackson's work establishes: warm afternoon light on pale sandstone, dramatic vertical scale, sparse juniper on upper slopes, and the North Platte valley floor as context for the pre-pass approach shots.

## 9. FLUX Failure Modes

**Both states:**
- FLUX may default to Chimney Rock's spire shape — explicitly prompt "not a spire, a massive flat-topped cliff wall / bluff complex / mesa-like formation." The two formations are visually opposite.
- May merge both formations into generic "canyon" — emphasize the two named walls (Eagle Rock north, Sentinel Rock south) as distinct formations facing each other.

**State A (Robidoux Pass / distant view):**
- May place the wagon too close, eliminating the scale drama. Push scale language: "wagon very small, bluff filling the entire northern horizon."
- May add a lone spire in the foreground — reject. Scotts Bluff from the south has no prominent foreground rock formations.
- May render brown generic badlands rather than the stratified color palette. Emphasize "horizontally banded, pale cream caprock above warm ochre-pinkish middle, eroded gray-green base."

**State B (Mitchell Pass / inside the gap):**
- May make the gap too wide or give it canyon walls without the specific sandstone character — it should look like sandstone cliff, not red-rock desert.
- Overexposure of the sky strip: keep it bright but small — the drama is the walls, not the sky.
- May add water (a river) — there is no significant stream in the pass itself; the floor is dry and sandy with wagon ruts.
- Watch for anachronistic wagon types: prairie schooner / farm wagon with white canvas, NOT Conestoga.

## 10. Period Gate — Code Recommendation

**Recommendation: implement as a year-switched backdrop variant on the Scotts Bluff landmark, NOT as a separate landmark record.**

Scotts Bluff (#21 in landmarks.md) is one landmark with one map pin. What changes is the visual state of the backdrop image. Two approaches:

**Option A — Backdrop variant array (preferred):**

```typescript
// In landmark definition
backdropVariants: [
  {
    id: 'robidoux-pass',
    label: 'South approach via Robidoux Pass',
    activeBeforeYear: 1851,
    fluxPromptKey: 'scotts_bluff_pre1851',
  },
  {
    id: 'mitchell-pass',
    label: 'Through the gap (Devil\'s Gap / Mitchell Pass)',
    activeFromYear: 1851,
    fluxPromptKey: 'scotts_bluff_post1851',
  },
]
```

The game selects the variant based on `currentYear`. No separate landmark record needed, no routing code changes.

**Option B — Narrative flag:**
Add a `routeNote` field to the Scotts Bluff event that fires in 1851 specifically: "A new road has been cut through the gap in the bluffs. Your party takes the shorter passage." This makes the route switch a diegetic in-game moment rather than a silent backdrop swap. Could pair with State A → State B.

**Do NOT:**
- Add `abandonedBeforeYear: 1851` to anything — neither route is abandoned, they coexist briefly.
- Create a second landmark for "Mitchell Pass" as a separate stop — it is not a separate waypoint, it is the transit of the Scotts Bluff landmark.
- Add NPC-train parity logic per the route switch (NPC wagon trains should use the same year-gated variant as the player; confirm this during #298 audit pass).
- Assume the route switch affects the Robidoux Post (#1095) — it does not disappear in 1851; the post operated until 1852. Its landmark record can remain independently active.

**Game-AI note (#302):** The AI decision layer has no meaningful choice to make at this landmark — the route was geographically determined. No branching decision needed. If the game ever adds a "take the long south route for less dust / take the gap for the shorter road" choice as flavor, frame it as a minor morale/time tradeoff with no mechanical weight.

## 12. What Could Not Be Verified

- **Who opened Mitchell Pass:** NPS says "unknown, possibly soldiers from Fort Laramie." No surviving contract record or Army order has been located in publicly accessible sources. Do not state it was army-blasted in game text — leave it as "a road was cut through the gap."
- **Exact 1851 date of opening:** The transition is "1850 road graded, 1851 widely used" — no single opening day or event. The game can treat any year >= 1851 as Mitchell Pass era.
- **Alfred Jacob Miller's Scotts Bluff sketches:** Miller is documented visiting the area in 1837 with the Sublette/Stewart expedition but his Scotts Bluff-specific artwork is held at the Walters Art Museum (Baltimore) and not freely available online. The description above draws from secondary accounts of his work.
- **Specific J. Goldsborough Bruff Scotts Bluff quote:** Bruff passed through in July 1849 and his journals contain Scotts Bluff observations, but the full text requires the Huntington Library or Yale Beinecke manuscript — not available via open web fetch. His blacksmith shop/trading post observation appears through Mattes's compilation.
- **Frances Sawyer (1852) specific Scotts Bluff quote:** Her diary is referenced in OCTA records but not found in open-web transcription at time of research.
