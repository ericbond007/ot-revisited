## What the crossing actually looked like

The Green River at the Oregon Trail crossings was nothing like the slow muddy Prairie streams the emigrants had spent months wading. By the time a wagon train arrived — typically late June or early July — the river was running on mountain snowmelt from the Wind River Range to the north and east. The water was cold, clear, and blue-green in shade, not the brown opacity of the Kansas or the silty grey of the Platte. It ran fast enough to unsettle stock and tip a wagon within seconds of losing a wheel-hold. Period accounts consistently describe it as swift and deep; at crossing points, 4–5 feet of moving current was typical in high-water season.

The valley the Green River ran through was wide and open, a basin bounded by low sage-covered hills on both sides, nothing like the canyon geography the river enters downstream in Utah. The surrounding landscape was sagebrush steppe — semi-arid high desert at around 6,400 feet elevation — receiving only four to eight inches of rain a year. The river itself created a narrow green corridor: a cottonwood riparian zone perhaps fifty yards wide on each bank, the trees not large but providing the only shade for miles. Beyond the cottonwoods, the ground was pale sage grey-green, rolling away in gentle undulations toward the sage hills. The Wind River Range was visible to the northeast on clear days — a snow-capped wall of sawtooth granite peaks rising dramatically from the plains, forming the dominant backdrop. These were the mountains Alfred Jacob Miller knew as "the Mountains of the Wind," and when he painted the 1837 rendezvous encampment on the Green, he placed that white-peaked wall anchoring the composition's far distance.

At the crossing sites themselves — the Lombard Ferry location near Farson and the Robinson Ferry a few miles south — the river was wide enough that waiting emigrants could not simply wade their stock across at high water. The crossings operated along a gravel bar where the river was marginally shallower and less chaotic, but emigrants who slipped off this bar into the main channel often lost animals, cargo, and occasionally lives.

The campground on the eastern bank was the site of prolonged delay. A ferry could handle roughly 40 wagons a day; at peak migration in June and July, parties often waited two to four days for their turn. The camp grew large, crowded, and fouled quickly — the same problems as every waiting-camp on the trail, but with less grass and no shade except the cottonwood fringe. Axe-scarred trees and wagon ruts are still visible on the eastern shore of the New Fork River near the Lombard Ferry site. Multiple ferry sites developed specifically because the bottleneck at a single crossing was so severe; by the early 1850s, a traveler had genuine choices — Robinson Ferry a few miles south, Lombard/Mormon Ferry on the main trail, and Kinney and Slate Creek Cutoff ferries further north for those on the alternate routes.

## The fur-trade backstory: rendezvous country

The Green River valley carried deep significance before any emigrant set foot on it. From 1825 through 1840, this was the heart of the Rocky Mountain fur trade's annual rendezvous — a summer gathering where mountain men, traders, and thousands of Shoshone, Bannock, Flathead, and Nez Perce traded buffalo robes and beaver plews for powder, lead, cloth, and whiskey. William Henry Ashley launched the first rendezvous in 1825; there were sixteen of these gatherings. Horse Creek, flowing into the Green just north of the main emigrant crossing area, hosted the largest of them.

Alfred Jacob Miller attended the 1837 rendezvous as an artist hired by the Scottish adventurer Sir William Drummond Stewart. His watercolors and field sketches — the only known images made at the rendezvous by a trained artist — show the full sweep of the valley: the white lodges of two to three thousand assembled Indians stretching across the broad prairie plain, the river glittering in the middle distance, and the Wind River Mountains anchoring the far horizon. Miller described camping "under the shadows of the great spurs of Wind River Mountains" and found the gathering "an imposing, animated and unique sight." The same valley where emigrants would wait for ferries a decade later had been the center of the Western fur economy for fifteen years. By 1843 when the first emigrant wagons reached the Green, the rendezvous was already dead — the beaver trade had collapsed, the mountain men were reduced to ferry operators and guides — but the Shoshone still worked the river and knew its crossings as they always had.

## The crossing operators: a complicated succession

The ferry history at the Green River is one of the more tangled on the trail, and popular sources contain some errors. The following is the best-supported reconstruction.

**Pre-1847 — ford and improvisation only (for emigrants)**

The 1843 Great Migration and subsequent wagon parties through 1846 crossed the Green without a dedicated ferry on the main emigrant route. Mountain men in the region had various informal arrangements — ropes stretched across, guidance on the ford line, sometimes improvised rafts — but no commercial toll ferry operated for emigrant wagons at the Lombard site before 1843 in any documented way. Emigrants built their own rafts of cottonwood logs or floated wagon boxes like boats, swimming the stock across separately. High water could stop a company for days. James Clyman, who made the crossing in 1844, documented the extreme danger: water that could rise fast enough to swim a horse within an hour of seeming passable. In 1846, the year before the Mormon Ferry was established, several companies reported losing animals and cargo to the current.

**1843 onward — mountain man ferry at the Lombard site**

The so-called Lombard Ferry was established by mountain men around 1843 — the same year the first major emigrant migration reached the Green. This was the site that eventually took William Lombard's name in 1889, decades after the emigration. The mountain men who ran it were operating under the general umbrella of Jim Bridger's commercial interests in the region; Bridger and his associates controlled Fort Bridger and various river operations along the trail corridor in the 1840s. The ferry charged what the traffic would bear. Prices ranged from $3/wagon at low water to as high as $16/wagon when the river was running hard and emigrants had no alternative.

**1847 — Mormon Ferry established (Robinson site)**

On June 30, 1847, Brigham Young's Pioneer Company reached the Green River and spent the next several days crossing. The Church News daily summary of the 1847 trek notes that the vanguard company worked on the crossing from June 30 through July 4, when most men rested from the labor, and that Brigham Young then sent five men back east to the Green River ferry "with instructions for the Saints they would meet." The Robinson Ferry — established by Mormons in 1847 — was built to assist subsequent pioneer companies heading to the Salt Lake Valley and to earn commercial revenue from other emigrants traveling to Oregon and California. It operated until 1856, when it passed to Isaac Bullock and Lewis Robinson. The Robinson homestead was later attacked by Indians; the ferry was abandoned afterward.

**1850 — Mormons acquire the Lombard site**

In 1850, the mountain men sold the original crossing that would become Lombard Ferry to the Mormons, who operated it for eight years as the "Green River Mormon Ferry." This was partly a commercial move and partly a play to consolidate control of the Green River crossing economy. Tensions between Bridger's mountain man faction and the Mormons over commercial control of Fort Bridger and the river ferries were running high through the early 1850s. By 1858, the Mormon presence at the ferry ended with the Utah War.

**1850–1860s — multiple operators, intense competition**

At peak emigration, between three and five ferry operations ran simultaneously along the 30-mile Green River crossing corridor. The Robinson and Lombard sites served the main trail; the Kinney and Slate Creek Cutoff ferries, opened with those cutoffs in the early 1850s, served alternate routes. The Names Hill Ferry operated on the Sublette Cutoff crossing near La Barge. Competition suppressed prices somewhat, but the crossing remained expensive. The charge of "up to $16 almost the price of an ox" cited by Legends of America likely reflects peak high-water monopoly rates from years when only one ferry was operational. The more common range in the 1850s, with competition present, was $3–$8 per wagon.

## Three voices from the crossing

**Charlotte Allis, June 1854** — traveling west on the main trail, Allis recorded conditions at the Mormon/Lombard ferry site in unusual moral detail. Her diary, held at the American Heritage Center, University of Wyoming, reads:

> "[T]here is a very good ferry, and we crossed in safety. Moreover it is not very safe crossing certain times in the day, for instance after the ferrymen all get the worse for liquor, as I understand they do get in this condition, which is endangering people's lives. Yes, one poor man lost his life last season by the means of the miserable ferryman's getting drunk. He left a wife and a family of children in Calafornia to mourn his loss. The man had a very large stock of cattle going to Calafornia and in crossing the ferry the men frightened the stock which caused the boat to sink, and the man was drowned. Such is the effect of liquor, which is dreadful to think of. That must have been the agony of that wife and children."

**Wilford Woodruff, July 4, 1847** — in his journal entry from the Pioneer Company's crossing of the Green River, Woodruff recorded the unexpected joy of reunion with members of the Mormon Battalion on the far bank:

> "But to our great joy who should they be but our brethren belonging to the Mormon Battalion who volunteered into the service of the U.S.A. one year ago this month... When we met it was truly a hearty greeting and shaking of hands."

This entry captures the social character of the crossing as a meeting point — the Green River ferry was where news arrived. The Pioneer Company had worked four days to get across; arriving at the far bank meant communication with the world coming east. Brigham Young sent his lieutenants *back* to the Green River afterward to organize the crossing for the thousands of Saints who would follow.

**James Clyman, 1844** — mountain man, veteran of Ashley's fur-trade brigades, traveling east in 1844 after guiding a California-bound company west. His diary records the Green River at flood, documenting how fast and completely the crossing could become impossible:

> "with the utmost exertion we crssed over 20 waggons by about 10 o'clock when the waters became too deep to cross and in about an hour it rose so as to swim a horse."

This is the pre-ferry crossing in its essence: a race against rising water, improvised crossing techniques, and the mountain-fed flood that could overtake a ford inside an hour.

## The Shoshone bull-boat option

The code's `nativeFerry` entry — Shoshone bull-boat, paid in beads — is supported by the general pattern of Shoshone river assistance to emigrants throughout the trail period, though specific documentary evidence for bull-boats at the Green River crossing (as opposed to other Snake River and Columbia crossings) is harder to pin down. The Green River valley had been Shoshone and Bannock territory through the rendezvous era; the Eastern Shoshone under Washakie maintained friendly relations with emigrant parties across the 1840s–1860s. The bull-boat design — three or more buffalo hides sewn together and stretched over a willow frame — was documented in use by Plains tribes throughout this region. Sergeant Pryor's Lewis and Clark–era account described boats "7 feet 3 inches in diameter and 16 inches deep," circular, with the hide drawn tight over crossed willow ribs and secured at the outer hoop. One person could carry the finished hull. A skilled operator could move a bull-boat "very fair speed" and pass through rough water without taking water.

The Shoshone presence at the Green River crossings during emigration season is documented in John Mathias's June 1850 diary: "Stoped at green River Saboth day June 2d Saw a few Indians" — the kind of casual contact that preceded barter. The code's detail that six strings of beads was the going rate for a bull-boat crossing reflects the period bead trade economy accurately: beads were among the most reliable high-value trade goods the Shoshone accepted from emigrants.

**Confidence level on the bull-boat specifically:** Moderate. The general Shoshone river assistance pattern is well-documented. Bull-boats are documented in this geographic and cultural zone. The specific three-hide construction cited in the code's blurb is consistent with period ethnographic accounts. What is NOT verifiable from available sources is a specific diary entry saying "we crossed the Green River in a Shoshone bull-boat" with a named diarist and date. The code comment cites "Sage 1846, Frizzell 1852" — Rufus Sage's 1846 published account covers his 1841–1843 travels in the Rocky Mountains (he was not on the trail in 1846); Frizzell's 1852 journal ends before reaching the Green River. These specific attributions should be treated with caution. The bull-boat crossing is historically plausible but the cited diary attributions may be garbled secondary sourcing. Dave should note this gap if period-gate precision matters.

## The period gate: ferry availability 1847+

**Recommendation: implement a hard period gate for ferry availability at 1847.**

Before the 1847 Pioneer Company established the Robinson Ferry, no formal toll ferry served the main emigrant crossing at the Lombard/Robinson site. Mountain men ran an informal crossing as early as 1843, but "established by mountain men in 1843" describes a proto-commercial operation, not a dedicated emigrant ferry. For game purposes:

- **1843–1846:** Ford or improvised raft only. The mountain-man crossing existed but was informal, unreliable, and not documented as a paid toll ferry with consistent operation. Dangerous high water; loss of stock and cargo common.
- **1847+:** Mormon Ferry (Robinson site) operates; Lombard site mountain-man/Mormon ferry operates. Multiple competing operators throughout the 1850s. The $8 in-game ferry price is within the documented range ($3–$16) and reasonable as a mid-period rate.

The Shoshone bull-boat option has no documented start date and can be available throughout the game window (1843–1860).

## What NOT to render

- Modern Green River city (Sweetwater County seat, 40 miles downstream) — that town grew after the transcontinental railroad (1868), not on the emigrant crossing sites
- Modern bridges — no bridge existed at the main crossing sites during the emigrant period
- John Wesley Powell's 1869 Green River Canyon expedition — that begins at the modern Green River city and enters the canyon country far downstream and south; it postdates the emigration window entirely and is visually nothing like the sage-basin crossing sites
- Flaming Gorge or canyon scenery — the Oregon Trail crossings were in the flat open upper basin, not the canyon sections downstream
- A slow prairie stream — the Green was cold, clear, mountain-fed, and visibly fast; this is not the Platte

### Prompt notes

- The river color (blue-green, clear) is the key differentiator from prairie crossings — the Green at this elevation in late summer runs clear snowmelt, not the muddy brown of lowland rivers; FLUX should emphasize this
- Wind River Range backdrop is load-bearing — the mountains make this crossing visually distinct from every other river crossing on the trail; include in the composition
- The ferry flatboat is wooden planks lashed on log floats, rope-guided; no steam, no modern fittings
- Waiting wagons on the east bank reflect the documented 2–4 day queue at peak season
- Sage steppe (not grassland, not forest) — the Green River basin here is high cold desert
- Late afternoon light: the shadows angle long from the west, catching the western faces of wagon canvas and the river surface

## Relationship to nearby landmarks

**Parting of the Ways (mile ~970, 5 miles east):** The game's previous landmark. Emigrants who took the Fort Bridger route arrived at the Green River crossing from the southeast; those who took the Sublette Cutoff hit the Sublette crossing near Names Hill, 25 miles north. The code places green_river as 50 miles from South Pass — this puts it on the Fort Bridger branch, not the Sublette Cutoff. The Sublette Cutoff crossings (Names Hill area) were a different set of ferry sites entirely and should not be conflated visually or historically with the Lombard/Robinson sites.

**Fort Bridger (mile ~1025, ~50 miles west):** The next game landmark. After crossing, emigrants followed Black's Fork southwest to Bridger's post. The terrain between the Green River crossing and Fort Bridger was more varied — less pure sagebrush steppe, with Black's Fork cottonwood bottomland — but the immediate post-crossing landscape was still sage and low hills.
