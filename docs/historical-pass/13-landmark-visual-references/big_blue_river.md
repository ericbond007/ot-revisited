## What the crossing actually looked like

The Big Blue River at Independence Crossing was not a dramatic torrent in the Hollywood sense. At low water, the ford was passable but still formidable: the river cut steeply through Kansas glacial till, leaving near-vertical clay banks on both sides. Emigrants had to excavate or chain-lower wagons down the eastern bank and winch or double-team them up the western side. The channel itself ran perhaps 200 yards wide in flood, narrower at low water, with a slow to moderate current except in full flood.

The surrounding landscape at mile 175 was classic tallgrass prairie broken by a cottonwood-oak bottomland along the river course. Bur oak, cottonwood, beech, and sycamore grew along the creek draws feeding the Big Blue. East of the crossing the prairie rolled in long swells, the grass in late May standing a foot or more. The river valley cut below the prairie level; approaching the crossing from the east, a wagon train would have descended into this timber-lined valley after days of open grassland. The shift from treeless plain to wooded riverbank was a visual marker that the crossing was near.

Edwin Bryant, traveling with the Donner-Reed party in May 1846, described the bottomland vegetation around Alcove Spring (5 miles upstream) as including "cottonwood, oak, beech, and sycamore trees" alongside "a clear brook [that] rushed over its stony bed to join the Big Blue." The vegetation at Independence Crossing itself would have been similar — a narrow ribbon of timber hugging the river between open prairie slopes.

At the ford itself, emigrants camped on the grassy east bank while they waited, using the bottomland timber for firewood and raft-building.

## The waiting character

This crossing's defining quality was the wait. The Big Blue drained a large Kansas watershed, and spring storms filled it fast. In 1846, George McKinstry — traveling with the Donner-Reed party — recorded on May 26: *"the rains had raised the Stream some 20 feet and are now waiting for it to fall."* By the next morning, the water had dropped only fifteen inches.

Edwin Bryant, also traveling May 28, 1846, wrote: *"The River having fallen only fifteen inches during the night, after breakfast the whole party capable of performing duty were summoned to repair to a point on the river about half a mile above us, to assist in the construction of a raft to ferry our wagons over the stream."* The party hollowed two cottonwood trunks — each "about three and a half feet in diameter" — into dugout canoes 25 feet long, lashed them together, and rolled wagons across, poling against the current. Even at this reduced level, Bryant noted: *"On account of the rapidity of the current, and the great weight of the wagons, much difficulty was experienced."*

Virginia Reed, then thirteen, wrote to her cousin in Illinois that summer: *"we came to the blue — the Water was so hye we had to stay thare 4 days — in the mean time gramma died."* The casual run-on captures how closely death and delay were braided at this crossing.

A company that arrived at the ford after rain could expect to find other trains already camped on the east bank, oxen loose in the bottomland grass, wagons drawn up in semicircles, campfires laid against the riverbank timber. In heavy traffic years (1849–1852), the wait compounded: emigrants arriving at a flooded Big Blue might find dozens of other wagons already queued ahead of them. The Kansas State Historical Society estimated 75,000 people crossed Marshall County's Big Blue crossings between 1846 and 1856 — most of them in May and June.

## Sarah Keyes and the first Donner death

On the morning of May 29, 1846, Sarah Handley Keyes died at the Independence Crossing camp. She was 70 years old, the mother of Margaret Reed, mother-in-law of James F. Reed. She had been suffering from consumption (tuberculosis) for some time and had insisted on making the journey despite being in poor health, hoping to reach Fort Hall and reunite with a son who had settled in Oregon.

She did not see the crossing. She died on the east bank, still waiting for the river to drop.

McKinstry noted in his diary: *"Mrs Keyes the mother of the wife of Mr Reed of Illinois died of consumption aged 70."* Bryant recorded: *"Last night Mrs. Sarah Keyes, a lady aged 70, a member of the family of Mr. J.H. Reed of Illinois...died."* The funeral was held that afternoon at 2 o'clock. Bryant wrote that "nearly every man, woman, and child of the company" attended.

A cottonwood was felled for the coffin planks. The grave was dug on a sloping hill at the foot of an oak tree, perhaps a quarter-mile from both the springs and the ford — on the eastern bank, not the western. Virginia Reed, in her 1891 memoir, described *"a coffin hewn out of a cottonwood tree"* and the grave *"under the shade of an oak."* The men of the party "carved with deep letters the name Sarah Keyes" on a stone shaped into a headstone. The inscription: *Mrs. Sarah Keyes, Died May 29, 1846; Aged 70.* Reverend J.A. Cornwall read prayers and gave a funeral discourse. The young men of the party *"soded it all ofer and put flores on it."*

Virginia's 1846 letter to her cousin described the burial: *"We buried her verry decent. We made a nete coffin and buried her under a tree we had a head stone and had her name cut on it and the date and yere verry nice, and at the head of the grave was a tree we cut some letters on it."*

This grave was subsequently visited and mentioned by later emigrants passing the same crossing. It was one of the more noted trail graves in Kansas — not because it was famous yet (the Donner Party's fate was still in the future) but because it was freshly marked, prominent, and near a crossing where many parties paused. By 1930, a researcher found the headstone still standing but all markings obliterated; the oak had become three trees growing from the same stump.

**Note on myth vs. record:** Secondary sources sometimes conflate the cottonwood (coffin material) with the grave tree. The primary accounts are clear: coffin was cottonwood, grave was under an oak. Virginia Reed's 1846 letter mentions letters cut on the grave tree; exact wording unknown. The KHS 1930 survey found only the stone headstone still standing; no legible tree carving remained. Treat tree-inscription specifics as plausible-but-unverified.

## The ferry question: period gate

### Pre-1849: ford only

From 1843 through 1848, the Independence Crossing had no ferry. Companies built their own rafts or found low-water fords. The 1846 Donner-Reed raft — two lashed cottonwood dugouts, self-built in a day — was the standard approach. Some later parties found the water low enough to simply wade wagons across.

### 1849–1853: Marshall's ferry at Independence Crossing

Frank J. Marshall of Weston, Missouri, received permission from the Fort Leavenworth military authorities and the Indian agent to establish a ferry and trading post at Independence Crossing in 1849. He operated here through roughly 1853. The ferry took three wagons per trip; fees ran $3–5 per wagon depending on year. The KHS record suggests this ferry operated but was not heavily documented in emigrant journals — raising some uncertainty about exactly when it was in routine operation vs. when emigrants self-ferried.

### 1851 onward: the upper (Marysville) crossing opens

In 1851, after Lieutenant Howard Stansbury surveyed a better route, the government opened a military road crossing the Big Blue six miles north of Independence Crossing at what became Marysville. Marshall established a second ferry there, and by 1852 the upper crossing had become "the favorite one with the traveling public." From 1851 onward, the traffic divided between the two points; after 1853 most emigrant traffic used Marysville.

### Implication for game implementation

The game landmark at mile 175 is the Independence Crossing (lower crossing). A period gate makes historical sense:

- **1843–1848:** Ford only; no ferry option. Raft-building event or simple ford.
- **1849–1853:** Marshall's ferry operates at this location. $3–5/wagon fee.
- **1854+:** Most traffic has shifted to Marysville. The Independence Crossing still exists but is less favored. Showing a ferry at this landmark after ~1853 is historically questionable; showing the Marysville crossing as a separate landmark would be more accurate.

The in-game $2 ferry fee is on the low end of attested rates. Marshall charged $3 initially, later varying by year and load. $2 is plausible as an early-season low-water rate but should be considered approximate. The $5 figure cited in some sources appears to be the initial high-water rate.

## What NOT to render

- Modern bridges or paved crossings — Marysville today has a modern bridge; do not include
- Dramatic rushing rapids — the Big Blue at low water was slow; the drama was the banks, not the water
- "Hollywood ford" — wagons driving boldly through churning white water; the party was waiting *because* that's what would happen
- A signposted or formal cemetery — the Keyes grave was an informal trailside burial under a tree, with a hand-shaped stone
- Modern Marysville buildings or streets
- The ferry at the wrong crossing — Marysville's ferry was upstream; Independence Crossing had the first, earlier ferry

### Prompt notes

- The lone oak with the fresh mound is the Keyes grave — present but not labeled or foregrounded; a background story beat, not a focal object
- Oxen are unyoked and loose in the bottomland grass — the party has been here at least a day; this is the *waiting* scene, not the crossing scene
- The steep clay bank is a required visual element — it should be visible as a near-vertical drop, not a gentle slope to the water
- Slow brown river, not a rushing torrent
- Driftwood in the current signals recent flooding without dramatizing it
- Overcast sky is period-appropriate for May Kansas and keeps the light soft, avoiding the heroic-orange Bierstadt look

## Alcove Spring relationship

Alcove Spring (mile ~170, 5–6 miles north) is treated as a separate landmark in the game. The two are historically linked — emigrant parties camped at the Spring while waiting for the Blue to drop, then moved downstream to the ford. The Donner-Reed party named Alcove Spring and buried Sarah Keyes in the same 1846 layover.

Visual distinction: Alcove Spring is intimate — a limestone ledge with a 10-foot waterfall into a clear pool, enclosed by fern and moss, stones carved with names. The Independence Crossing is wide, open, exposed. The drama is scale and the steep clay banks. Prairie sky dominates. Do not share a backdrop between them; they are 5 miles apart and visually very different.
