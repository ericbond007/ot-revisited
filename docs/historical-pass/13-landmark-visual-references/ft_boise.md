**Historical site:** Near Parma, Canyon County, Idaho — confluence of the Boise River and the Snake River

**Code gate:** No `abandonedAfterYear` set in game code — see note below.

## Why this landmark is visually distinct

Fort Boise is not impressive. That is the point. Every other major HBC post on the trail — Fort Hall, Fort Laramie — has bulk, walls, bustle, commerce. Fort Boise is the far end of the supply chain at the western extreme of the Snake River desert: a small mud-brick rectangle planted in a wash of gray-green sagebrush at a river confluence, a Union Jack snapping in the wind, a kitchen garden, a few Hawaiian workers, some drying salmon racks, and indigenous camps ringing it at a distance. Emigrants who had just slogged 130 waterless miles from Three Island Crossing expected relief and got modesty. The visual has to read as both a lifeline (cottonwood shade, river water, a British flag meaning safety) and a disappointment (low walls, one gate, no parade ground grandeur).

## Period states

### State 1: 1834–1837 — McKay's log post (pre-game, context only)

Thomas McKay — HBC Chief Factor John McLoughlin's stepson, veteran of the annual Snake Country brigades — built the original post in fall 1834 at the Boise River's mouth as a private venture to compete with Nathaniel Wyeth's newly built American Fort Hall sixty miles to the east. The first structure was a simple log construction on a site previously occupied by Donald Mackenzie's 1819 North West Company cache. This early post is pre-trail-era and does not need a game rendering. By 1836 HBC had taken full control and by 1838 it had been completely rebuilt in adobe.

### State 2: 1838–1854 (PRIMARY GAME STATE) — Adobe fort at the confluence

This is the fort as emigrants 1843–1854 encountered it. In 1838 François Payette, who had managed the post since 1835, built a replacement fort near the exact confluence of the Boise and Snake Rivers, approximately five miles northwest of present-day Parma, Idaho, and south of Nyssa, Oregon.

**Construction:** Built in the form of a parallelogram approximately 100 feet per side, originally surrounded with a stockade of poles 15 feet high; the poles were subsequently covered and replaced with sun-dried adobe bricks. By the 1840s it was a fully adobe structure.

**Walls:** The 1851 HBC inventory — filed as part of the company's post-Oregon Treaty claim against the United States — recorded approximately 400 linear feet of adobe wall enclosing the establishment, walls 12.5 feet high and 1.5 feet thick. This is consistent with a roughly square footprint (~100 ft per side). The adobe is sun-dried brick, not fired ceramic, meaning it was earthen-tan to buff in color, with a slightly eroded, handmade surface texture. Unplastered mud brick in a desert setting weathers to a warm ochre-sand hue, mottled by moisture staining near the base where spring floods periodically crept.

**Bastions:** The 1851 HBC inventory specifically mentions a bastion on the northeast corner. Period forts of this type (compare Fort Hall, also HBC Snake Country) typically had two bastions at diagonally opposite corners for crossfire coverage. Most sources describe blockhouses or bastions at the corners — the exact count is uncertain, but two (northeast and southwest, or northwest and southeast) is the standard HBC pattern. Each bastion would project slightly from the main wall, square-plan, slightly taller than the wall line, with loophole openings. They are small — a few feet on a side — not large towers. Do not render them as imposing fortification.

**Gate:** Single gate, almost certainly on the side facing the river approach (the east or south face, toward the Boise River confluence). A flagpole with the HBC's Union Jack — white field with Union Jack in the canton, distinctive from a plain British flag — would have flown above or beside the gate. The HBC flew its company flag at all interior posts.

**Interior structures:** The 1851 HBC inventory for Fort Boise lists: one adobe main dwelling (42 × 15 feet), Indian housing, residential quarters, kitchen, milk house, and two bastions. This is a sparse interior — a single elongated dwelling block and service rooms. No parade ground or internal fort yard comparable to Fort Laramie's scale. Think of it as a small farmstead enclosed in adobe walls rather than a military installation.

**Garden:** By 1846 the fort had two tilled acres, 27 cattle, and 17 horses. The garden was irrigated — a rarity in the high desert and noted by several emigrants — and produced vegetables (the exact crops are not consistently documented, but peas, potatoes, and garden greens are consistent with other HBC posts). The garden would lie outside the main walls, on the river-bottom soil nearest the Boise River.

**Cattle:** A small herd grazed the river bottom near the fort. By the 1840s the fur trade had declined so severely that the fort's economic base had shifted toward the emigrant resupply trade and the Snake River salmon fishery. Oxen, horses, and a few trade cattle are period-consistent.

### State 3: Post-1853 flood damage (context only — no separate rendering needed)

A major spring flood in 1853 destroyed much of the fort. The site was only partially rebuilt. By 1855 HBC had abandoned it entirely, moving remaining stock and personnel north toward Flathead country. The Ward Massacre of August 1854 — in which Shoshone fighters killed 19 emigrants within a few miles of the fort — accelerated the decision. The physical site was eventually reclaimed by the Snake River; the modern state historical marker and wildlife management area near Parma, Idaho, are several miles from where the adobe walls once stood.

## Site geography and terrain

**Elevation:** ~2,100 feet above sea level. The Snake River Plain at this point is in the high arid basin — broad, flat, and relentlessly exposed.

**The confluence:** The Boise River, draining the mountains to the east, runs roughly west before turning southwest to join the Snake. At the confluence the two rivers merge in a braided floodplain with some gravel bars and willow tangles. The Snake itself is wide and swift here — emigrants who needed to ford it described it as broad and deep, crossing near 400 yards wide, with water deep enough to swim the smaller cattle. Frémont in 1843 noted that the Snake was "broad and deep" at Fort Boise and that his party required their boat and two borrowed canoes to cross.

**The desert surround:** Immediately beyond the river-bottom cottonwood and willow fringe, the terrain is classic Snake River Plain sagebrush steppe: big sagebrush (*Artemisia tridentata*) on sandy-loam volcanic soil, mixed with bunchgrass (bluebunch wheatgrass), occasional rabbitbrush, and sparse forbs. Emigrants had been crossing this landscape since Fort Hall — 240 miles of "wormwood barrens," as some diaries put it. By the time they reached Fort Boise they were grinding through monotony, their oxen thin, their wagons dry-cracked and rattling. The sagebrush here was not the compact low variety; it grows knee- to waist-high in this region, gray-green and pungent.

**Color palette:**
- Desert floor: `#c4b89a` (dry sand-loam), `#a8976e` (alkali-crusted flats closer to the river)
- Sagebrush: `#8b9e7a` (gray-green tops), `#6b7a5a` (shadowed lower), `#7a7a5c` (dry-season silver-sage)
- Adobe walls: `#c8a96e` (buff-tan sunlit face), `#a88c52` (shadowed wall, weathered), `#8c7440` (flood-stained base)
- Cottonwoods along river: `#6b9a4e` (summer green canopy), `#8cc96e` (bright new growth in spring — most emigrant arrivals are late August-September so summer green is correct)
- Snake River water: `#5a8c9a` (deep channel, silted), `#7ab5c4` (shallows over gravel)
- Sky: high-desert cloudless or scattered cumulus; the Snake River Plain is not a mountains-blocking-the-sky scene; it is wide open sky

**The Blue Mountains:** Emigrants and period accounts confirm that the Blue Mountains were visible to the west from the Fort Boise area on clear days. They appear as a low, dark blue-purple ridge on the horizon — not dramatic peaks like the Rockies, but a forested upland rising from the plain. Including them as a distant horizon feature is historically correct and visually important: they represent both the next major obstacle and the promise of Oregon. Their silhouette: `#4a5e7a` to `#3a4e6a` (blue-gray ridge under western haze).

## People and activity visible at the fort

**HBC staff:** François Payette managed the fort 1835–1844; James Craigie (sometimes spelled "Craggie") succeeded him and held the position into the 1850s. James Nesmith's 1843 diary describes Payette as "a very agreeable old French gentleman" who had been in the country since 1810. Thomas J. Farnham, visiting in 1839, described him as "a merry, fat old gentleman of fifty" who seated guests immediately and served them with "every mark of kindness." By the 1850 season, the post was staffed by Craigie and fourteen "Owyhees" — Hawaiian workers who had been recruited into HBC service (the name "Owyhee" is a phonetic rendering of "Hawaii" in the pronunciation of the period and is the origin of Owyhee County and the Owyhee River). These workers were skilled swimmers and laborers who handled domestic duties, gardening, and river work.

**Visual staffing:** The total European/Canadian-background HBC presence at any time was one to two named factors plus a small number of Hawaiian laborers. Do not render this as a busy trading-post scene; it is quiet, understaffed, and remote. Perhaps four to six visible individuals at any time — one European factor figure, two or three Hawaiian workers, and whatever indigenous visitors are present.

**Native camps:** The fort consistently had Bannock and Shoshone people camped at close range, and sometimes Nez Perce. Nesmith's 1843 diary describes seeing native people coming daily to sell dried salmon in the days approaching Fort Boise along the Boise River: "Indians were in camp that evening; they had been seen for the last four or five days, coming daily to sell dried salmon." The natives present are the salmon fishermen of the Snake River — the "Snake Tribe" (Northern Shoshone and Bannock bands) who had established fishing stations at the salmon falls to the east. Small camp groupings — tipis or brush shelters — ringed the fort at a short distance.

**Salmon drying racks:** This is the most evocative visual detail. The Snake River salmon run was abundant enough that by the time emigrants arrived (late August–September), dried salmon was the primary trade commodity available at and near Fort Boise. Fish racks — simple frames of peeled willow or cottonwood poles with salmon split and hung to dry in the sun and wind — would be visible near the native camps and possibly near the fort's trade area. Farnham (1839) described being feasted with "excellent bread, and butter made from an American cow, obtained from some of the missionaries; with baked, boiled, fried, and broiled salmon" — indicating that salmon was prepared in multiple forms and was a dietary staple at the post.

## What emigrants actually found

The historical record is consistent: Fort Boise was a welcome sight after the Snake River desert, but a modest one. The combination of genuine HBC hospitality (Payette and Craigie both earned strong praise in emigrant journals) and objectively limited trade stock meant that emigrants experienced gratitude mixed with disappointment.

**James Nesmith, 1843 emigrant diary** (Great Migration):
> "Haggard and myself went to Fort Boise ahead of the wagons; distance ten miles. The wagons arrived in the afternoon. The wind blowing very hard from the Northwest made it impossible to ford the river, as the swells rolled very high, and they encamped for the night just below the fort. [We visited] Monsieur Payette, the commandant; found him a very agreeable old French gentleman, and has been in this country, in the fur trade, since 1810."
> — James Nesmith, *Diary of the Emigration of 1843*, Oregon Historical Quarterly vol. 7 (1906)

**Thomas J. Farnham, 1839 (pre-emigration-era visitor):**
> "He feasted us with excellent bread, and butter made from an American cow, obtained from some of the missionaries; with baked, boiled, fried, and broiled salmon."
> — Thomas J. Farnham, *Travels in the Great Western Prairies* (1843), describing François Payette's hospitality

**John C. Frémont, September 1843:**
> "We came suddenly in sight of the broad green line of the valley of the *Rivière Boisée* (wooded river)... a beautiful rapid stream, with clear mountain water, and, as the name indicates, well wooded with some varieties of timber — among which are handsome cottonwoods."
> — Frémont, *Report of the Exploring Expedition to the Rocky Mountains* (1845), describing arrival at the Boise River approach to the fort

> "We were received with an agreeable hospitality by Mr. Payette, an officer of the Hudson's Bay Company."
> — ibid., describing Fort Boise itself

**Joel Palmer, September 1845:**
> "The road crosses the [Snake] river; the ford is about four hundred yards below the fort, and strikes across to the head of an island, then bears to the left to the southern bank; the water is quite deep, but not rapid; it swam some of our smallest work cattle; the bottom is solid and smooth."
> — Joel Palmer, *Journal of Travels Over the Oregon Trail in 1845* (1847)

These quotes give the visual texture: the sudden green of the cottonwood-lined river valley emerging from desert, the low adobe walls, the hospitable but modest HBC factor, the practical problem of a deep river ford with swimming cattle, and the salmon as both food and trade commodity.

## What the fort was NOT

The most dangerous research error for this landmark is confusing the HBC Fort Boise with the later US Army Fort Boise built in 1863 approximately 40 miles to the east, near present-day Boise city.

**Do not use:**
- Any imagery of the 1863+ Army post: brick/sandstone construction, multi-building parade ground, flagpole with American colors, officers' quarters, barracks, any US military insignia
- The modern VA hospital / Boise Barracks / Veterans Home complex — this is the Army post's later incarnation
- The modern city of Boise, Idaho — it grew around the 1863 Army installation, not the HBC post
- Any "Fort Boise" imagery that shows a large stockaded American frontier fort — that is the wrong fort entirely
- Photographs labeled "Fort Boise" without verification that they are the Parma-area site — most image searches surface the 1863 Army post because it has more surviving documentation

**Also do not use:**
- The modern Parma replica — it is a steel-and-stucco interpretation built for tourism, not a historically calibrated reconstruction
- Fort Hall imagery as a substitute — Fort Hall was also HBC, also adobe, but it was larger and more substantial; Fort Boise was the smaller western outpost

## Period visual documentation

**Major Osborne Cross, 1849:** The single most important period visual record. Cross commanded the supply train on the first US military expedition to travel the full Oregon Trail (Regiment of Mounted Riflemen, 1849). His report to the Quartermaster General included a lithograph titled "Fort Boise showing exterior walls, Snake River near the mouth of the Boise River, Idaho, 1849," held in the University of Washington digital collections. The lithograph shows the exterior adobe walls from an exterior viewpoint — the fort as an approaching emigrant would see it: low, rectangular, sun-bleached walls in a flat desert setting with the river visible nearby. This is the primary period visual source. The fort reads as small and flat against the desert horizon — not a dramatic fortification but a modest mud-brick enclosure. The Cross lithograph should be the first reference any artist checks.

**No known period paintings or sketches of the interior** are documented in the secondary literature. Interior reconstructions should be built from the 1851 HBC inventory: a 42×15-foot main dwelling block and support structures, not imagined as spacious or well-appointed.

### Compositional notes

- The dominant visual impression should be **horizontal flatness** — desert floor, low walls, flat sky. Unlike Fort Laramie (dramatic bluffs, parade ground activity), Fort Boise is a low shape on a flat plain.
- The **cottonwood ribbon** is the strongest visual feature beyond the fort itself — a bright green stripe against dust-tan, following the river. This is what emigrants saw first and described as a welcome sight.
- The **Snake River** is wide, silted, and important to include — it establishes the crossing challenge and the geographic context. The Boise River entering from the east creates a visible Y or V of water in the foreground or middle ground.
- **Salmon drying racks** near native camp in the middle distance are historically correct and visually distinctive. They read as thin vertical poles with horizontal fish silhouettes hung in rows.
- The **Union Jack** at the gate is a critical identifying detail — it tells the viewer this is British territory (or was, in the joint-occupation years) and marks it unmistakably as an HBC post. After 1846 the Oregon Treaty made this nominally US territory, but HBC continued flying its company flag under the treaty's property rights provisions.
- **Scale correction:** The fort should look small relative to the surrounding landscape. A wagon train pulling up to this fort should make the fort look like a farmstead, not a citadel. Bierstadt-scale romance is wrong here.
- **Avoid:** Any hint of a large stockaded military fort, any US flag or American military elements, any suggestion of a populous trading center. The staff was small, the walls were low, the stock was modest.

### Variant: late-season (September) light

Most emigrants arrived in August–September. The light should be:
- High desert afternoon — strong, bleaching sunlight from the south-southwest
- Long shadows from the low bastions and gate post
- Dust haze in the middle distance — September is dry season; any wagon or cattle movement raises visible dust
- The cottonwood leaves beginning to show early-fall yellow at their tips (`#d4b84a` hints)

### Variant: flood damage / 1853–1855 period

Not needed as a primary rendering but useful if a "ruined" state is eventually coded:
- One wall section partially collapsed, revealing adobe brick cross-section
- Water-stained base, debris line at ~3 feet showing past flood level
- Gate sagging on one hinge
- Flag absent (post abandoned)
- Salmon racks and native camps absent; the Ward Massacre context = no native trade at this point

## Game implementation notes

### `abandonedAfterYear` recommendation

**Strongly recommend adding `abandonedAfterYear: 1855` to the `ft_boise` landmark definition.** The historical reality is unambiguous: the 1853 flood critically damaged the fort and the 1854 Ward Massacre made the site untenable; HBC formally abandoned it in 1855. The current code renders it as active for any party starting 1843–1860, which means an 1857 or 1860 start would encounter a fort that had been a ruin for years. This is a meaningful historical error and the fix is one line of code. The rendering should switch to a simplified `kind: 'landmark'` shell for years 1856+, similar to how `whitman_mission` gates on `abandonedAfterYear: 1847`.

If the ruined state is not separately implemented, simply gating the `trading_post` mode off (so late parties see an empty landmark event rather than a trade screen) would be a correct minimum.

### Stock and pricing accuracy

The current `stockScale: 0.6` and `blurb` are historically calibrated — Lucinda Frizzell's 1852 diary records flour, bacon, coffee, and salt at Fort Boise; sugar was rare; the post was genuinely sparse. The current `barterPreferred: ['buffalo_robe', 'raw_hide', 'game_meat', 'jerky']` is well-sourced (Carpenter 1857 note in the code). One potential addition: **dried salmon** as a native-trade item at or near this landmark, reflecting the Shoshone/Bannock fishery context. Salmon appears in the landmark at mile ~1450 (Salmon Falls), but Fort Boise itself had active salmon trade.

The task description noted `priceMultiplier: 1.2` but the actual landmark definition in code does not set one — it would default to the baseline (1.0 or whatever the system default is). If intentional, this is fine; if `1.2` was planned, it is not yet in the code.

### HBC flag rendering

The `postKind: 'hbc'` flag drives the HBC visual treatment in `FortBoiseArt.svelte`. The Union Jack component of the HBC flag is historically correct for 1843–1855. After 1846 (Oregon Treaty) the post was technically on American soil but HBC's property rights under the treaty were respected until abandonment, so the HBC flag continued flying. No change needed for post-1846 rendering within the active window.
