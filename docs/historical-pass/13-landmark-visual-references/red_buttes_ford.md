## Identity verdict

**This landmark is almost certainly the Red Buttes / Bessemer Bend ford**, not a generic "west crossing" of the North Platte. The site is now a BLM National Historic Site south of Casper, Wyoming (Natrona County), maintained specifically because it was the last fording location on the North Platte for emigrant parties who could not or would not pay the downstream toll ferries and bridges. The NPS and BLM both call it "Red Buttes Crossing" or "Bessemer Bend National Historic Site." The emigrant-era name was simply "Red Buttes" — the nearby sandstone formations so distinctive they named the crossing.

See [Disambiguation and code notes](#disambiguation-and-code-notes) below for the rename recommendation and the sequence quirk.

## Historical identity

### What it is

Bessemer Bend is a large meander loop on the North Platte River about ten miles southwest of present-day Casper. At this bend the river hooks sharply south and southwest, turning away from the westbound trail for good. The Red Buttes — two high, cherry-red sandstone points of the Chugwater and Goose Egg Formations — rise above the bend on its south side, stained brick-red by iron oxide. They are visible for miles across the sage plain and served as the navigational signal that the crossing was near.

This was the final crossing of the North Platte for every emigrant traveling west on the Oregon, California, and Mormon Trails. After 450 miles following the river from Fort Kearny, Nebraska, through the entire Nebraska panhandle and across most of Wyoming, parties crossed here — or at the toll ferries and bridges a few miles downstream — and turned southwest toward the Sweetwater River and South Pass.

The ford at Bessemer Bend, specifically, was the route for parties unwilling or unable to pay the ferry tolls. It was not a comfortable option: the North Platte here was wide, swift, and cold with Rocky Mountain snowmelt in June, and the riverbed of large angular rocks made it treacherous for wagons. Frémont measured it as 200 feet of active channel within a 1,500-foot-wide bed, with channels generally three feet deep — which matches the game's river data (depthFt: 4.0 at peak, 3.0 ft at lower water) closely.

### Why it was used

From 1847 onward, the Mormon Ferry operated a few miles downstream near present-day Mills (where Wyoming Boulevard now crosses the river). By 1852–53, Reshaw's Bridge (John Baptiste Richard) charged $8 per wagon at high water; Guinard's Bridge (1859) was even more elaborate at $40,000–$60,000 construction cost, charging $6–$8 per wagon. For a party of even modest means — family wagons that may have already paid tolls at the Kansas River, Papan's Ferry, and elsewhere — $8 per wagon was a meaningful sum.

The result: parties who arrived in late summer, when water had dropped enough to make the ford passable, crossed at Bessemer Bend. This included not just budget-conscious civilian emigrants but also the Mormon handcart companies of 1856 (who as church members bypassed the by-then-defunct church ferry and forded instead) and many early parties before the ferry infrastructure existed at all. In the fur-trade era of the 1820s and 1830s, this was simply *the* crossing — the only practical ford of the upper North Platte.

### The pre-emigrant era

Warren Angus Ferris of the American Fur Company crossed here in June 1830, describing it as "the Red Hills — these are two high cherry-red points of rock, separated by the river, which here turns away to the southward." The toponym stuck. Frémont used "Red Buttes" in 1842. Emigrants of 1845–1865 uniformly called the site "Red Buttes."

Robert Stuart's 1812–13 Astorian party — the first Euro-Americans to traverse what would become the Oregon Trail — camped here on their return from Astoria and built the first European cabin in Wyoming in November 1812 near the confluence of the North Platte with Poison Spider Creek. Fear of local Arapaho induced them to abandon it within two days, but the site's strategic importance was recognized from the outset.

A Pony Express station and Overland Stage station were later established in the immediate vicinity, confirming its role as a permanent landmark on the route west.

## Diary record

### Fremont, 1842

Lt. John C. Frémont of the U.S. Corps of Topographical Engineers crossed here on July 29, 1842, during his survey of the route to the Rocky Mountains. His report — circulated widely and read by thousands of prospective emigrants — gave the ford its lasting authority:

> "There was two hundred feet breadth of water at this time in the bed, which has a variable width of eight to fifteen hundred feet. The channels were generally three feet deep, and there were large angular rocks on the bottom, which made the ford in some places a little difficult. Even at its low stages this river cannot be crossed at random, and this has always been used as the best ford."
>
> — Lt. John C. Frémont, *Report of the Exploring Expedition to the Rocky Mountains*, July 29, 1842

"This has always been used as the best ford" — Frémont was writing for an audience he expected to follow in his footsteps, and they did. His report made Bessemer Bend the canonical crossing in the popular consciousness before the ferry infrastructure existed to replace it.

### Jim Clyman, 1846

James Clyman was a former mountain man traveling eastbound in June 1846. He encountered the westbound emigration at its peak — 1846 was the year of the Donner Party, Bryant, and the first mass California rush:

> "[W]hen we came in sight of N. Platte we had the Pleasant sight of Beholding the valy to a greate distance dotted with Peopl Horses cattle wagons and Tents their being 30 wagons all Busily engaged in crossing the River which was found not to be fordable and with the poor material they had to make rafts of it took two trips to carry over one wagon with its lading we however ware not long in crossing as we threw our baggage on the returning rafts and swam our animals over and encamped once more in the Buisy humm of our own Language."
>
> — James Clyman, journal, June 1846, at the Red Buttes crossing

Clyman's eastbound party could improvise — they threw their gear on rafts and swam their horses. Westbound parties with loaded wagons, weakened livestock, and families aboard faced a different calculation. The note that the river "was found not to be fordable" at late June levels explains exactly why the Mormon Ferry, established the following year, found immediate commercial success.

### Rebecca Ketchum, 1853

Ketchum was a young woman traveling with a party from Ithaca, New York. Her diary, later published in the *Oregon Historical Quarterly* as *From Ithaca to Clatsop Plains*, records the emotional weight of arriving at Red Buttes:

> "I believe we have left the Platte River for good, and I am glad indeed. We have been so long on it. There is so little beauty about it, and we are so much afraid to drink it on account of its being unhealthy that I have grown heartily sick of it."
>
> — Rebecca Ketchum, journal, June 1853

By 1853 most parties were crossing downstream at Reshaw's Bridge. Ketchum almost certainly did too. But her reaction captures what the Red Buttes meant: not just a ford, but the psychological end of the Platte. Five hundred miles of that river, day after day — wide, sandy, shallow, alkaline-tasting, churned to mud by the cattle ahead. The Red Buttes visible to the south, the trail turning away from the water toward sage hills and the Sweetwater — it was the moment the journey changed character.

## Landscape and visual description

### The Red Buttes

The formations are composed primarily of Chugwater Formation (Triassic) redbed siltstone and sandstone, colored brick-red to cherry-red by iron oxide (hematite) in the matrix. They sit on the south side of the river at the bend — two prominent buttes separated by the river gorge where the North Platte squeezes through before swinging south. Warren Ferris's "two high cherry-red points of rock" is accurate. From the north side of the river, where the trail ran, they rise as a wall of red against the buff-and-sage background of the high Wyoming plain.

The river at this point runs in a wide gravel-and-cobble bed backed by cottonwood groves in the bottomland — cottonwoods being essentially the only tall timber in this part of Wyoming, and used by early emigrants to build ferries and rafts. The surrounding terrain is open sage prairie, the sage growing ankle-to-knee high on the flat with occasional greasewood in the drier alkaline patches. The Red Buttes are the one visually arresting feature in an otherwise horizontal landscape, which is why they served as a navigation marker visible from miles away.

### The ford itself

The North Platte at Bessemer Bend is 300 yards wide (900 feet) with the active channel typically occupying 200 feet within the gravel bed. The bottom is large angular rock — the geology here is the west nose of the Casper Mountain Anticline — which made it superior to the soft-bottomed, quicksand-prone crossings further east, but still treacherous for iron-rimmed wheels. At peak spring flow (May–June) the main channels ran three to five feet deep with a brisk current. By late July–August the water dropped to two to three feet with a somewhat reduced current, making it genuinely fordable for experienced parties. The ford is due east of the visible buttes — emigrants crossed, looked up, and saw them framing the gap in the bluffs where the Sweetwater country began.

### After the crossing

Immediately past Bessemer Bend, all trail variants converged for the "hot, hard haul" over Devil's Backbone (Avenue of Rocks / Emigrant Gap) to Willow Springs — described in the code as only 4 miles, which aligns with the real trail. Edwin Bryant (1846) recorded the terrain past the crossing as "an arid plain, the only vegetation upon which is the wild sage, grease-wood, and a few perishing plants." Peter Decker (1849) noted "Dust blown in road in places white like snow with Alkali." After the green cottonwood bottomland of Bessemer Bend, it was an abrupt transition into genuinely difficult country. Willow Springs, 4 miles on, was the first good water after the crossing.

## Relationship to ft_caspar (mile 770)

Fort Caspar in the code represents the Mormon Ferry / Guinard's Bridge crossing at the site of present-day Casper. This is the *downstream, downstream-toll* crossing — the bridge/ferry option that most emigrants took from 1847 onward. `north_platte_2` at mile 808, 38 miles further in the code sequence, represents the Bessemer Bend ford — the *upstream, free* option used by parties in low-water seasons, the early years before the ferry, and any party that chose to avoid the toll.

In the real geography:
- Bessemer Bend / Red Buttes ford is actually about 10 miles **upstream** (southwest) of the Fort Caspar bridge site.
- Historically, the sequence is: arrive at Fort Caspar area → pay toll and cross at the bridge/ferry, then travel southwest → Red Buttes → Sweetwater country. OR: bypass the bridge/ferry, continue along the south bank ~10 miles → cross at Bessemer Bend → same destination.

The code currently models them as two sequential stops (Fort Caspar → Martin's Cove → north_platte_2) rather than as branch-point alternatives. The comment at line 184 of `landmarks.ts` flags the `north_platte_2/martins_cove` geographic-sequence quirk as a known issue tracked under #1040.

## Disambiguation and code notes

### Identity recommendation

**Rename `north_platte_2` to `red_buttes_ford` or `bessemer_bend`.** The current name "North Platte (west crossing)" is accurate in a directional sense but completely opaque — it gives no indication that this is a specific, historically named, and currently managed historic site with a distinctive visual identity. The Red Buttes are the landmark. Every source from 1830 (Ferris) to 1853 (Ketchum) calls this place "Red Buttes." The NPS and BLM call it "Bessemer Bend / Red Buttes Crossing."

Recommended new ID: `red_buttes_ford`
Recommended display name: "Red Buttes Ford" or "Red Buttes Crossing"

### Consolidation question

There is a design question about whether `ft_caspar` and `north_platte_2` should be modeled as two sequential landmarks or as a single branch-point landmark with a toll/free choice. Historically, parties made a single choice — pay at the bridge/ferry, or continue upstream to the ford — not both. The current sequential model implies every party did both crossings, which is not accurate. A future version might:

1. Merge them into a single `north_platte_crossing` event with a branch: pay toll (faster, safer) vs. ford free (risky, saves $5–$8)
2. Keep them separate but add a flag that skips `north_platte_2` if the party paid at `ft_caspar` (and vice versa)
3. Keep the current design as a game simplification and document the historical distortion

Option 3 is the pragmatic choice for now. The comment at line 373 of `landmarks.ts` already acknowledges the era-gated ferry-vs-bridge pricing is a follow-up. This is that follow-up.

### Sequence quirk (existing known issue)

In the current code, `martins_cove` (mile 800) appears *before* `north_platte_2` (mile 808), but Martin's Cove is historically ~45 miles west of Bessemer Bend (at real-world mile ~855 vs ~810). This is the "north_platte_2/martins_cove geographic-sequence quirk" flagged in the comment at line 184 and tracked under #1040. The visual reference doc is written for the real historical Red Buttes site; the game's sequence inversion is a separate code concern.

## Open questions / unverified

1. **Exact depth and current at Bessemer Bend ford.** Frémont's 1842 measurement of "generally three feet deep" with "large angular rocks on the bottom" is the best primary source. The game uses `depthFt: 4.0, currentMph: 3` — those figures may have been set by an earlier research pass; they're in the right range for high-water season (June) and plausible for the ford character. Verify source of those specific figures.

2. **J. Goldsborough Bruff at Red Buttes, 1849.** Sources confirm Bruff passed through the area and kept detailed journals (published as *Gold Rush: The Journals, Drawings, and Other Papers of J. Goldsborough Bruff*, 1944). His drawings of the ford or Red Buttes would be valuable visual references. The full text is at the Bancroft Library / California Digital Library. Not directly accessed for this pass.

3. **Edwin Bryant's July 6, 1846 entry.** Sources describe Bryant writing "the trail here finally leaves the Platte river" on this date. The full passage in *What I Saw in California* (1848) would be a cleaner diary pull than Ketchum's 1853 entry. Bryant was a journalist-diarist and his language was vivid. Worth pulling if another diary quote is needed.

4. **North vs. south bank routing.** Some parties crossed to the north bank of the North Platte at Deer Creek (near Glenrock) and traveled the north bank to Casper before crossing back south. This "north side" route was reportedly sandier and less preferred. The relationship to north_platte_2 in the game is unclear — does the game model north-side routing as a separate event? Probably not, and probably fine to ignore.

5. **Pony Express station at Red Buttes.** The NPS confirms a Pony Express station was "located in this vicinity." Its exact location relative to the ford is not established in sources consulted. Probably within a mile or two. Not relevant to the 1840s–1850s emigrant game window.
