# Fort Hall — Visual Reference

**Vikunja task:** #1118 https://projects.ericbond.net/tasks/1118
**Code keys:** `postKind: 'hbc'` · `priceMultiplier: 1.0` · `abandonedAfterYear: 1856`
**Period gate (CRITICAL):** Two rendering states required — ACTIVE (1843–1856) and RUIN (1857–1860). The game code gates on `abandonedAfterYear: 1856`; any emigrant arriving 1857 or later finds an empty, roofless shell.
**Trail mile (approx.):** ~1217–1230 (sources vary ±15 mi; NPS Oregon NHT places it ~1230 from Independence)
**Location:** Left bank of the Snake River, ~9 miles above the mouth of the Portneuf River, now Bannock County, southeastern Idaho; on the modern Fort Hall Indian Reservation of the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes

---

## Overview

Fort Hall is the Oregon Trail's most symbolically charged resupply post: a British Crown-chartered company outpost flying the HBC Red Ensign deep inside what Americans increasingly regarded as their territory. Its history divides cleanly into three arcs. Nathaniel Wyeth built it from Idaho cottonwood in August 1834 as a commercial bet that failed. The Hudson's Bay Company bought it in 1837 and rebuilt it in sun-dried adobe, operating it for nearly twenty years as the Oregon Country's last inhabited outpost before the Pacific. The HBC walked away in 1856, leaving adobe walls to soften back into the Snake River bottomland. The 1863 spring flood largely finished the job; by the 1870s the site was lost entirely. The modern Fort Hall replica in Pocatello's Ross Park (built 1963–64 from HBC archival floor plans) is at a different location — do not render from it.

**Why it matters for rendering:** The active-state fort is a genuine oasis — whitewashed adobe catching Idaho summer light, a British flag over a grid of flat-roofed rooms, cottonwood shade on a sagebrush plain. The ruin state is the opposite: low slumping walls, no flag, roofs collapsed, doorways gaping, the desert reclaiming the site. Both states sit in identical geography — the Snake River bottomland — so sky, vegetation, and landform are shared across the period gate.

## Period gate: Active HBC post (1843–1856)

### Historical context

When the HBC took over in August 1837 (formally settled June 1838), they tore down Wyeth's rotting cottonwood palisade and built in adobe — the only practical durable material available in quantity on the Snake River plain. Adobe was also the HBC's standard construction medium for interior Oregon Country posts. The rebuilt fort was substantially enlarged: where Wyeth's stockade was 80 ft square (described as 80 × 80 in most sources), the HBC-era fort grew to roughly 80 × 100 ft in some accounts, with additional rooms built against the inside faces of the perimeter wall. Post-1837, the outer walls were kept whitewashed — the single most remarked visual feature in emigrant diaries. The white walls were visible miles out across the flat sagebrush plain, acting as a literal beacon.

The fort's chief factor during peak emigration years was Richard (Capt.) Grant, who managed it from the early 1840s through the early 1850s. Grant is a pivotal figure in the scene: the HBC's official policy was to discourage American emigration to Oregon (to preserve British territorial claims), and Grant actively showed arriving emigrants the abandoned wagons of the previous season's companies as evidence that wagons couldn't make it to Oregon. This psychological drama — exhausted Americans arriving at a British post to be told their wagons are worthless — is the fort's core historical moment.

### What it looked like (active state)

**Form and layout**

Square-to-slightly-rectangular adobe stockade, approximately 80 × 80 ft (80 × 100 ft under HBC expansion; sources disagree and the HBC archival plans used for the 1963 replica suggest the larger number). Wall height: 15 feet, consistent with Wyeth's original log specification which the adobe rebuild maintained. Walls approximately 2 feet thick — adobe brick construction, larger-format "dobie" bricks (contemporaries describe them as roughly 2 ft long × 6 in deep).

Two bastions at **diagonally opposite corners** (not all four corners — this is a classic trading-post economy; the opposite-corner placement gives overlapping fields of fire along all four walls from just two structures). Each bastion was approximately 8 ft square in plan, projecting slightly beyond the wall face, with a second story that overhung the wall for vertical fire. Bastion walls had narrow gun ports sized for rifle barrels, not cannon embrasures. The bastions were integral to the adobe construction, not bolt-on structures.

Single main gate in one wall (sources disagree on which face; the 1963 replica puts it on the south face toward the trail approach, which is consistent with emigrant accounts of approaching from the southeast).

Inside the stockade: rooms built against the interior wall faces — factor's quarters, trade room/store, men's quarters, storage. The central courtyard was open dirt. Roofs were flat mud-and-brush construction over log rafters, typical of Southwestern/Great Basin adobe vernacular. No raised walkways are documented; defenders would use the bastions.

**The whitewash**

This is the defining visual element. Contemporary accounts are specific: *"It was the custom of the company to keep these outer walls well whitewashed."* (Idaho State Historical Society Reference Series No. 121, citing multiple sources.) The whitewash was applied to the exterior adobe faces. In the Idaho summer sun — fierce, high-altitude UV, low humidity — the walls would have been a brilliant chalky white-cream color, reflecting light and visible at great distance across the flat sagebrush plain. This was not decorative; whitewash sealed the adobe against rain erosion and insects.

**Color notes for FLUX:** The adobe base is a warm tan-buff #C4A882 to #B89B73. The whitewash over it reads as chalk white #F2EFE8 to #E8E4DC — not pure white, because adobe roughness and lime-calcite formulas produce an off-white with yellow undertones. In harsh summer noon light the walls would blow toward #F5F0E8. At golden hour or morning, warm #E8D9C0.

**The flag**

The HBC flew its own ensign at trading posts: a **Red Ensign** (red field) with the Union Jack in the canton (upper hoist corner) and the letters "HBC" in the fly half. This flag flew at all major HBC interior posts in the 1840s–1850s. It is the single most politically charged visual element of the scene — British colors flying in what Americans increasingly claimed as US territory (the Oregon Treaty of 1846 set the 49th parallel boundary, making Fort Hall clearly US soil, but the HBC continued operating under treaty terms until 1856). The flag would have been standard canvas bunting, faded and sun-bleached in the Idaho summer. Flagpole position: likely center of the courtyard or above the gate, visible above the wall.

**Surrounding landscape**

The fort sat in the **Snake River bottomland** — the flat alluvial ground immediately adjacent to the Snake. This creates a distinct two-zone landscape visible in the scene:

- **Immediate foreground/foreground zone (within ~200 yards):** Dense riparian vegetation. Townsend (1834, accompanying Wyeth) described the opposite bank as covered with "large timber of the cottonwood and willow, with a dense undergrowth of the same, intermixed with serviceberry and currant bushes." The bottomland supported tall cottonwoods (30–50 ft), clumps of willows, and dense low brush. The Snake itself was visible as a bright ribbon — Townsend: *"It is here as wide as the Missouri at Independence, but, beyond comparison, clearer and more beautiful."* The river ran swift and clear-blue in summer before agricultural diversions.
- **Midground/horizon:** The high desert Snake River Plain began immediately beyond the bottomland — hard sagebrush-and-bunchgrass flats, pale gray-green in summer (Artemisia tridentata, the dominant shrub). Emigrants approaching from the southeast described the vista as "dispiriting" — sand, sagebrush, and the deep canyon chasm of the Snake.
- **Background — Three Buttes:** Visible on the northern horizon from the fort site, the Three Buttes (Middle Butte, East Butte, and Cinder Butte) are a trio of isolated volcanic peaks rising abruptly from the Snake River Plain. They are mentioned in period accounts as distant landmark reference points. In summer haze they read as blue-gray silhouettes.

**Vegetation color notes for FLUX:** Cottonwoods in July–August: full-leaf bright yellowish green #8BA848 to #7A9940. Sagebrush plain beyond: dusty gray-green #8A9070 to #7A8468. Snake River in background: cobalt-to-steel blue #5B7FA8 in sunlight. Idaho summer sky at noon: deep cyan-blue #4A80C8 with high-UV bleach toward #6A98D8 at zenith.

**Condition of the active fort**

By the 1840s the fort had been standing a decade and was neither pristine nor picturesque. Theodore Talbot, traveling with Fremont's 1843 expedition, recorded the canonical dismissal: *"Fort Hall is a small and rather ill constructed Fort, built of 'Dobie.'"* This was a trained military observer's assessment — the construction was competent adobe vernacular, but nothing grand. Emigrants who had been romanticizing "the fort" for months often found it smaller and rougher than expected. It was functional, not impressive. But after hundreds of miles of open prairie and mountain, the whitewashed walls, the smell of wood smoke from cooking fires, and the sound of voices not belonging to their own party made it feel like civilization.

## Period gate: Abandoned ruin (1857–1860)

### Why it was abandoned

The 1846 Oregon Treaty explicitly allocated the Snake River valley to the United States, but the HBC retained commercial rights for a decade-long wind-down. By the early 1850s the beaver trade — the fort's original purpose — was commercially extinct in the region. The Snake River buffalo herds had also collapsed. The fort was surviving on emigrant trade, but that traffic was shifting to newer routes and the HBC's anti-American-emigration stance had cost it goodwill. A severe flood in 1853 damaged the Snake River bottomland around the fort. George Simpson, HBC governor, assessed the fort in 1848 as in "a defenseless state." Operations were formally discontinued around 1856.

After 1856 the fort was genuinely empty — no factor, no trade staff, no flag. For the next several years it served as a campsite for emigrant companies (the walls still provided wind shelter), for military patrols (Regulars and Oregon Volunteers camped there in 1859–60), and occasionally for itinerant traders. The 1863 spring flood largely destroyed the remaining structure. By the mid-1860s the site was difficult to locate; by the 1870s it was functionally lost. In the early 20th century, survivor Joe Rainey helped relocate the original site. Today only low earth mounds outline the fort's former walls, on the Fort Hall Indian Reservation approximately 11 miles west of the modern town of Fort Hall.

### What it looked like (ruin state, 1857–1860)

Adobe ruins fail in a specific and visually distinctive way that FLUX must be directed toward carefully. Adobe does **not** collapse like stone or brick, leaving dramatic tall jagged walls. It **slumps**. Rain and frost soften the upper courses of an adobe wall first; the wall rounds and lowers from the top, collapsing not as a drama but as a slow deflation. Within 3–5 years of abandonment in a semi-arid climate with occasional heavy rain or snow, an adobe stockade would show:

- Wall height reduced from 15 ft to perhaps 8–10 ft on the best-preserved sections, with no section retaining full height
- Corners and wall tops rounded (not sharp), covered with the granular residue of dissolved adobe brick — essentially mud returning to dirt
- The whitewash entirely gone or surviving only as isolated white patches at sheltered lower sections; the exposed adobe face returns to tan-buff, darker where wet, lighter where dry
- Upper courses of brick visibly melting — indistinct layer structure, surface crumbling at touch
- Gate opening remains (no door, no lintel structure — just a gap in the wall, wider at top because the jamb corners slump first)
- Bastions more likely to retain height than walls, because the projecting corner geometry drains better — but the overhang structure that gave them their defensive function would be collapsed, leaving stumps
- Interior rooms: flat mud roofs collapse when the log rafters rot (5–10 years) or when a heavy snow sits on them — the rooms are now open to the sky, filled with roof-fall debris, mud, and weeds
- No flag, no flagpole (wooden poles rot within a few years or are taken for firewood)
- The main gate gap: travelers in 1857–1860 could walk through the gap and find nothing but rubble, collapsed room walls, and desert vegetation invading the courtyard

**Vegetation reclaiming the site:** Sagebrush begins recolonizing disturbed ground in the Snake River plain within 2–3 growing seasons. By 1860 sagebrush plants 1–2 ft tall would be established inside the former courtyard and at the base of the walls. The bottomland cottonwoods and willows are unaffected — they continue to frame the site. The contrast between the lush riparian fringe and the crumbling fort interior is a key visual element.

**Color shift in the ruin state:** Without whitewash, the adobe reads as warm tan-brown #C4A882 to #A88860 on dry surfaces, darkening to #8A7050 where moisture-stained. The wall tops are the darkest and most granular, approaching the color of the surrounding plain. The overall scene is more monochromatic than the active state — no bright white, no flag color, no flag pole vertical. The sky is unchanged.

### No flag — this is important

The absence of the HBC Red Ensign is not a small detail. Emigrants arriving post-1856 would have recognized immediately that the fort was dead — no flag means no factor, no trade, no help. The empty flagpole socket (or its absence) is a period-accurate signal of abandonment as clear as any collapsed wall.

## Primary source diary excerpts

**Nathaniel Wyeth, October 6, 1834 (letter to his uncle Jarvis):** On completing the original wooden fort:
> "We manufactured a magnificent flag from some unbleached sheeting, a little red flannel, and a few blue patches, saluted it with damaged powder, and wet it in villainous alcohol, and after all it makes a very respectable appearance. Its Bastions stand a terror to the sculking Indian and a beacon of saf[e]ty to the fugitive hunter."

(Source: Wyeth correspondence, as quoted in multiple secondary sources including Legends of America and the Quartux Journal. The flag described was a homemade American flag — the HBC replaced it with their ensign after the 1837 sale. The "bastions" quote gives us Wyeth's word for the corner structures and confirms they were present in the original 1834 log construction.)

**Theodore Talbot, September 1843 (journals of the Fremont expedition):** On the HBC adobe fort:
> "Fort Hall is a small and rather ill constructed Fort, built of 'Dobie.'"

(Source: *The Journals of Theodore Talbot, 1843 and 1849–52*, edited by Charles H. Carey. This is the most frequently cited period architectural assessment. Talbot was a trained U.S. Army officer traveling with Fremont; his characterization of "ill constructed" reflects a professional's eye comparing it to US Army masonry standards, not a fair assessment of adobe vernacular craftsmanship.)

**Lansford Hastings, 1845 (The Emigrants' Guide to Oregon and California):** On the HBC factor's hospitality and the wagon-abandonment pressure:
> "Upon arriving at Fort Hall, we were received in the kindest manner, by Mr. Grant."

(Source: Spartacus Educational's Fort Hall article, citing Hastings's 1845 guidebook. Hastings's account is notable for its surface politeness — Grant was personally hospitable while systemically discouraging emigration with the abandoned wagon display. This is the same Hastings whose "Hastings Cutoff" would doom the Donner Party the following year.)

**John Kirk Townsend, August 1834 (Narrative of a Journey Across the Rocky Mountains):** On the Portneuf-Snake confluence vegetation, recorded during the Wyeth expedition:
> "There is some large timber, of cottonwood, willow, &c., with a dense undergrowth of the same, intermixed with service-berry and currant bushes."
> 
> (On the Snake itself:) "It is here as wide as the Missouri at Independence, but, beyond comparison, clearer and more beautiful."

(Source: South Fork Companion blog, citing Townsend's published *Narrative*. Townsend was a Philadelphia ornithologist traveling with Wyeth's 1834 expedition; this is the earliest naturalist description of the Fort Hall site's vegetation and river character.)

## What NOT to render

- **The Pocatello replica:** The 1963 Fort Hall Replica in Ross Park, Pocatello, is a 20th-century reconstruction at a different location, based on HBC archival plans. It is better-maintained and more visually tidy than the original ever was. Do not reference photos of the Pocatello replica for visual inspiration — it has modern trim details, a paved interpretive area, and is inside a city park.
- **The modern Fort Hall (BIA agency town):** The present-day town of Fort Hall on the Shoshone-Bannock Reservation is a 20th-century federal agency complex approximately 11 miles from the original 1834 site. No visual relationship to the historic post.
- **Masonry or stone:** The fort was adobe mud brick and cottonwood log, not cut stone. No quarried stone details, no mortar coursework visible, no dressed-stone lintels.
- **Multiple bastions:** Some generalized "trading post" imagery defaults to four corner towers. Fort Hall had **two bastions at opposite corners only** — the other two corners were plain wall-meets-wall joints.
- **Grand scale:** The active fort was approximately 80 ft square (roughly the footprint of a large suburban house or a small city block half). It was compact and low. The walls were 15 ft — shorter than a modern two-story building. It would look modest, not impressive, in a landscape dominated by open sky.
- **Non-HBC flags:** The American flag is wrong for the active state (1843–1856). Wyeth flew a homemade American flag briefly in 1834, but the HBC ensign replaced it by 1838. A plain Union Jack is also not quite right — it was the HBC Red Ensign with HBC letters. If the prompt simplifies to "British flag / Union Jack" that is acceptable shorthand; a plain Stars and Stripes is historically wrong.
- **Dramatic tall ruin walls (FLUX failure mode):** FLUX/SDXL models trained on images of stone ruins (European castles, Pueblo ruins, Southwestern cliff dwellings) default to showing tall jagged standing walls with dramatic silhouettes. Adobe ruins do not look like this. The failure mode is a prompt like "abandoned adobe fort" producing something resembling Mesa Verde or a Southwestern kiva. The corrective language is in the ruin FLUX prompt below.
- **Post-1870 features:** No barbed wire, no corrugated iron, no glass windows, no telegraph poles, no wheeled vehicles of later construction. The game's Oregon Trail era is 1843–1860.

## Unresolved questions / verification gaps

- **Exact dimensions:** The 80 × 80 ft figure is from Wyeth's 1834 log construction journal. Multiple secondary sources state the HBC expanded this to 80 × 100 ft, citing the 1963 Pocatello replica's HBC archival floor plans as evidence. The expansion claim is plausible but this researcher could not verify the archival floor plan dimensions directly. The game should render to the larger HBC-era figure (~80 × 100 ft) but the difference is not visually significant at the scene scale we need.
- **Interior building count and arrangement:** Period sources confirm there were multiple rooms against the interior walls (factor's house, trade room, men's quarters, storage), but exact room layout is uncertain. The 1963 replica's interior arrangement is the best available approximation; treat it as approximately correct for the HBC expansion period.
- **Bastion details:** The 8 × 8 ft dimension is from Wyeth's 1834 original description and probably carried through to the HBC rebuild at similar scale. The overhanging second story is standard HBC/fur-trade fort bastion design (confirmed at Fort Vancouver, Fort Nisqually, and other comparable posts) but is not explicitly described at Fort Hall specifically.
- **Post-1856 emigrant diary accounts:** This researcher did not find a directly quoted emigrant diary passage describing the abandoned ruin state in 1857–1860 from an identified source. The general picture — crumbling buildings, itinerant traders camping in the shell, military patrols staging there — is attested in secondary sources citing period records. Dave should verify against OCTA's emigrant diary archive or the Idaho State Historical Society's Fort Hall file (Reference Series No. 121, a PDF that was unreadable in this session).
- **Mormon brief occupation:** Some sources mention Mormon travelers or settlers briefly using the abandoned fort post-1856 but this researcher could not pin down the specific account. If this is needed for game events, it warrants direct archival lookup.
- **Richard Grant's departure date:** Grant managed the post through approximately 1854; a succession of factors or reduced-staff operations covered 1854–1856. The exact last year of HBC staffing is not pinned.
